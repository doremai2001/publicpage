WHAT NOBODY ASKS IN CLINIC

No period is not contraception

Amenorrhoea is not contraception, men must use it too, and the durations online come from US labels — not from your Taiwanese one.

Patient Education · What Nobody Asks In Clinic · 14 references
#Sex & Intimacy#Prevention#Chemotherapy

Two wrong readings do equal harm: that pregnancy during treatment is certain disaster, and that after twelve weeks it stops mattering. Both are closed off below, with the actual intervals and denominators. If you are already pregnant, the last two sections are the ones to read first.

This is the piece I most hope nobody has to use. Becoming pregnant during treatment puts a person somewhere with no good options: on one side, carrying on with anticancer treatment; on the other, ending the pregnancy. Neither is what you set out to choose. It is also almost entirely avoidable, by means that are not complicated — so use contraception, men and women both.

"I'm probably not going to get pregnant during treatment" — that thought has to go first

It is wrong, and there are numbers. Of 100 patients under 40 in the Basel breast cancer database, 58% used an ineffective method during the first year after diagnosis, and that group's unintended pregnancy rate over one year was 3.5%[1]. A systematic review of 16 studies records the same picture — inadequate contraceptive counselling, low uptake of highly effective methods — with uncertainty about whether one is still fertile running underneath it[2].

The most direct figure is in the International Network on Cancer, Infertility and Pregnancy (INCIP) registry: of 29 women given chemotherapy before 12 weeks' gestation, 17 (58.6%) did not know at the time that they were pregnant[3]. The pregnancy test before treatment starts is not us doubting you. It is the process.

Periods stopping does not mean the ovaries have stopped

The commonest misunderstanding in clinic, and the costliest. A prospective study enrolled hormone receptor-positive breast cancer patients selected for chemotherapy-induced amenorrhoea of two years or more with oestradiol in the postmenopausal range — clinically and biochemically, menopausal. After a change of endocrine agent, ovarian function recovery was 32% (95% CI 19.5–44.5%; a confidence interval is the range in which the estimate plausibly sits), at a median of 5.4 months, and the authors concluded that women under 48 with chemotherapy-induced amenorrhoea should not be managed as menopausal[4]. In the Dutch DATA phase 3 trial, of 329 patients with chemotherapy-induced ovarian function failure, recovery within 30 months was still 12.4%[5].

In plain words: no period, and blood tests that look menopausal, are neither of them contraception. Ovulation may return once when you are not expecting it, and pregnancy needs only that once.

The evidence is stratified: not "it will definitely cause birth defects", and not "after 12 weeks it's fine"

I have written this section with care, because misreadings in either direction do harm. INCIP collected 755 women given chemotherapy during pregnancy from 1977 to 2019. The overall major congenital malformation rate was 3.6% (2.4–5.2%), and it splits very unevenly — in those exposed before 12 weeks' gestation the major congenital malformation rate was 21.7% (95% CI 7.5–43.7%); in those whose chemotherapy started after 12 weeks it was 3.0% (1.9–4.6%), comparable with what is expected in the general population[3]. The odds ratio comparing the two was 9.24 (3.13–27.30): an odds ratio divides one group's odds of the event happening against not happening by the other's, and is not a multiple of probability; an interval stretching from 3 to 27 means the figure is very unstable — that cell holds only 29 people.

Read 21.7% exactly as it stands. It is high, high enough not to risk; and it also means roughly four children in five have no major malformation. That second half has to be written down, because a woman who is already pregnant should not be pushed into an irreversible decision by an article.

In the other direction, "past 12 weeks there is no problem" does not hold either. A separate INCIP cohort of 1,170 patients over 20 years: of 1,089 singleton pregnancies, 88% ended in a live birth, and of the 887 with data, 430 (48%) were preterm; platinum chemotherapy was also associated with small-for-gestational-age infants (odds ratio 3.12, 1.45–6.70)[6]. The same caution applies: 3.12 does not mean the probability triples.

Men have to use contraception too, and there are two reasons

Contraception is not the woman's job alone: men must use contraception too, during treatment and for a period after treatment ends. There are two reasons and they need separating.

The first is chromosomal damage in sperm. A study scanning 635,396 sperm compared testicular cancer and Hodgkin lymphoma patients before and after chemotherapy: six months after treatment started, sex chromosome abnormalities and chromosome 13 and 21 losses were significantly more frequent in all patients; most returned to pre-treatment levels by 18 months, and for some chromosomes the elevation persisted to 24 months. The authors recommend genetic counselling for any conception within two years of treatment starting[7].

The second is that the drug reaches the partner through semen. The US label for lenalidomide states outright that it is present in the semen of men taking it, and that a man must use a condom for any sexual contact with a woman of reproductive potential during treatment and for four weeks after stopping, even after a vasectomy[8].

One frequently misread study should also go back where it belongs. A Finnish nationwide cohort compared the children of 6,862 survivors with those of 35,690 of their siblings: congenital anomalies 3.2% against 2.7%, prevalence ratio 1.07 (0.91–1.25), not significant[9]. That is about children born to survivors after treatment ended. It is not a licence saying conception during treatment is fine. Wanting a child afterwards, and how to preserve fertility, is the subject of "Some options close when treatment starts" — the two do not conflict.

How long do you have to keep it up? These durations come from US labels, not from Taiwanese rules

Finishing treatment does not mean you can conceive straight away, and how long to wait varies greatly by drug. Every duration below is taken from US FDA labels, not from Taiwan's TFDA Chinese-language labels (中文仿單) — I could not retrieve the Chinese labels when verifying, and different manufacturers of the same ingredient may differ. Go by the label of the drug you are actually on and by what your team tells you. The list is here so you can see how wide the spread is[8]:

  • cyclophosphamide: women 1 year after completing treatment, men 4 months

  • ifosfamide: women 12 months after the final dose, men 6 months

  • cisplatin: women 14 months, men 11 months

  • methotrexate: women 6 months, men 3 months

  • doxorubicin: women 6 months, men 3 months; if the partner is already pregnant, condoms during treatment and for at least 10 days after the final dose

  • gemcitabine, capecitabine: women 6 months, men 3 months

  • olaparib: women 6 months, men 3 months, with no sperm donation during that period

  • osimertinib: women 6 weeks, men 4 months

  • trastuzumab emtansine: women 7 months, men 4 months

  • pembrolizumab 4 months, nivolumab 5 months, bevacizumab 6 months (women; the labels carry no recommendation for men)

  • tamoxifen: 2 months after the final dose, with the label specifying non-hormonal contraception

  • letrozole: at least 3 weeks after the final dose

From 3 weeks to 14 months. That is why there is no universal answer, and why I will not give you a single number in an article.

That list of drug names is also a reminder: this piece is about contraception, but the drug you are on has its own emergency list. During treatment, a single ear or axillary reading of 38.0°C or above, shaking chills, sudden breathlessness or chest pain, swelling and pain in one calf, or heavy bleeding all mean the emergency department the same day — and say what treatment you are on when you arrive. Anyone on an immune checkpoint inhibitor has one more line: new diarrhoea, rash, cough, breathlessness, unusual tiredness, thirst or palpitations during treatment should not be handled by you as a cold or gastroenteritis — contact the team that day. The full fever threshold and what to do is in "The fever that cannot wait until morning".

Which method suits you depends on where the tumour is

Here there is a graded table you can look up. The US CDC's 2024 Medical Eligibility Criteria for Contraceptive Use (US MEC) gives a recommendation cell by cell: with current breast cancer, the copper intrauterine device is category 1 (can be used) and the progestogen-releasing device category 4 (not recommended); with breast cancer and five years with no evidence of disease, category 1 and category 3. For cervical cancer awaiting treatment and for endometrial cancer, "initiation" is category 4 and "continuation" of a device already in place category 2, on the grounds of raised infection and bleeding risk at insertion (plus perforation for endometrial cancer) and the likelihood it has to come out at treatment[10]. Category 4 means "not recommended", and the reason is concern about the hormone sensitivity of these tumours, not proof that they are made worse — copy the classification across, do not add a causal claim of your own.

The same standard lists breast, endometrial and ovarian cancer, and thrombophilia, among conditions in which pregnancy itself carries higher risk[10]. Combined pills containing oestrogen need one more layer of thought: the cancer, chemotherapy and a central venous catheter are all thrombosis risk factors, and recognising the symptoms is in "A swollen calf: can it wait until tomorrow?". As for when a device can be inserted, US MEC gives no category for chemotherapy-induced low blood counts, and I am not going to invent one; the principle is to avoid the nadir, so have both departments agree a date.

If unprotected sex has already happened, emergency contraception is an option and the sooner the better — one review records higher use of it among cancer survivors than in the general population, alongside a fall in contraceptive use after diagnosis[11]. It is a fallback, not a method.

If you are already pregnant, this article's job is to tell you who to see

Not to decide for you. Taiwan has its own figures: the National Health Insurance (健保) database linked with the cancer registry and birth notifications shows that from 2011 to 2020, 504 of 1.79 million pregnancies (0.03%) had a cancer diagnosed during the pregnancy, most often breast and cervical, with pregnancy-associated breast cancer trending upward[12]. Rare, not zero.

The first thing to do is get three parties around one table: obstetrics (or maternal-fetal medicine), oncology, and where needed genetic counselling. Bring three pieces of information: which drugs you have had, the exact gestational age, and the week at which the exposure occurred.

The legal framework exists. Article 9 of the Genetic Health Act (優生保健法) provides that a pregnant woman diagnosed with or certified as having one of the six circumstances listed in that article may undergo an induced abortion of her own will, with conditions including a minor's legal representative consenting[13]; Article 15 of the Enforcement Rules states that "an induced abortion shall be performed within 24 weeks of gestation, except where it constitutes a medical procedure"[14]. It is set out only so that you know it exists. Which clause applies to you and how the procedure runs is for you and your obstetrician to work out together, not something I can answer from a distance.

These three sentences — do not leave them to next time

First: for the drugs I am on, how long after treatment ends does contraception have to continue? (Ask your doctor to look at the Chinese label for that drug; do not use a generic answer from the internet.) Second: given where my tumour is and how I am being treated, which method suits me, and when can it start? Third: if it is the man on treatment, what does he need to watch for? Taiwan has no national standard for contraceptive counselling in cancer care, and I could not find a citable formal document — which means nobody will usually raise it, and you will have to open the subject yourself.

From the physician

The duration table shows how wide the spread is; it is not a prescription for you. It comes from US FDA labels, and your drug's Taiwanese label and your own team override it. If you are already pregnant, this article decides nothing — obstetrics, oncology and, where indicated, genetic counselling do, and they need your drugs, your exact gestational age and the week of exposure.
