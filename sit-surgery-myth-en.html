IS LETTING GO EASIER

Does exposure to air during surgery spread cancer?

After percutaneous needle biopsy in stage I lung cancer, the relative risk of pleural recurrence is 2.15. That is a needle tract, not air.

"If they cut you open and the air gets in, it spreads." I have been hearing that sentence in clinic for years. I used to say flatly that it was a myth. I no longer say that, because the research of the past few years has made it more complicated — there really is a true seed inside that sentence, only the seed is not the air. It is the needle. This article separates the needle, the knife and the air, and then writes the other half as well: the cost of delay, and of not having it done, which has been measured more precisely than most people realise.

Needle-tract seeding is real, it has a name, and somebody has counted it

Tumour cells growing back along the path a biopsy needle took is called needle-tract seeding, and it exists. A systematic review and meta-analysis of eight observational studies put the pooled incidence of tract seeding after biopsy of hepatocellular carcinoma at 2.7% (95% confidence interval 1.8% to 4.0%), which converts to roughly 0.9% per person-year, with heterogeneity between studies consistent with chance[1]. The year matters: that analysis stopped searching the literature in March 2007, which makes it an eighteen-year-old figure.

The lung cancer numbers of the past few years are larger than most people assume

A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis collected comparative cohort studies of stage I non-small-cell lung cancer treated with curative resection, comparing patients who had and had not undergone percutaneous transthoracic needle biopsy beforehand. Nine cohorts were eligible and eight entered the main pooled analysis (3,531 patients: 1,621 biopsied, 1,910 not). The relative risk of postoperative pleural recurrence was 2.15 (95% confidence interval 1.17 to 3.94). (A relative risk is a ratio of the rates in two groups; it does not mean every individual's chance doubles.) Where the tumour was already abutting the pleura, the figure was 5.05 (1.95 to 13.10); where it was not abutting, 1.84 (0.36 to 9.47), no difference detected. For recurrence at any site the relative risk was 1.79 (1.41 to 2.27)[2].

Two things go with those numbers. First, what the absolute probability is, I could not find out — the numerators and denominators of those eight cohorts are not in the abstract, so I can give you the relative risk and I cannot convert it into "so many in a hundred". Second, when the same analysis pooled the adjusted estimates from two of the cohorts, the confidence interval crossed 1 (2.26, 0.59 to 8.68), and the authors themselves write that these estimates come from observational designs and should be read with caution, and argue that the diagnostic route for pleura-abutting lesions should be individualised[2]. Individualised means discuss it with your doctor. It does not mean decide on your own not to have it.

Two single-institution retrospective studies sit underneath. A Korean hospital's 1,733 resections for clinical stage I non-small-cell lung cancer between 2018 and 2020: in the matched multivariable analysis, preoperative percutaneous needle biopsy was independently associated with isolated pleural recurrence (subdistribution hazard ratio 3.74, 1.06 to 13.20; p=0.040), as was visceral pleural invasion[3]. A Japanese hospital's 1,047 curative resections between 2008 and 2017: 191 patients (18.2%) recurred, and in 25 of them the ipsilateral pleura was the first site; in the multivariable analysis preoperative CT-guided needle biopsy was an independent risk factor (hazard ratio 6.92, 2.25 to 17.8), while fine-needle aspiration performed during surgery was not[4].

But that is the needle. Not the knife, and certainly not the air.

Every number above describes the same event: a needle passing through tissue and leaving cells along its own path. That is a puncture tract. It is not air.

And the mechanism itself — surgery exposing a tumour to air and thereby causing it to spread? I could not find a single study describing this mechanism. Not "the evidence is insufficient", not "it is disputed" — I searched and found no study, no review and no guideline discussing it. I stop here, because this is the whole of what I know. There is a true seed inside that sentence, but it has been planted in the wrong place.

Different biopsy routes carry different levels of risk. A narrative review of 58 papers published between January 2000 and August 2025 found, for seeding after transbronchial biopsy, only four fully documented cases; the review states that the true incidence remains unknown but is believed to be far lower than for the percutaneous route, and concludes directly that the diagnostic benefit of transbronchial biopsy clearly outweighs that very small seeding risk[5]. That is a review built mainly on case reports, and I cite it to show the difference in order of magnitude, not to suggest that anyone should ask for a different kind of biopsy — which route is used depends on where the lesion is, and that is the physician's judgement.

No biopsy, no treatment

This is the section I am most worried will be read backwards, so I will say it straight: not one of the numbers above supports "so do not have the biopsy". Without a pathological diagnosis there is no way to decide on treatment — without knowing what it is, which subtype it is, and whether there is a targetable change, nobody can plan your next step. In every source cited here, the authors' conclusion is either "individualise" or "the benefit outweighs the risk". Not one of them says don't do it.

The cost of delay, and of not having it done, has been measured more precisely than seeding

A systematic review and meta-analysis of the literature from 2000 to April 2020 restricted its main analysis to high-validity studies that controlled for the major prognostic factors: 34 studies, 17 treatment indications, 1,272,681 patients. In 13 of the 17, delay to treatment was significantly associated with a rise in the risk of death; the surgical findings are consistent, at a hazard ratio of about 1.06 to 1.08 per four weeks of delay (colectomy 1.06, 1.01 to 1.12; breast surgery 1.08, 1.03 to 1.13). For systemic therapy the range was 1.01 to 1.28[6].

The same study also has items with no association, and they belong here too: radical radiotherapy for head and neck cancer at 1.09 per four weeks (1.05 to 1.14), adjuvant radiotherapy after cervical cancer surgery at 1.23 (1.00 to 1.50), and adjuvant radiotherapy after breast-conserving surgery, where delay showed no significant association (0.98, 0.88 to 1.09); a further five radiotherapy indications and cervical cancer surgery had no high-validity data at all[6]. And a delay of a few weeks is not the same thing as a delay of six months.

The lethal part is not the delay. It is the treatment not given.

In the US National Cancer Database, among 1,901,815 patients with non-metastatic breast, prostate, lung or colorectal cancer treated between 2004 and 2013, 258 patients who used alternative medicine alongside conventional treatment were matched to 1,032 controls. That group did not start conventional treatment any later, but their refusal rates were far higher: surgery 7.0% versus 0.1%, chemotherapy 34.1% versus 3.2%, radiotherapy 53.0% versus 2.3%, hormone therapy 33.7% versus 2.8%. Five-year overall survival was 82.2% versus 86.6% (p=0.001), with a hazard ratio for death of 2.08 (1.50 to 2.90) — but once delay and refusal were entered into the model, the association became 1.39 (0.83 to 2.33) and was no longer significant. The authors' conclusion is that the mortality risk was mediated by refusal of conventional treatment[7]. A second study by the same group looked at 281 patients who used alternative medicine as their entire anti-cancer treatment and received no conventional treatment at all; after matching, the hazard ratio for overall death was 2.50 (1.88 to 3.27)[8].

What those two studies point at is not the therapies themselves. It is the treatment that did not happen. Both are retrospective analyses of American registry data, and "use of alternative medicine" was identified from a single database field ("unproven therapy administered by non-medical personnel") rather than by asking anyone; I could not find Taiwanese refusal or delay rates, so I do not print any. How to read the evidence on Chinese medicine and alternative therapies is in "Starving the tumour, and chemotherapy as poison"; which supplements really do collide with your drugs is in "Which supplements really do collide with your drugs".

Three questions to take to the surgical or interventional team

Whether to biopsy, which route to take, when to operate — all of those are decided by you together with the surgical, chest or interventional team, and no article can decide them for you. What you can say out loud is these three: "Is my tumour abutting the pleura?" "Is there a route to a diagnosis that avoids a percutaneous needle?" "If we follow the current plan, roughly how long will it be from today to surgery?" Biopsy and imaging are the steps that decide the treatment; arranging them is not "having extra tests". I could not find Taiwanese data on the incidence of seeding, so every number above comes from studies done elsewhere, and your own risk is a conversation for your attending physician. The goal of treatment shifting from cure to control is a different subject, and it is in "Giving up, and stopping one particular treatment".

From the physician

Every figure here comes from studies done abroad; I found no Taiwanese data on seeding, and the relative risks cannot be converted into an absolute chance, because those cohorts' numerators and denominators are not published. Whether to biopsy, which route to take and when to operate: take the three questions at the end of the article to your surgical or interventional team.
