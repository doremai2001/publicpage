BEYOND STANDARD TREATMENT

Before going abroad for treatment, count these costs

The real money is not in the treatment-fee column. Japan's published advanced-medicine averages are written for people with Japanese insurance.

Situation Guides · Beyond Standard Treatment · 7 references
#Treatment abroad#Cost & benefits#Decisions#Regulation#Reading evidence

Written as a list to be added up, because the variables are scattered: record formats and translation, the guarantor organisation's fee, weeks of accommodation and a companion, full payment in foreign currency, the exchange rate. The two most often skipped decide it — am I a candidate, and who takes over at home.

"Doctor, we are thinking of going to Japan for carbon ion." What I usually ask next is: who picks it up when you come back? This article compares no technologies and names no institution — protons, carbon ions and boron neutron capture each have their own piece, in "Protons: precise. Then what?" and the carbon-ion and BNCT articles. What gets counted here is different: what the trip buys, and who takes over afterwards.

Six steps, with the money and the risk sitting at the first and the last

The order runs: one, get records, pathology slides and imaging into a format the other country accepts, and translated; two, a remote assessment of whether your disease makes you a candidate at all; three, find a guarantor organisation and apply for the visa; four, flights, accommodation and a companion, counted in weeks; five, the treatment fee, in full, in advance, in foreign currency; six, come home to a follow-up system that had no part in the treatment.

Most people spend the most time researching step four and the least on steps two and six. Those are the two that decide whether the trip is worth making.

The number Japan publishes is written for people with Japanese insurance

Japan has moved some particle therapy indications out of "advanced medicine" (先進医療) and into public insurance; what remains inside the advanced-medicine scheme is paid entirely by the patient, with neither public insurance nor the high-cost medical expense system applying. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's performance report for FY Reiwa 6 (July 2023 to June 2024) gives the scale: carbon ion therapy, 442 cases across 7 institutions, advanced-medicine costs totalling about ¥1.39 billion; proton therapy, 827 cases across 19 institutions, about ¥2.22 billion. Divide the totals by the case counts (that division is mine; both figures are in the report): about ¥3.14 million per case for carbon ion, about ¥2.68 million per case for protons. Mean inpatient stay in the same report was 14.7 days for protons and 3.2 days for carbon ion[1] (verified 2026-08-30). Take those two figures as a magnitude, not a quotation: they are average amounts under Japan's own domestic advanced-medicine scheme, and for what a foreign patient is actually charged I could find no public source to cite — that number only exists on the written quotation you are given.

Three things have to be read together here. First, that is the price Japanese patients pay — someone travelling from Taiwan is on a free-price basis (自由診療) and is not bound by that schedule. Second, it covers one item only: not flights, accommodation, a companion, translation, the guarantor organisation's service fee, pre-travel investigations, treatments given alongside, or any complication. Third, yen has to be converted into New Taiwan dollars and the rate moves daily. I am not converting it here, because that number would expire faster than this article — do the sum yourself at the rate on the day you pay, and ask who carries the exchange difference.

And: bringing something into insurance coverage is a decision about paying, not a finding that it is better. That is "Approved, covered, effective: three different things".

The visa route has a legal structure, and that includes the intermediary

Japan has a dedicated medical stay visa. The Ministry of Foreign Affairs' own page: it is for "foreign patients and others resident overseas visiting Japan for the purpose of receiving treatment"; the period of stay is up to 90 days, 6 months or 1 year, decided by the patient's condition; a multiple-entry visa runs up to 3 years with each stay within 90 days; companions need not be relatives and may travel as required; and the applicant must go through a guarantor organisation registered under the scheme (身元保証機関), which arranges the hospital appointment and issues the documents the visa requires[2] (verified 2026-08-30).

One thing here is worth saying neutrally. The guarantor organisation is a commercial intermediary whose role is defined in regulation. Its service fee is part of the total cost, and its incentives do not overlap completely with yours. That is not to say it cannot be trusted. It is that the assessment it gives you cannot stand as a second opinion.

Back in Taiwan: the NHI will not refund it, and policies have to be read one by one

NHI reimbursement of self-advanced medical expenses is restricted by the regulation to "an unforeseen emergency illness, injury or childbirth arising suddenly and requiring immediate treatment at a local medical institution", and the application must be made within 6 months of the day of emergency or outpatient treatment or the day of discharge[3]. Planned treatment abroad is not within that scope. Even for a genuine emergency the refund is capped, and the caps are announced quarterly — for 民國115 Q3, NT$1,162 per outpatient visit, NT$3,866 per emergency visit and NT$7,239 per inpatient day[4] (verified 2026-08-30). Put those two facts side by side and the paragraph is finished; it needs no adjectives.

Private insurance is the other half. Article 2 of the model clauses for hospital medical expense insurance, reimbursement type (住院醫療費用保險單示範條款（實支實付型）) defines "hospital" as "a public, private or medical-corporation hospital holding a practice licence under the Medical Care Act and having wards for the admission of patients"[5] — and the Medical Care Act (醫療法) is a Taiwanese statute. A policy written to those clauses is built around Taiwanese hospitals; whether yours reaches a foreign one has to be read policy by policy. There is also a separate set of model clauses for an overseas sudden-illness medical insurance rider[6], and the key words in its title are again sudden illness, not planned treatment.

The action is concrete: phone the insurer and ask whether the definition of "hospital" in your policy wording includes hospitals outside Taiwan, and ask for the answer in writing. Do not accept it verbally. How to ask about a price and get a written itemisation is in "How to ask about a self-pay item".

Ask the earlier question first: can this be done in Taiwan?

There is an official clue few people know about. Proton therapy already has codes in the NHI schedule of medical service payment items: N21301 to N21308, covering CT and MR simulation, immobilisation device fabrication, proton beam treatment per session, stereotactic radiosurgery of brain and body, and the treatment planning fee, effective 2016-12-05 — but every one carries 0 points, with the remark "HTA項目"[7] (verified 2026-08-30). Registered and tracked, in other words, but not paid: in Taiwan too it is self-pay.

What that record is useful for is turning the question from "should we go abroad" into "can this be done in Taiwan, and if so, what is the difference?" Asked that way, it forces out what is actually worth comparing: a newer, costlier machine does not automatically mean a better outcome — "New does not mean better". As for the American clinical trial route — eligibility for foreign nationals, visa category, who pays for standard-of-care costs inside a trial — I verified no citable rule this time, so this article does not cover it, and please do not infer a direction from fragments found elsewhere. What a trial is and is not is in "A trial is not treatment designed for you".

Which variables make it reasonable, and which do not

The combination that makes it reasonable to enquire: the disease appears on the published particle therapy indication lists; the same site has not been irradiated before; the disease is localised, not widely spread; the body can take several weeks of travel and lie still through treatment; the money is genuinely idle money; and the team in Taiwan has been asked, and is willing to take the follow-up back.

The combination that makes it unreasonable: the disease is already widespread; the same site has been irradiated (usually a hard limit, and worth confirming before any money is spent); physical condition makes the long flight itself a risk; spending this money leaves the treatment and living costs that come next unsupportable; and nobody has agreed to take over the care once you are home — that does not disappear because you went abroad.

One more thing to count in: travel with advanced disease carries its own risk — clots, infection, deteriorating somewhere nobody speaks your language. Not scaremongering; a question to ask while planning the itinerary. And one thing to say at the front: not going abroad does not mean you have left something undone. Standard treatment available in Taiwan is standard treatment. What this article works out is what the extra trip buys, not "you lose if you stay".

Before you book the flights, ask who takes over when you get back

  1. In what format do my records, images and pathology go out, and in what format do they come back? The dose and volume records especially — if radiotherapy is ever needed again in Taiwan, that file is not optional.

  2. Who is responsible if side effects appear during treatment or after I return? Write "who picks this up in Taiwan" into the plan, rather than leaving it until the day something goes wrong.

  3. What does the quotation include and exclude, and what happens if treatment stops partway? In writing.

From the physician

Two things are worth settling before any money moves: previous radiotherapy to the same site is usually a hard limit, and someone in Taiwan has to agree to take the follow-up back. Bring the dose and volume records home — if radiotherapy is ever needed again, that file is not optional. And to say first: not going abroad does not mean you have left something undone.
