Skin reactions: when they come, how to care for them

The timeline has only three anchors and the peak falls about 2 weeks after the end; randomised trials threw out the bans on washing and deodorant, and broken skin with weeping is the grade that needs medical assessment within 24 hours.

Breast Radiotherapy Guide · Treatment and Self-Care · 19 references
#Skin Care#Side Effects#Daily Life

It is not one or two minor rules that have been overturned: no washing, no deodorant — both lost to randomised trials. This article also draws the other line clearly — which situations are not solved by a different pot of cream, but need the clinical side to see them within a day.

On the day of the last fraction, plenty of people say "graduated at last" at the treatment room door. I usually keep them a minute longer to say something that runs against the intuition: the skin has not handed in its homework yet — the reddest, sorest days are often not during the course but after it. This article covers skin reactions from end to end: when they come, where they show up, which of the old rules randomised trials have thrown out, which products actually have evidence behind them. And the most important part — which situations are not a question of what to put on, but a question of coming back and letting us look.

My position first: every technique compared in this topic — ordinary intensity-modulated radiotherapy, TOMO, deep inspiration breath-hold — has a self-paid component among the more expensive options, and all of them are delivered in my own department; protons mean a referral elsewhere. So every "better" here comes with its primary source and the level of evidence behind it, and on cost I cite the official schedule where one exists and tell you to ask the medical affairs office where it does not. Concluding that the basic option is enough for you is a completely reasonable conclusion.

The timeline has only three anchors: it arrives, it peaks, it fades

The order of magnitude first: taking radiotherapy to all sites together, roughly 90 to 95% of people develop radiation dermatitis to some degree[1] — in breast radiotherapy almost everyone gets some, and the difference is how deep it goes. The shape of the timing has three anchors. Arrival: the nursing guideline from BC Cancer in British Columbia says the reaction usually appears 1 to 4 weeks after treatment starts[2]. Peak: a prospective observational study in a conventionally fractionated population (mostly 50 Gy in 25 fractions) followed 148 breast cancer patients, and the proportion with a grade 2 reaction peaked at 61.9% two weeks after the end of treatment, with grade 3 peaking at the same point; across techniques and doses, the peak fell 1 to 2 weeks after the end[3]. Fading: the guideline says the reaction can persist for several weeks after the end[2], and medically the acute phase is bounded at 90 days[1].

So finishing is not the same as being finished. If the skin goes on reddening in those two weeks after the end, or even starts to break down, you have not done anything wrong — that is the reaction's own timetable. Do not let your guard down, and do not panic either. Anyone on a hypofractionated course has one extra piece of good news: the acute toxicity substudy of the FAST-Forward randomised trial found that erythema on the 1-week schedule was milder than on the 3-week schedule and settled about 2 weeks earlier[4]. A finer week-by-week script of what happens when does not exist in the literature, and I am not inventing one.

Where the skin breaks first: the folds

The international grading standard defines grade 2 moist desquamation — broken skin, weeping — as "mostly confined to skin folds"[2][4] — brought down to breast radiotherapy, that means the axilla and the inframammary fold. Who gets the heavier reaction also has numbers behind it: a randomised trial of breast intensity-modulated radiotherapy found breast size independently associated with the risk of moist desquamation, with larger breasts at higher risk[5]. For chest wall irradiation after mastectomy with a layer of bolus (tissue-equivalent material) on the skin, a systematic review of 27 studies calculated a pooled grade 3 dermatitis rate of 9.6%, against 1.2% without bolus[6] — if you have had a mastectomy and feel your reaction is worse than other people's, this is often the reason. The same randomised trial also showed that breast intensity-modulated radiotherapy brought moist desquamation down from 47.8% in the wedge era to 31.2%[5]; that is routine practice today, not care you have to pay extra to get.

You can wash, and you can use deodorant: two bans randomised trials threw out

The old patient advice was that you must not wash and must not put anything on during treatment. Randomised trials overturned both. Washing: a randomised study of 99 patients divided people into a no-washing group and a wash-as-usual group, and moist desquamation was 33% in the no-washing group against 14% in the washing group; the authors concluded that washing should not be discouraged[7]. Do it the way the guideline sets out: lukewarm water, wash gently with the palm of the hand, a mild unscented soap, pat dry with a soft towel rather than rubbing[2]. Deodorant: a randomised non-inferiority trial in 84 patients used an aluminium-containing antiperspirant directly — a non-inferiority design asks whether the thing being tested is any worse than not using it — and grade 2 axillary dermatitis was 23% in the deodorant group against 30% in the group not using it, meeting non-inferiority, with nobody at any point developing a grade 3 or higher reaction[8]. The guideline's current wording is that it can be continued while the skin is dry and intact[2].

The rest of daily life is written more loosely in the guideline than most people assume: an electric razor is fine, loose cotton clothing is fine, a chlorinated pool is fine — rinse off and moisturise after swimming, and stop if the skin has broken[2].

What to avoid falls into three groups. Physical irritation: tape or plasters inside the treated area, ice packs or heat pads applied directly, scratching and hard rubbing. Chemical irritation, which matters especially if the field includes the axilla: no shaving the underarm, no waxing or depilatory creams, no perfumed products. And sun: from treatment until it has healed, cover it with clothing[2].

Creams and ointments: the list with evidence is much shorter than the shelf

Start with the strongest item: prophylactic steroid cream. A double-blind randomised study of 49 patients found mometasone cream with an emollient significantly reduced acute dermatitis[9]; a second double-blind trial of 176 patients, N06C4, has to be given from both sides — the primary endpoint (maximum grade as scored by the clinician) showed no difference, but patient-reported itching, burning and bother were significantly lower[10]. The grade does not necessarily come down; the symptoms genuinely are easier to live with. An analysis pooling 13 randomised trials and 1,172 breast cancer patients calculated that topical steroids brought the odds ratio for moist desquamation down to 0.31 — an odds ratio is not a multiple of the probability, but the direction is unambiguous: less chance of skin breaking and weeping[11]. The 2023 guideline from MASCC, the Multinational Association of Supportive Care in Cancer, also formally recommends mometasone and betamethasone for prevention[12]. This is a prescription decision — discuss it with your radiation oncologist rather than buying something at the chemist. Plain moisturising has a meta-analysis too: 13 randomised trials, 1,203 people (breast and head and neck combined), with a higher chance of developing no dermatitis at all and markedly less itching among moisturiser users, though not every endpoint differed[13] — moisturising helps; do not expect it to be a shield.

The list that does not work, and a recommendation list with six items on it

For the products most often recommended, this is what the randomised trials say. Aloe vera: a randomised study of 225 patients found aloe gel did not reduce skin reactions, and an inexpensive aqueous cream did better on dry desquamation and pain[14]. Trolamine cream: the RTOG 97-13 randomised trial found no difference against ordinary supportive care[15]. Calendula: two trials contradict each other — a 254-patient trial found it better than trolamine[16], but the comparator there was itself a loser; a blinded trial of 411 patients found no difference at all against ordinary aqueous cream[17]. The MASCC guideline sums the whole field up bluntly: only six interventions are recommended for prevention (photobiomodulation, Mepitel film, Hydrofilm, mometasone, betamethasone, olive oil), only one dressing is recommended for management, and most other interventions are not recommended, for insufficient evidence or lack of consensus[12]. Most of the advice you find online sits in that "other".

Mepitel film, on that list, has the strongest preventive evidence: a phase III randomised trial (376 patients) in a high-risk population with large breasts or after mastectomy found grade 2 to 3 dermatitis fell from 45.6% under standard care to 15.5%[18] — those are numbers from a high-risk population, not the going rate for an ordinary whole-breast irradiation population; and 8% of the film group still developed moist desquamation[18], so it is not a charm. On how these dressings are obtained and charged for in Taiwan, I could find no citable official source; whether your hospital uses them and how the cost works is a question for your radiation oncology nurse.

These situations are not a care problem — they are a come-back-in problem

All of the self-care above gives way when it meets this section. First, moist desquamation: broken skin, blisters, weeping. The BC Cancer guideline grades this as "urgent — medical assessment required within 24 hours"[2]. The meaning is plain: come back and let us look at it. Do not buy dressings and cover an open wound over yourself — what a wound is dressed with, and how it is changed, is a decision made after clinical assessment[2][12]. Second, the signs of infection, which the guideline sets out as four items: fever; odour from the wound; purulent discharge; pain and swelling spreading beyond the treated area[2] — if any one of these appears, that is a clinic visit, not more moisturiser; rapidly worsening pain and a wound that opens up are handled by the same rule. This course has one safety net built into it: you see the treatment team almost every day, so raise any change at the next session rather than holding out until your clinic date.

Three habits you can start after tonight's shower

One sentence on cost first: the NHI fee schedule contains a reimbursed item called "skin care for radiotherapy" (37026B), so skin care procedures during radiotherapy do have a corresponding item under National Health Insurance[19]; which specialist dressings are used and what they cost differs between hospitals and is decided after clinical assessment, so ask the medical affairs office or your radiation oncology nurse. Then three habits you can start today. One: every night after your shower, look at two places in the mirror — the axilla, and the fold under the breast; changes usually start there. Two: before you put any new product on, bring it to clinic and ask first. Three: write "broken skin or weeping, fever, odour, pus, pain and swelling spreading beyond the treated area" into the notes app on your phone — if any one of them turns up, you do not wait for the next appointment.

From the physician

There is plenty you can do about your skin: wash as usual, moisturise regularly, ask before you put anything new on. But broken skin with weeping, fever, odour from the wound, pus, and pain and swelling spreading beyond the treated area — those five are not self-care, they are come back and let us look at it. You see the treatment team every day; do not hold out until your clinic date.
