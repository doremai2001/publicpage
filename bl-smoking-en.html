TAIWAN, AND AFTERWARDS

Smoking and this disease

The evidence linking smoking to whether this disease occurs is hard; the evidence on whether stopping after diagnosis keeps it from coming back is empty. Two cells of different strength, written separately here.

Bladder Cancer Guide · Taiwan, and afterwards · 10 references
#Quitting#Prevention#Evidence#Quality of Life

Up to 2022, the whole literature on stopping smoking after diagnosis and recurrence came to 9 eligible studies and about 5,500 people, and pooled they give a sub-distribution hazard ratio of 0.99. That cell is not a small effect; it is evidence that has not been produced yet. The incidence cell is nothing like that strength.

A risk factor is a statistic epidemiology works out across a very large number of people. It is not a personal fault. I say that first in clinic, because a conversation about smoking turns into a post-mortem more easily than any other, and a post-mortem does nothing for treatment. This article is not going back to look for a cause. It is separating two things that get said in one breath: what smoking has to do with whether this disease occurs, and what happens when someone already diagnosed stops. The evidence in those two boxes is of completely different strength.

One more thing first, in case anyone decides this article is not about them. In the paired upper tract urothelial carcinoma cohorts of hospitals in Kaohsiung and Hirosaki (765 patients in Taiwan against 325 in Japan), the Taiwanese patients had less of a smoking history than the Japanese ones (p less than 0.001)[1]. In Taiwan, a large part of this disease grows in people who never smoked at all.

The incidence column, where the evidence is hard

A meta-analysis of 89 observational studies and 57,145 bladder cancers: against people who never smoked, the pooled odds ratio for current smokers is 3.14 (95% confidence interval 2.53 to 3.75; a confidence interval means that if you ran the same study many times over, the answer would land in that range), and for former smokers 1.83 (1.52 to 2.14)[2]. An odds ratio is the odds of having it against not having it in each group, not a multiple of probability, so it reads far more clearly alongside absolute numbers. The American NIH-AARP cohort (281,394 men, 186,134 women, 4.519 million person-years) gives incidence as 39.8 per 100,000 person-years in those who never smoked, 119.8 in former smokers (hazard ratio 2.22) and 177.3 in current smokers (hazard ratio 4.06, 95% confidence interval 3.66 to 4.50; a hazard ratio divides the rate at which events happen in one group by the rate in the other)[3]. Another meta-analysis (83 bladder cancer studies) gives relative risks of 2.58 for all smokers, 3.47 for current and 2.04 for former smokers[4]. Three independent pieces of work landing on the same order of magnitude: this is not a column I am going to call weak.

Amount and years matter, but not in proportion

The same 89-study meta-analysis takes the dose apart: risk rises step by step with the number of years smoked, but intensity plateaus after about 15 cigarettes a day and cumulative amount after about 50 pack-years — beyond that the increase is no longer proportional; and the younger someone was when they started, the higher the risk[2]. Two more sentences from the same paper have to be read together: the benefit of stopping is clearest "from 20 years before diagnosis" onwards, and even after stopping a long time ago, risk does not return to the level of someone who never smoked[2]. That second sentence does not mean "so there was no point stopping". It means this curve was never a reversible straight line.

A population attributable fraction is an estimate at population level — how much a population could in theory be spared if the factor were absent — and not a verdict handed down on any one person. The European estimate is 43% in men and 26% in women[2]; the American cohort gives 50% in men and 52% in women[3]. Different populations, different eras; they are not interchangeable. For Taiwan's own attributable proportion I could not find any local estimate at all, so I am not carrying the European or American figures over and calling them Taiwan.

The upper tract side is a different set of numbers

The label cannot be dropped. The European Association of Urology's 2025 upper tract guideline says tobacco exposure raises the relative risk of upper tract urothelial carcinoma by 2.5 to 7.0 times, and that in a registry of 2,380 people across 101 centres in 29 countries, 53.3% were former or current smokers[5]. But Taiwan is a different shape — which is the sentence this article opened with: Taiwanese upper tract cancer patients have a lower proportion of smokers than their Japanese counterparts[1]. Why the upper tract is so much commoner in Taiwan is written up in "It grows in the upper tract too".

Does stopping after diagnosis keep it from coming back? The evidence in this cell is empty

The level of evidence in this section is nothing like the level above it, and I have given it a section of its own precisely so that the two do not get used interchangeably. Up to January 2022, a systematic review of stopping smoking after diagnosis and recurrence found only 9 eligible papers and about 5,500 bladder cancer patients, and the great majority of those were non-muscle-invasive (only 1 paper included muscle-invasive disease). The pooled sub-distribution hazard ratio is 0.99 (95% confidence interval 0.61 to 1.61) — a sub-distribution hazard ratio compares recurrence risk once competing events such as death are counted in as well, and 0.99 means no lower recurrence risk can be seen in those who stopped than in those who carried on; overall survival, cancer-specific survival and progression-free survival had only 3 studies each, and those conflicted with one another[6].

The one prospective cohort to be found explains why it comes out that way. The British BCPP study enrolled 722 patients with non-muscle-invasive disease (pTa, pT1, CIS), median follow-up 4.21 years; of those who were smoking at diagnosis only 25 (14%) stopped during follow-up, and stopping was unrelated to recurrence risk (p=0.352). The authors' own conclusion is that too few people stopped for this study to answer the question[7].

So the correct way to say it is "the evidence in this cell is empty" — not "stopping does not help", and not "stopping stops it coming back". And the guideline wording has to be laid alongside: the European Association of Urology's 2026 non-muscle-invasive guideline still writes that "smoking increases the risk of tumour recurrence and progression in NMIBC patients", but its recommendation is the single line "Counsel smokers to stop smoking", and the reason given in the text is the harm smoking does to health overall, not a reduction in recurrence[8]. The guideline and the meta-analysis are not in conflict; they are talking about two different things.

The mortality cell: smaller numbers, and imprecise ones

The same 83-study meta-analysis separately worked out disease-specific mortality: relative risk 1.47 (1.24 to 1.75) for all smokers, 1.53 (1.12 to 2.09) for current smokers, and 1.44 (0.99 to 2.11) for former smokers — that last confidence interval crosses 1[4]. For people who have already stopped, 1.44 is the lowest of those three point estimates, but its confidence interval crosses 1 and statistically it is uncertain. That is the honest shape of it. It is neither good news nor bad news.

One cell where the evidence is positive: stopping is a thing that can be done

The non-muscle-invasive guideline cites a randomised trial in which an intensive cessation programme given around the time of resection produced a quit rate of 36%, against 6% with standard health education[8]. The evidence in this cell is positive: whether someone manages to stop has a lot to do with whether anyone is alongside them while they do it.

Taiwan has both a legal basis and a service. Article 24 of the Tobacco Hazards Prevention Act (amended 15 February 2023) reads, in translation of the clause: "The central competent authority may designate medical institutions and public-interest organisations to provide smoking cessation services, and may subsidise them. Competent authorities at each level may give awards to outstanding medical institutions and public-interest organisations under the preceding paragraph."[9] The shape of the actual service follows the Health Promotion Administration's operating notes for the smoking cessation service subsidy programme: cessation treatment applies to "an NHI insured person aged 18 or over whose nicotine dependence test score is 4 or above … or who smokes an average of 10 or more cigarettes a day, and who has a clear intention to stop", while cessation health education has "no age limit". The course is limited to "two courses per calendar year, each course limited to eight cessation consultation fees, eight weeks of medication, eight cessation health-education fees and three case follow-up fees (at three months, six months and one year)", and "each course is to be completed within ninety days at the same institution"[10]. Subsidy amounts and current conditions follow whatever the Health Promotion Administration has announced at the time; how to book, and whether anything is self-paid, is a question for the hospital's smoking cessation clinic or your case manager. I did not get the quitline number from an official page, so I am not writing one down from memory.

Not only tobacco — and having done those jobs does not mean you will get it

Occupational exposure is the second largest risk factor for bladder cancer. The guideline says that occupational exposure to aromatic amines, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and chlorinated hydrocarbons accounts for around 10% of all bladder cancers, mostly in industrial settings that handle paint, dye, metal and petroleum products — and the sentence immediately after it has to be read with it: in developed industrial settings these risks have fallen with occupational safety regulation, and the incidence of bladder cancer in chemical workers is no longer higher than in the general population[8]. That sentence is for the old hands: having done that work does not mean you were going to get this. Metals and arsenic are covered in "Electroplating, welding, and that old well" and organic solvents in "Washing your hands in solvent"; I am not rewriting them.

A few things that get asked often. Low-tar cigarettes do not lower the risk. The risk from e-cigarettes has not been adequately assessed, although carcinogens are already detectable in users' urine — so I will not tell you they are safer, and I will not tell you they cause cancer just the same. Second-hand smoke exposure is associated with a rise in bladder cancer risk[4][8]. That last one is written down so a household can stop together, not so anyone can be blamed. The upper tract side has to be more conservative still: the guideline states plainly that beyond smoking and aristolochic acid there is no strong evidence supporting a causative role for other factors[5].

If you are still smoking, one thing that can be done today

Not making a resolution — booking an appointment. Get the sentence "I want to stop; does the hospital have a cessation clinic?" said out loud at the next visit, so that it becomes part of the treatment rather than something running alongside it. That difference between 36% and 6% is a difference in whether someone is doing it with you, not a difference in willpower[8]. If you stopped many years ago, this article is not asking you to go back and settle accounts. If you never smoked and got this disease anyway, that is an ordinary thing to have happened; Taiwan's data look exactly like that. There is only one direction for the sentence to confirm with your doctor: what my treatment and follow-up schedule looks like over the next twelve months.
