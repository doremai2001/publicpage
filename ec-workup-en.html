HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

The tests that decide whether surgery is possible

Which question endoscopic ultrasound, CT, PET and bronchoscopy each answer, how accurate each is, and which findings turn the treatment route sharply.

Oesophageal Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 16 references
#Staging#Imaging#Treatment Pathway#Decisions

"Can I have the operation" has no answer on the day of diagnosis; the answer lies in the tests that follow, and every one of them misses something — knowing where each misses matters more than knowing how accurate it is. Fitness and your own wishes are a further layer of decision, outside the tests altogether.

"Doctor, can I have the operation?" At the first visit I usually cannot answer that — not because I am dodging, but because the answer is not in yet. It is in the handful of tests that come next. Whether an oesophageal cancer can be cut out depends on three things: whether the tumour has grown through the outer wall of the oesophagus and reached the trachea or a major vessel, where the lymph nodes have gone, and whether there is distant spread; plus a fourth, whether your body can withstand that operation. Below I go through the tests one by one: which question each answers, how accurate it is, where it misses, and which findings turn the route sharply.

Endoscopic ultrasound, CT, PET: three tests that are not looking at the same thing

The three tests divide the work; each looks at one thing. A 2008 meta-analysis in the British Journal of Cancer set the three tools side by side on the question "have the lymph nodes next to the oesophagus been invaded": endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) sensitivity 0.80, specificity 0.70; computed tomography (CT) 0.50 and 0.83; FDG-PET 0.57 and 0.85[1]. Sensitivity is "of those with disease, what proportion gets caught"; specificity is "of those without disease, what proportion gets correctly cleared". So EUS is the sharpest at catching regional nodes, at the cost of wrongly labelling about 30% of people without nodal spread as having it; CT and PET raise fewer false alarms but miss more. Depth is EUS's home ground: another meta-analysis of 49 studies and 2,558 patients, using surgical pathology as the standard, found EUS sensitivity 81.6% and specificity 99.4% for T1, and sensitivity 92.4% and specificity 97.4% for T4 (grown through the outer wall into a neighbouring structure); adding fine-needle aspiration raised the sensitivity of nodal staging from 84.7% to 96.7%[2]. Distant metastasis is PET's home ground: PET sensitivity 0.71 and specificity 0.93, CT 0.52 and 0.91, and the meta-analysis concluded that PET is significantly better and that using all three together has clinical value[1].

The CT is already clear, so why do a PET as well

Because among people whose CT is clear, PET will still find another batch. The American College of Surgeons Oncology Group's prospective multicentre trial Z0060 enrolled only thoracic oesophageal cancer patients with no metastasis visible on CT who were heading for surgery; 189 were analysable: PET found biopsy-confirmed distant metastasis in at least 4.8% (95% confidence interval 2.2 to 8.9%), with a few more PET-positive findings that were never confirmed and a few PET-negative cases discovered at operation; in the end 22% of patients did not undergo oesophagectomy[3]. A Belgian prospective study of 74 patients: PET's accuracy for stage IV was 82%, against 64% for CT plus EUS; PET changed the stage in 22% of patients, upstaging 15% and downstaging 7%[4]. Another systematic review of 12 studies gave a sensitivity of 0.67 and specificity of 0.97 for distant metastasis[5]. Turn those numbers around: a negative PET does not mean no metastasis — it misses around 30%; and its sensitivity for the nodes next to the oesophagus is only 33% to 57%[1][4], so that question is handed to EUS. A Dutch retrospective study of 279 patients asked "once PET/CT has been done, what does EUS still change": it changed management in 28.7% of patients, mainly the radiotherapy field (22.6%) and the extent of lymph node dissection (17.2%); it changed "whether cure is possible" in only 1.8%[6]. This is why most of my patients have both: one decides whether to take the curative road at all, the other decides where that road's boundaries are drawn.

Tumour above the carina: bronchoscopy is not optional

The front of the upper and middle oesophagus lies against the trachea and the left main bronchus. For a tumour above the carina — the point where the trachea forks — I always arrange bronchoscopy, and it has to include biopsy, not just a look. Two prospective studies from Munich made the case. The 1998 one (116 patients): 32% of tumours above the carina had a visible abnormality at bronchoscopy; among those with a normal appearance, 98.5% truly had no invasion (the negative predictive value), but among those with an abnormal appearance the proportion with real invasion was not high (a low positive predictive value), and misjudgement was especially easy after radiotherapy; with multi-site biopsy and brushing added, overall accuracy was 95.8%; 9.7% of patients originally judged operable were excluded from surgery because bronchoscopy proved airway invasion; bronchoscopy and CT disagreed in 40% of cases, with bronchoscopy the more specific[7]. The 2001 study expanded to 166 patients: 18.1% of those originally operable changed route as a result, overall accuracy 93.3%; all 6 false negatives were patients who had had preoperative treatment before surgery[8]. A Japanese prospective study of 59 patients compared accuracy in diagnosing tracheobronchial invasion: bronchoscopy 78%, endobronchial ultrasound 91%, EUS 85%, CT only 58%[9]. That 10 to 20% of patients, if they go to the operating table without a bronchoscopy, find out mid-operation that the tumour cannot come out.

T4: touching the trachea or a major vessel, and the road turns

In staging terms, T4b means the tumour has invaded a structure that cannot be resected: trachea, bronchus, aorta. This box does not get resection; it goes to chemoradiotherapy first. If the tumour then shrinks and starts to "look resectable", should it be operated on? The Japan Esophageal Society's 2022 guideline, question 14, in its original wording: "There is only weak evidence to recommend surgical resection for patients with unresectable, locally advanced esophageal cancer (cT4 [e.g., aorta, trachea, bronchus] N0-3M0) that becomes resectable after definitive chemoradiotherapy or induction chemotherapy" — a weak recommendation, evidence level C; the same guideline gives a weak recommendation for definitive chemoradiotherapy in unresectable cStage IVA[10]. A 2022 meta-analysis from a Singapore team (23 studies, 1,119 patients, 93% SCC) set the survival of T4 patients treated with "chemoradiotherapy then surgery" beside "chemoradiotherapy alone": five-year overall survival 20% against 10%, treatment-related fistula 4% against 9%, treatment-related death 3% in each; the authors themselves stressed the lack of high-quality evidence and that the two groups are fundamentally not comparable — the people who can be operated on are the ones who responded well and were fit to begin with[11]. So those two numbers cannot be read as "adding surgery doubles survival". The T4 box has its own place on "The oesophageal cancer treatment map: which box are you in".

Why cervical oesophageal cancer is almost never operated on

The topmost segment of the oesophagus sits in the neck. It accounts for a small share — in Japanese data about 5%, with the middle thoracic segment 47%, lower 28%, upper 12%, abdominal 8%[10] — but it is handled differently from the thoracic oesophagus. This segment sits right against the larynx, and the extent and cost of surgery here are not the same thing as in the chest. The footnote to ESMO's 2025 interim-update algorithm says it directly: "Even many experienced centres prefer definitive CRT for oesophageal tumours with a very proximal/cervical location" — even many experienced centres prefer definitive chemoradiotherapy for tumours very close to the neck; the same footnote also says that patients unwilling to have, or medically unfit for, major surgery should preferentially receive definitive chemoradiotherapy[12]. So when a patient with a cervical tumour hears "no operation", that is not because the disease is worse than other people's; it is the anatomy of this segment that decides it. Hypopharyngeal cancer and cervical oesophageal cancer are anatomical neighbours, but the former belongs to head and neck cancer and is not covered in this topic.

Your report will show three sets of stages: c, p and yp cannot be converted into each other

The AJCC 8th edition (from 2017) set up three staging tables for oesophageal cancer: clinical stage before treatment (cTNM), pathological stage for surgery alone (pTNM), and pathological stage after chemoradiotherapy followed by surgery (ypTNM). The reason for three tables is that the survival each corresponds to is different; the same "T3N1" means something different on each of the three; SCC and adenocarcinoma are grouped separately for cTNM and pTNM, while ypTNM is shared[13]. The 8th edition also split T1 into T1a and T1b, and redrew the oesophagus-specific regional lymph node map and the definition of the gastro-oesophageal junction[14]. The translation for patients: the stage before surgery, the stage after surgery, and the stage after chemoradiotherapy then surgery are three different tables; they cannot be converted into each other, and do not take a post-chemoradiotherapy yp stage and look it up in an online "how long do people with stage X live" table. One more common misunderstanding: repeating the EUS after chemoradiotherapy to see whether "anything is still there" — in a 2015 meta-analysis of 724 patients, EUS after chemoradiotherapy had a sensitivity of only 43% for T4, and for lymph nodes a sensitivity of 69% and specificity of 52%; the authors wrote outright that patients restaged as T4 by EUS should not be excluded from surgery on that basis[15]. How accurate response assessment after chemoradiotherapy really is lies at the heart of "After chemoradiotherapy, do you still need surgery".

The rhythm of the first month, and the sentence to say out loud

Between the endoscopic biopsy that confirms the diagnosis and the start of treatment, these tests have to happen: EUS, CT and PET once each, bronchoscopy added for upper and middle tumours, then the multidisciplinary team meeting. The Japan Esophageal Society guideline's overview for cStage II/III is: once the clinical stage has been correctly established with endoscopy, CT and PET, confirm first whether the patient can tolerate surgery[10] — the tests answer whether the tumour can be cut out; fitness and your own wishes answer whether you want it cut out, and those are two separate layers of decision. On the administrative side, oesophageal cancer falls under the catastrophic illness category "cancers requiring active or long-term treatment"; the certificate is valid for five years, the doctor issues a diagnosis certificate plus a medical summary, and the hospital can file it on your behalf[16]. During the weeks of waiting for tests, if your weight drops by more than 10%, speak up; the feeding tube question is in "Can't swallow, weight falling: a feeding tube first?". At the visit where all the results are back, bring this sentence: "Given my depth, my lymph nodes, whether there is distant spread, and where the tumour sits, which box of the treatment map am I in?" That map is "The oesophageal cancer treatment map: which box are you in".

From the physician

At the visit when all the results are back, bring four questions: how deep, where the nodes are, whether there is distant spread, and how close the tumour is to the trachea. If a tumour in the upper or middle oesophagus has not been scheduled for bronchoscopy with biopsy, ask why. If your weight drops by more than 10% while waiting for tests, do not wait — raise it at that visit.
