Low, intermediate, high: what decides whether you get instillations

The stratification decides more than whether a drug goes into the bladder; it also decides whether removing the bladder is put on the table at this stage. And that progression table was worked out on people who never had BCG.

Bladder Cancer Guide · Non-muscle-invasive · 9 references
#Active Surveillance#Decisions#Evidence#Recurrence

"A new scoring model has replaced the old risk tables" is a piece of misinformation — both tables still carry a Strong recommendation today, they simply answer different questions. This article also puts the 40% for very high risk and the 14.9% to 22% after adequate BCG into the same frame.

Once the resection is over and your doctor says "we will need to put a drug into the bladder", there is a whole stratification sitting behind that sentence. In non-muscle-invasive disease, what decides everything that follows is not the two characters of the stage; it is the risk group — low, intermediate, high, very high. That group decides whether you get instillations at all, what goes in, for how long, and whether removing the bladder is something to be put on the table at this stage.

What Ta, T1 and CIS actually mean is in "Ta, T1 and CIS on the report"; whether the first resection was complete, and whether there was detrusor muscle in the specimen, is in "The first resection decides everything after it". This article handles one thing only: how that stratification is worked out, and who it is actually about.

Two questions, two tables, and neither has retired the other

The version you hear most often is that the European Association of Urology has issued a new scoring model and the old EORTC tables have been retired. That is wrong. The 2026 edition of the association's guideline on non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer gives two recommendations side by side and rates both of them Strong: use the 2021 EAU model to assign the risk group, which is a prediction of progression; use the 2006 EORTC tables to predict recurrence in people who are not given BCG; and for people who are given BCG, there is the 2016 EORTC model or the CUETO model instead[1].

The two tables answer two different questions: will something grow back, and will it grow down into the muscle. The 2006 table was built on 2,596 patients from seven EORTC trials, a population made up mainly of people who received intravesical chemotherapy, and the five-year probabilities it produces run from 31% to 78% for recurrence and from under 1% to 45% for progression[2].

That progression table was worked out on people who never had BCG

The 2021 EAU model used individual data from 3,401 patients, all of them with primary non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer, treated between 1990 and 2018. It included only people who had transurethral resection with or without intravesical chemotherapy; those who received adjuvant BCG were excluded. The endpoint was time to progression to muscle-invasive disease or distant metastasis. The authors list their own limitations: retrospective enrolment, no central pathology review[3]. So what it describes is the natural course of the disease when it is not treated further.

The guideline puts two numbers in the same chapter. For the very-high-risk group in table 6.3, the five-year probability of progression is 40% (95% confidence interval 29% to 54%; in plain words, run the same study many times over and the answer lands in that range), and the footnote under the table says these percentages refer to patients who were not (immediately) treated with adjuvant BCG instillations after their primary resection. In the treatment section for the same very-high-risk group, the same guideline writes that if adequate BCG is given, five-year progression rates fall between 14.9% and 22%[1]. The 40% and the 14.9% to 22% have to be looked at together; either one on its own misleads.

There is a colder piece of data as well. A Japanese cohort of 3,024 BCG-treated patients tested the 2021 model and got a C-index for progression of only 0.617 (the 2019 version scored 0.572, p=0.011); and within that cohort the survival curves of the five very-high-risk subgroups were not significantly different from one another[4]. A C-index, in plain words, is this: pick any two patients at random, and can the model put the one who runs into trouble first ahead of the other? Half is a coin toss, one is perfect. So 0.617 means better than the older version, and still a long way from accurate.

In the low-risk box, doing more does not make it better

In that same population who never had BCG, low risk carries a five-year probability of progression of 0.93% (confidence interval 0.49% to 1.7%), 3.7% at ten years, and a five-year recurrence probability of 10% to 35%. The guideline's instruction for this box is short: a single immediate instillation of chemotherapy after the resection "is considered sufficient treatment in these patients"[1].

What that one instillation does has been measured. A meta-analysis obtained the individual patient data of 11 studies, 2,278 patients with pTa–pT1 disease (1,161 resection alone against 1,117 resection plus a single instillation): the risk of recurrence fell by 35% (hazard ratio 0.65, 95% confidence interval 0.58 to 0.74), and the five-year recurrence rate went from 58.8% to 44.8%. A hazard ratio of 0.65 is about the rate at which events happen, not the probability discounted to 65%. But the other half of the same paper matters more — in people whose prior recurrence rate was more than one a year, or whose 2006 EORTC recurrence score was 5 or above, it did not work; it did not lengthen the time to progression and did not lengthen the time to death from bladder cancer; and across the whole cohort the overall risk of death went up (hazard ratio 1.26, 95% confidence interval 1.05 to 1.51, p=0.015; five-year mortality 12.0% against 11.2%). Both figures have all 2,278 patients as their denominator, and both are all-cause death rather than death from bladder cancer (only 59 of the 460 deaths were from bladder cancer); the hazard ratio covers the whole of the follow-up while the percentages are read at five years, which is why the two are not in conflict — how long that follow-up ran, I could not find. The people with a recurrence score of 5 or above are where that difference showed up, not the population the hazard ratio was computed in. The authors' own patient summary says this instillation "should not be given to patients at high risk of recurrence"[5].

It runs the other way too: low risk is not who BCG is for. The limitations-of-use section of the US TICE BCG label says so itself — "not recommended for stage TaG1 papillary tumors, unless they are judged to be at high risk of tumor recurrence"[6]. And more intravesical chemotherapy is not better than less: the guideline says the length and frequency of intravesical chemotherapy should not exceed one year, and a systematic review of 16 comparative studies concluded that most of the evidence does not support maintenance intravesical chemotherapy in non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer[1].

The very-high-risk box does not fit inside the word "early"

Now the other end of the same table. One of the things that puts a person in the very-high-risk group is high-grade T1 with carcinoma in situ, plus at least one further risk factor. Without BCG, that group has a five-year probability of progression of 40% and a ten-year probability of 53%; the denominator is the 3,401-patient model, followed out to ten years[1][3]. Reading "non-muscle-invasive" straight off as "early, nothing to worry about" is the misunderstanding this box collects.

The guideline says carcinoma in situ cannot be cured by endoscopic management; in retrospective data the complete response rate was 48% with intravesical chemotherapy and 72% to 93% with BCG, and up to half of those who achieve a complete response still recur later[1].

For very high risk the guideline says immediate radical cystectomy should be discussed with the patient — not that it should be done. The same section writes the other half as well: radical cystectomy is the safest approach from an oncological point of view, but it comes with complications and a loss of quality of life, and "represents over-treatment in some patients"[1]. How those two roads compare is not this article; it is "Remove the bladder, or keep it".

Intermediate risk is one box with both ends inside it

Intermediate risk is awkward by definition — it means everyone with no carcinoma in situ who does not land in the low, high or very-high box. This layer has a ten-year probability of progression of 7.4% and 8.5% (one figure from each of the two grading systems) and a five-year recurrence probability of 18% to 50% — again in the people who never had BCG. First-line treatment is usually a course of induction intravesical chemotherapy, with or without up to a year of maintenance; a year of full-dose BCG is an alternative[1].

The International Bladder Cancer Group's sub-stratification model, revised in 2022 and validated in 677 patients, found that people with none of its risk factors had a progression risk close to low risk, and people with three or more were close to high risk[1]. Being put in the intermediate group does not mean you are in the middle of the box.

The 2026 guideline gained a chapter, and it is about doing less

This edition added a chapter 10, titled "Pragmatic deintensification strategy in NMIBC", listing things that can be scaled back within shared decision-making: low-grade Ta does not always have to be resected down into the muscle layer, the single postoperative instillation can be used selectively, recurrent low-grade Ta can be watched actively or handled with office fulguration or chemoablation, the frequency of surveillance cystoscopy and CT urography can be lowered, and in selected frail patients no treatment at all with supportive care can be considered. The condition is written in the same paragraph: all of it rests on "a shared decision-making process in fully informed and consenting patients"[1]. Deintensification is something discussed into place, not a decision to come in less often.

The same chapter mentions an artificial-intelligence model, PROGRxN-BCa, built on 14 clinicopathological variables, including re-resection and BCG treatment, which the 2021 model does not contain; its discrimination beat the 2021 model overall and in the subgroups. The guideline's verdict on it is three words: "Pending further validation"[1]. Until that validation is done, it is not the current standard.

Why I am not giving you a table you can tick off yourself

The independent predictors in the 2021 model are tumour T stage, grade in both grading systems, whether carcinoma in situ is present, the number of tumours, tumour size and age[3]. The cut-points and the combinations I do not intend to lay out as tick boxes, for three reasons.

First, the model itself leaves several groups out — primary carcinoma in situ on its own, carcinoma in situ of the prostatic urethra, recurrent tumours, variant histology, lymphovascular invasion — and the guideline places those groups using other literature instead[1]. Second, national guidelines do not stratify the same way in the first place: a 2022 review puts it as international guidelines "slightly differ in their categorisation of low, intermediate and high-risk NMIBC"[7]; Europe uses four groups, the United States three, and the 2026 update from the American Urological Association and the Society of Urologic Oncology adjusted its risk stratification again[8]. Third, the whole stratification assumes the first resection was complete, that there was muscle in the specimen, and that a second resection was done where it was needed — and that box is not in your hands, it is in the pathology report and the resection record.

Whose percentages are the ones on that table

This article comes down to one sentence: the stratification decides the treatment, and the percentages on the table depend on what that group of people received. The same 40% for very high risk is two different numbers in people who never had BCG and in people who had adequate BCG, and which of the two you are being shown is worth settling there and then — ask that question and the gap between 40% and 14.9% to 22% comes out by itself.

Two other things fit into the same appointment. To know which layer you are in, what you want to hear are the concrete words on the report: T stage, grade, carcinoma in situ or not, how many, how big. What is going to be instilled — chemotherapy or BCG — and how long it is meant to run is the second question at the same table. If the answer is BCG, that is a completely different thing; see "BCG is not chemotherapy, and not a vaccination". The money half of it you can start on: bladder instillation is a listed item in Taiwan's National Health Insurance fee schedule (50011C, 260 points)[9], but how the drug that goes in is paid for is another matter, and that one goes to the hospital's medical affairs office or your case manager.

One last line for anyone out there looking for that table on the internet: take the report to clinic and get the person opposite you to point at the words on it and say which ones they are reading — that is worth a great deal more than scoring yourself against a table.

From the physician

If you have been put in the intermediate group, it is worth spending an extra appointment pinning it down — some people in that layer sit close to low risk and some sit close to high risk. Take the pathology report with you and get the person opposite you to point at the words on it and tell you which ones they are reading, rather than scoring yourself against a table you found online.
