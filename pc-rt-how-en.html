HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

How many visits does radiotherapy actually take

37, 20 or 5 fractions, spacers, brachytherapy, hormones — what each option costs in visits and in side effects.

Prostate Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 20 references
#Prostate#Short Course#SBRT / SRS#Decisions#NHI Coverage

Most men want three answers before anything else: how many visits, how long each takes, and whether they will be admitted. Along the way, several options you will read about online turn out to have a price nobody mentions.

When a man asks how radiotherapy is done, he usually wants three practical things: how many visits, how long each takes, whether he has to be admitted. This piece follows that order, and lays out on the way several options you find online without being told what they cost.

Counting the visits first: what separates 37, 20 and 5

External beam radiotherapy means coming in once every working day, a quarter of an hour on the treatment couch, and home again. What differs is the total number of visits. Conventional fractionation is 74 Gy in 37 fractions, about 7.4 weeks; moderate hypofractionation 60 Gy in 20 fractions, about 4 weeks; compressed further, ultra-hypofractionation is 42.7 Gy in 7 fractions three days a week over about 2.5 weeks, or 36.25 Gy in 5 fractions over 1 to 2 weeks[1][2][3].

Going from 37 visits to 20 is not just convenience; it is a conclusion the UK CHHiP trial bought with 3,216 men at 71 hospitals. Five-year freedom from biochemical or clinical failure was 88.3% with 74 Gy, 90.6% with 60 Gy and 85.9% with 57 Gy: 60 Gy in 20 fractions met non-inferiority (hazard ratio 0.84, meaning failures accrued slightly more slowly), and 57 Gy in 19 fractions did not. Non-inferiority means "not worse by an unacceptable margin", not "better" — a distinction used repeatedly here. Five-year cumulative moderate or worse bowel and bladder events did not differ significantly across the three groups[1]. So "fewer fractions is better" is not a line that extends indefinitely — in the same trial, compressing to 19 missed.

And five fractions? Does that save you the side effects too

PACE-B randomised 874 men to SBRT in five fractions against a conventional 39 or 20 fractions, median follow-up 74 months: five-year freedom from failure 95.8% against 94.6%, meeting non-inferiority. The other half of the same paper: five-year cumulative moderate or worse (RTOG grade 2 or above) late urinary side effects 26.9% with SBRT against 18.3% with the control course, P<0.001; bowel events 10.7% against 10.2%, no difference[3].

The two-year report reads far more gently: at 24 months, moderate or worse urinary side effects were 2% against 3% and bowel 3% against 2%, neither significant[4]. Both are true, because they measure different things — what is still happening at one moment (prevalence) and what has happened at any point in five years (cumulative incidence) differ several-fold by construction. Seeing "only 3%", ask which it is. One limit: neither PACE-B nor Sweden's HYPO-RT-PC allowed concurrent hormone therapy[2][3], so neither answers what happens when a high-risk man has five fractions alongside hormones. SBRT combined with pelvic irradiation is covered in the research-update piece "Five fractions, and the pelvis in the field too?", which I will not repeat.

What brachytherapy buys, and what it charges

Brachytherapy places the radiation source inside the prostate, which needs anaesthesia and a surgical implant. The Canadian ASCENDE-RT trial randomised 398 men, both arms given 12 months of hormone therapy and 46 Gy of pelvic radiotherapy, then either an external beam boost to 78 Gy or a low-dose-rate brachytherapy boost: biochemical progression-free survival at five, seven and nine years was 89%/86%/83% with brachytherapy against 84%/75%/62% with the external beam boost — a clear gap, but no difference in overall survival[5].

The price is in a second paper from the same trial: five-year cumulative grade 3 urinary events 18.4% in the brachytherapy arm against 5.2% in the external beam arm (P<0.001), grade 3 bowel events 8.1% against 3.2%[6]. Brachytherapy alone in low-risk disease comes with a bill too: in the CEASAR cohort it came out worse than active surveillance in the first year on urinary irritation (−7.0), sexual function (−10.1) and bowel score (−5.0)[7].

What sort of peace of mind a rectal spacer buys

A spacer is an absorbable hydrogel injected between prostate and rectum to push the rectum aside. The key randomised trial enrolled 222 men: placement succeeded in 99%, the gap went from 1.6 mm to 12.6 mm, and the rectal volume receiving 70 Gy fell from 12.4% to 3.3%; between 3 and 15 months after treatment, late rectal toxicity of any grade occurred in 2.0% of the spacer group against 7.0% of controls[8]. The three-year final results switch rulers — grade 2 or worse (moderate or worse) rectal toxicity was 0% with the spacer and 5.7% without[9]. Those two percentages are not the same measure and cannot be subtracted.

A 2026 systematic review of 35 studies and 4,664 men puts the ceiling where it belongs: the spacer does substantially cut rectal dose and reduce bowel side effects of any grade, but it did not reduce moderate or worse (grade 2 or above) acute or late bowel side effects, and it did not help urinary side effects; the authors conclude its effect on severe toxicity is limited and needs further study[10]. The other side: between January 2015 and March 2019 the US Food and Drug Administration's adverse event database received 22 reports covering 25 patients — pulmonary embolism, severe allergic reaction, prostatic abscess and sepsis, rectal wall erosion, rectourethral fistula[11]. Passive reporting has no denominator, so no probability can be computed from it — which is not the same as pretending it is not there.

Whether to irradiate the pelvic nodes as well is still unsettled

India's POP-RT trial enrolled 224 men with estimated nodal risk of 20% or more, whole-pelvic against prostate-only radiotherapy: five-year biochemical failure-free survival 95.0% against 81.2% (hazard ratio 0.23), a large gap — but five-year overall survival 92.5% against 90.8%, no difference[12]. Put four randomised trials and 4,465 men together and it reverses: no benefit for overall survival (hazard ratio 1.05), biochemical recurrence or distant metastasis, and almost all the heterogeneity comes from POP-RT alone — remove it and it disappears[13]. A larger field also means more bowel and marrow irradiated. For now this is a case-by-case judgement, not an upgrade option.

How long hormone therapy runs depends on your risk group

RTOG 94-08 enrolled 1,979 men, radiotherapy plus 4 months of hormone therapy against radiotherapy alone: ten-year overall survival 62% against 57%, ten-year disease-specific mortality 8% down to 4%; a post hoc analysis put the benefit mainly in intermediate risk, none visible in low risk[14]. Locally advanced disease is different: EORTC 22961 randomised 970 men, after radiotherapy plus 6 months of hormone therapy, to stop or continue a further 2.5 years, and non-inferiority of the short course was rejected (five-year mortality 19.0% against 15.2%)[15]. Nor is stretching it indefinitely the answer: 630 high-risk men randomised to 36 or 18 months gave five-year overall survival 91% against 86%, p=0.07 — 36 months did not beat 18, and the 18-month group scored better on six quality-of-life scales[16].

MARCAP, pooling individual patient data from 12 randomised trials and 10,853 men, settles one thing: adding hormone therapy improves metastasis-free survival (hazard ratio 0.83), and extending the part after radiotherapy from 4–6 months to 18–36 months also helps (0.84), while extending the part before radiotherapy from 3–4 months to 6–9 months does not (0.95)[17]. The EAU guideline's durations differ widely by risk group: short for intermediate risk, considerably longer for high risk[18]. How many months exactly is for your treating team to set from stage, comorbidity and response — not a number an article can prescribe; ask instead "for my group, how long do you recommend, and on which trial?" The price of hormone therapy is set out in "What hormone therapy costs you", and that is not a scheduling detail. As for PROTEUS, the trial of adding one of the newer hormonal drugs around surgery, the research-update piece "Is one more tablet around surgery worth it?" has covered it already.

How National Health Insurance counts all this in Taiwan

Prostate SBRT has had a National Health Insurance (健保) item since 1 April 2026 (115 年 4 月 1 日) — 37047B, stereotactic body radiotherapy — but the indication is tight: low and intermediate recurrence risk only, no regional nodal or distant metastasis (T+N0M0), ECOG 2 or better, the whole course within two weeks and limited to six fractions, bundled payment, prior review required; an incomplete course is deducted proportionately[19]. High-risk patients are not within this indication — which matters, because many men arrive in clinic having first read online that "five fractions is covered".

The proton therapy items (N21301 to N21308) all pay 0 points and are flagged as HTA items, which in practice means self-paid[20]. Rectal spacers and fiducial markers are special materials and do not appear in the schedule of covered services I could search, so I will not tell you they are covered or not — ask your case manager (個管師) or the hospital billing office (醫務課) whether each is a self-paid material or a covered item, what it costs, and ask for the itemised quotation in writing.

Four things to settle before the schedule is fixed

For my risk group, how many fractions, and how many weeks of visits is that? If hormone therapy is added, how many months, and how long does it continue after radiotherapy ends? Do I need a spacer or fiducial markers, and is each self-paid or covered? And during the course, which symptoms mean coming in the same day, and which can wait?

On that last one, several do not wait for the next clinic: a large volume of fresh rectal bleeding or passing clots, or bleeding with dizziness, palpitations or a cold sweat; blood in the urine with inability to pass urine and lower abdominal distension and pain; fever with burning on urination or with chills — for any of these, contact the team or attend the same day. And one more that has nothing to do with the treatment machine but matters to anyone on hormone therapy: new back pain together with leg weakness, numbness, or a change in bladder or bowel function means going in the same day. Do not wait. That is spinal cord compression, and it is measured in hours. Telling the other side effects apart is covered in "That 13.7% is being read wrong", and spinal cord compression has its own section in "Back pain plus a weak leg: do not wait". Asking beforehand beats searching online afterwards.

From the physician

Fraction number, hormone duration and whether you need a spacer all follow from your own risk group and comorbidity, so settle them with your team rather than from a schedule. Confirm before the course starts which symptoms mean coming in the same day — new back pain with leg weakness is one of them.
