HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

Immunotherapy is not an option for everyone

About one in twenty patients with metastatic colorectal cancer is dMMR; in 2026 ATOMIC brought stage III into the discussion too, and the cost arrives together with the benefit.

Colon Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 16 references
#Immunotherapy#Biomarkers#Decisions#Side Effects#NHI Coverage

The NHI provision confines pembrolizumab to first-line use in "unresectable or metastatic" disease, and for the early-stage adjuvant setting I could find no provision at all. This article sets out the precondition the option rests on, the endocrine damage that may last for life, and the full qualifiers on the sentence about MSS disease.

"Doctor, can I have immunotherapy?" The person asking is usually still holding a news article. The answer is not in the drug. It is in one box on your pathology report — and for most colon cancer patients, that box closes the option.

Start with the denominator: this is roughly a one-in-twenty option

Among the 3,063 patients with metastatic colorectal cancer pooled from the four phase III trials CAIRO, CAIRO2, COIN and FOCUS, only 153 (5.0%) were mismatch repair deficient (dMMR)[1] — about one person in twenty. The proportion is higher in colon cancer that has not metastasised; the NICHE-2 paper opens by putting it at 10% to 15%[2], because dMMR tumours are less inclined to metastasise in the first place. Plenty of people take that 10% to 15% and apply it to their own, already metastatic, situation. Those are two different denominators.

The test itself is written up in "Your gene report decides more than your treatment"; here I am only dealing with what it means for treatment choice. One more thing belongs here: metastatic dMMR patients actually have a worse prognosis (progression-free survival hazard ratio 1.33, overall survival hazard ratio 1.35), which the authors put down mainly to BRAF mutation[1]. dMMR is not good news or bad news. It is the fork that decides which drug.

If you are in that group: KEYNOTE-177's numbers, and what arrives with them

KEYNOTE-177 randomised 307 previously untreated patients with metastatic MSI-H/dMMR colorectal cancer to pembrolizumab or chemotherapy[3]. After a median follow-up of 73.3 months, median overall survival was 77.5 months against 36.7 months, hazard ratio 0.73 (a hazard ratio is what you get by dividing one group's rate of running into trouble by the other group's), 95% confidence interval 0.53 to 0.99 (a confidence interval marks out where the estimate could reasonably have landed; this one does not cross 1, so the difference stands up, but an upper bound of 0.99 is already very close to 1). Five-year overall survival was 54.8% against 44.2%; grade 3 to 5 adverse events 22% against 67%[4]. Inside that 22% are several that, once they happen, may stay with you for the rest of your life — adrenal insufficiency caused by hypophysitis, type 1 diabetes, thyroid failure. The benefit and the cost come out of the same prescription; they cannot be read apart, and they cannot be waved past with "fewer side effects than chemotherapy": the kinds of adverse event are not the same, so it is not one measuring stick.

Two limitations travel with those numbers. Of the 154 people in the chemotherapy arm, 57 (37.0%) crossed over to pembrolizumab as the protocol allowed, and another 39 (25.3%) received a PD-(L)1 inhibitor outside the trial, an effective crossover rate of 62%[4] — so this is not a comparison of "immunotherapy" against "no immunotherapy at all". And five-year overall survival of 54.8% is not a guarantee.

In Taiwan there is a provision I can quote. Section 9.69 of the NHI drug reimbursement regulations restricts pembrolizumab to first-line treatment of adult patients with "unresectable or metastatic" MSI-H/dMMR colorectal cancer, effective from 1 June 2025; the same section also sets a reimbursement period of "two years from the date of the first prescription", with each application limited to 12 weeks[5]. Whether it can be continued once those two years are up, I could not find an official explanation; that one is to confirm with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.

Where the words "for the rest of your life" come from

In a retrospective study of 83 people, 62 had confirmed immune-related hypophysitis, and after a median follow-up of 1.75 years the recovery rates of the endocrine axes were: thyroid axis 24%, gonadal axis 58%, adrenal axis 0%. Whether high-dose corticosteroids were given, and whether immunotherapy was stopped, had no bearing on recovery[6]. An adrenal axis at 0% means those people will be on hormone replacement from then on.

For type 1 diabetes caused by immunotherapy, a meta-analysis covering 65,925 patients works out an incidence of 0.58% (95% confidence interval 0.35% to 0.92%), with about 37% presenting in diabetic ketoacidosis[7]. The incidence is not high, but the day it starts is an emergency, and insulin afterwards is for life. On the thyroid, a meta-analysis of 38 randomised trials and 7,551 people reports odds ratios (OR) — an OR is a ratio, not "so many times the probability", and it cannot be converted into a percentage. What it can give is direction: with two immunotherapy drugs used together, hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism turn up more readily than with ipilimumab alone, and so does hypophysitis[8]. For primary adrenal insufficiency and insulin-deficient diabetes the event numbers were too few for the authors to draw inferences, and I am not going to invent a percentage either.

Fatal immune adverse events are uncommon: in a meta-analysis of 112 trials and 19,217 people, toxicity-related mortality was 0.36% with anti-PD-1 and 1.23% with two classes of immunotherapy drug combined, with a median of 32 days from the first symptom to death[9] — it moves fast, which is what makes speaking up a day earlier worth something.

What changed in 2026: immunotherapy is no longer only for people who have metastasised

ATOMIC randomised 712 patients with resected stage III dMMR colon cancer to atezolizumab plus mFOLFOX6 (followed by atezolizumab alone, twelve months in total) or mFOLFOX6 alone. At a median follow-up of 40.9 months, three-year disease-free survival — the proportion who, since surgery, have not had a recurrence, have not grown a new cancer, and are still alive — was 86.3% against 76.2%, hazard ratio 0.50, meaning recurrence or death happened at half the rate of the control arm, 95% confidence interval 0.35 to 0.73; in the same trial, grade 3 or 4 adverse events were 84.1% against 71.9%[10]. The extra 10.1 percentage points of disease-free survival is bought with an extra 12.2 percentage points of serious adverse events, and those two numbers have to be read together.

Two 2026 guidelines are at different points. The patient version of NCCN's Colon Cancer version 2.2026 (7 April 2026) already says that stage III dMMR or MSI-H cancer may have atezolizumab added to chemotherapy[11]; in the Express Update to its localised colon cancer guideline published by ESMO on 27 January 2026 (its subject is exercise, but the stage II and III treatment algorithms are reprinted in it), the stage III algorithm still lists only FOLFOX/CAPOX-related options[12].

On the Taiwan side, I went through the NHI Administration's Section 9 revision comparison tables one by one from June 2024 to August 2026 and did not see a provision covering immune checkpoint inhibitors in early-stage or adjuvant colon cancer. Whether that is an express exclusion or whether it is regulated somewhere else, I cannot tell — that one is to confirm with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office. I am not going to tell you it is covered or that it isn't.

Neoadjuvant immunotherapy: why NICHE-2 cannot yet be taken as evidence of cure

NICHE-2 is a phase II study: 115 patients with non-metastatic, more locally advanced dMMR colon cancer received nivolumab plus ipilimumab before surgery. 113 (98%) had their operation as planned; of the 111 evaluable for efficacy, 109 (98%) had a pathological response, and in 75 of them (68%) no viable tumour could be found in the specimen; grade 3 or 4 immune-related adverse events occurred in 5 (4%); and up to a median follow-up of 26 months nobody had recurred — but that is where a single-arm study of 115 people with no control group has got to so far, and its co-primary endpoint of three-year disease-free survival has to this day not been formally published, so that sentence cannot be read as evidence of cure[2].

One more thing gets misread here: every patient had an operation — "whether surgery can go ahead as planned" is one of this trial's own primary endpoints. It is not a scheme for avoiding surgery.

If you are not dMMR: the qualifiers on this sentence have to be written out in full

The accurate statement is this: in metastatic microsatellite stable (MSS) colorectal cancer, a single-agent immune checkpoint inhibitor has, at present, no efficacy evidence that stands up. IMblaze370 is a randomised phase III trial: 363 metastatic patients who had failed at least two lines of chemotherapy (enrolment of microsatellite instability-high patients capped at 5%), single-agent atezolizumab against regorafenib, overall survival hazard ratio 1.19 (95% confidence interval 0.83 to 1.71), primary endpoint not met; the authors' conclusion was how difficult it is to extend the benefit of immunotherapy to tumours with a low degree of immune inflammation[13]. In an earlier study, metastatic colorectal cancer patients with proficient mismatch repair given single-agent pembrolizumab had an immune-related objective response rate of 0% (0 of 18), against 40% in the dMMR patients (4 of 10)[14].

Both qualifiers have to be written every time, because dropping them makes the sentence wrong: in the pMMR cohort of the NICHE study in 2025, 31 early-stage, non-metastatic pMMR colon cancer patients received nivolumab plus ipilimumab before surgery, with a response rate of 26%[15]. That is early-stage, before surgery, two drugs together, single-arm — not metastatic, not single-agent, and not yet a clinical option either. So "immunotherapy does nothing at all in MSS" is imprecise, and "in metastatic MSS disease a single-agent checkpoint inhibitor has no efficacy evidence that stands up" is accurate. That difference decides whether you should put off a treatment already shown to work in order to chase it.

These situations mean contacting your treatment team the same day

Immune-related adverse events do not keep to chemotherapy's rhythm and can appear after the course has finished. Any of the following, contact your treatment team the same day rather than waiting for your next appointment: new and persistent diarrhoea or blood in the stool; getting more breathless, a dry cough; unusual tiredness together with dizziness or a low blood pressure; unexplained thirst, drinking constantly, passing urine frequently — that could be the type 1 diabetes described above, which presents in ketoacidosis[7]. There is a dedicated guideline for managing this kind of event[16]; the point is to let the team know early. Immunotherapy aside, if you are on chemotherapy drugs at the same time, the same-day warnings for that side are a separate list, in "The chemotherapy you take at home".

Before you can discuss immunotherapy, check which report you are holding

For that appointment, bring these:

  • The result in the MMR/MSI box of the pathology report. Without that box, the discussion has no starting point.

  • Where you are now: adjuvant treatment after resection, or already metastatic. Every set of numbers above is tied to one of those two situations and does not transfer to the other.

  • Your thyroid, adrenal and blood glucose history and medication, and whether autoimmune disease runs in the family.

  • Two questions to get out of your mouth: with my report, is immunotherapy an option? If it is, what are the conditions under the NHI for my situation, and who should I ask?

A last line for readers who are not dMMR: none of the numbers in this article are your numbers, because every one of them is tied to the dMMR/MSI-H precondition. What your box calls for is a conversation to have in clinic with your own report, not with this article; and spending your time trying to get hold of a drug that has no efficacy evidence supporting its use for you carries a real cost.

From the physician

Check the MMR/MSI box first and discuss everything else after it. During treatment, if persistent diarrhoea, increasing breathlessness, unusual tiredness with dizziness, or unexplained thirst and frequent urination appear, contact your treatment team the same day; the reimbursement conditions in the adjuvant setting are to confirm with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.
