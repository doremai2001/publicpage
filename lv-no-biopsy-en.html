Why liver cancer is often diagnosed without a biopsy

When typical imaging in a high-risk group calls it liver cancer, the chance it is wrong is roughly 4% to 6% — but the same standard's sensitivity is only 61.3%. You need to know both sides.

Liver Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 6 references
#Imaging#Staging#Decisions

The case for skipping the biopsy stands on the 'it almost never wrongly convicts' side, not on 'it sees everything'. Biopsy has limits of its own: sensitivity for small tumours can drop to about 60%, a negative result cannot exclude the disease, and complications run at about 3%.

"You haven't even done a biopsy — how can you tell me it's liver cancer?" I have heard that sentence in clinic many times, half disbelief, half indignation. Most cancers need a piece of tissue for the diagnosis; liver cancer is one of the few exceptions — but the exception comes with strict conditions, and with honest limits of its own. This article lays out both sides: when imaging is allowed to convict on its own, and when it is not.

My position first: stereotactic body radiotherapy and proton therapy for liver cancer are treatments I deliver myself — and much of it is self-paid. The other side of every comparison in this topic is my colleagues' surgery and ablation. That is why every comparison comes with its primary source, so you can take it to the surgical and hepatology clinics for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

Every other cancer needs a biopsy. Why does the liver get an exception?

The condition is written in the first line of the guidelines: you have to belong to a high-risk group. The American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD), in its 2023 liver cancer guidance, makes it a strong recommendation at the highest level of evidence: in patients with cirrhosis, or with chronic hepatitis B infection, the diagnosis of liver cancer can rest either on non-invasive imaging criteria or on pathology; the imaging tools are restricted to dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or multiphase computed tomography (CT)[1]. What the radiologist reads is a combination of features: the size of the nodule, enhancement in the arterial phase, washout in the delayed phase, whether there is a capsule — the scoring system is called LI-RADS, and AASLD endorses it explicitly[1]. Taiwan's own criteria point the same way: the 2023 consensus of the Taiwan Liver Cancer Association and the Gastroenterological Society of Taiwan states, with 100% agreement, that in a patient with cirrhosis or chronic hepatitis B or C, a nodule larger than one centimetre showing the typical vascular pattern on four-phase contrast imaging can be diagnosed without a biopsy[2]. The European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) takes the imaging-diagnosis route as well, but I could not obtain a publicly citable version of the full text of its new 2025 guideline, so I will not restate its specific conditions here[3].

When imaging says "yes" — how low is the chance it is wrong?

The case for skipping the biopsy stands on the "it almost never wrongly convicts" side. A systematic review of seventeen studies and 2,760 patients from high-risk surveillance populations: of nodules given the highest imaging category, LR-5, 94% did turn out to be liver cancer, and 97% were malignant tumours[4]. A more recent individual participant data meta-analysis (forty-six studies, more than 6,700 people) put the figure at 95.8%[5]. In other words, when LR-5 calls you a liver cancer, the chance it is wrong is roughly 4% to 6%. Both of those numbers come from surveillance populations with cirrhosis or hepatitis B; they cannot be extrapolated to someone without those conditions whose liver nodule was picked up by chance on a health check — that is an entirely different group of people, and I come back to them below.

When imaging says "no", that does not mean there is nothing there

This is the other side I am obliged to write honestly. The very same LR-5 standard has a sensitivity for liver cancer of only 61.3% — that comes from an individual participant data meta-analysis of 3,840 patients and more than 4,700 lesions[6]. In plain language: of the tumours that really are liver cancer, only about 60% land in the LR-5 box at first reading; the rest get placed in lower categories to begin with. And a lower category is not an all-clear: about three-quarters of LR-4 nodules, and about 38% of LR-3 nodules, still turn out to be liver cancer in the end[4]. So imaging actually gives three kinds of answer: yes, no, and look again. AASLD is very concrete about the middle ground: for LR-3, repeat imaging in three to six months; for LR-4, biopsy or repeat imaging within three months, with multidisciplinary team discussion; and if an immediate diagnosis would change the treatment decision, biopsy takes priority over waiting another round of imaging[1]. The selling point of imaging diagnosis is "when it says yes, it almost always is" — it has never been "it sees everything".

Wouldn't a biopsy be safer anyway?

Biopsy has limits of its own, and this part often gets skipped. The numbers AASLD cites: the sensitivity of a liver tumour biopsy mostly falls between 70% and 93%, and for tumours under two centimetres it can be as low as about 60%; a negative biopsy cannot exclude liver cancer, and the guidance recommends continued follow-up and a second biopsy where needed; complications such as needle-track bleeding or tumour seeding run at about 3%, though coaxial technique has brought that down substantially[1]. The Taiwan consensus writes the same thing: a nodule with a negative biopsy should be followed for two years[2]. Nor can a blood test replace any of this — AASLD explicitly recommends against using alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) alone, or liquid biopsy, to confirm a diagnosis of liver cancer, on the grounds that the accuracy is not good enough[1]. So "I'd only feel safe with a biopsy" does not hold for this disease: when the conditions are all met and the imaging is typical, a biopsy adds very little certainty and adds one invasive risk.

Turned round, though, these situations still need tissue

The imaging criteria have boundaries. First: no cirrhosis and not a hepatitis B carrier — AASLD says it plainly: the LI-RADS criteria do not apply to this group, imaging is not accurate enough there, and the diagnosis should be made on pathology; that too is a strong recommendation at the highest level[1]. Second: lesions that look atypical on imaging and get classed LR-M — only about one-third of these are liver cancer, yet more than nine in ten are some form of malignancy — possibly cholangiocarcinoma or a mixed tumour (which sit outside this topic) — so the guidance recommends a biopsy to establish what it actually is[4][1]. Third: LR-TIV, where there is tumour inside a vein, and enrolment in a clinical trial — trials usually need tissue and molecular information[1]. The Taiwan consensus says the same thing in its own words: if the vascular pattern is atypical, or the patient has neither cirrhosis nor chronic hepatitis B or C, do the biopsy[2]. "No biopsy needed" is a conclusion that holds under specific conditions — it is not a general rule for liver cancer.

Three questions to say out loud at your next appointment

First: "Was my diagnosis made on imaging criteria or on pathology? Do I have cirrhosis or chronic hepatitis B — do I belong to the group the criteria apply to?" That question decides whether this whole set of rules holds for you at all. Second: "Which category is my nodule given in the report? Is the next step a diagnosis, follow-up, or a biopsy?" If the answer is LR-3 or LR-4, ask on about the follow-up interval and which kind of imaging — three to six months is not being left on a shelf, it is the management the criteria prescribe[1]. Third: "If treatment comes next, has every specialty looked at the same set of images?" The other disease you will be facing at the same time once the diagnosis is in — the cirrhosis itself — is in "You have two diseases: the cancer and the cirrhosis", and that article is the foundation of this whole topic.

From the physician

If your report stops at LR-3 or LR-4 and you are booked for another scan in three to six months, you are not being left on a shelf — that is the management the criteria prescribe. At your next visit, start by asking: 'Do I belong to the group the imaging criteria apply to?'
