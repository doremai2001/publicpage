MUSCLE-INVASIVE: TWO ROADS

The bladder you kept, and what it needs

How long the cystoscopies run, what a recurrence looks like, the rate of salvage cystectomy and what it costs, and how much function a preserved bladder actually has.

Bladder Cancer Guide · Muscle-invasive: two roads · 13 references
#Follow-up#Late Effects#Urinary Function#Recurrence

One set of figures is worth having now: most recurrences after bladder preservation are non-muscle-invasive, 36% cumulative at ten years, more than twice the muscle-invasive rate — and most of them can still be salvaged, provided the surveillance is close enough.

The bladder is still there, and what follows is something with no end date. The most dangerous misreading I hear in clinic after trimodality therapy is "a few more years of scopes and then I can stop". Not one of the three guidelines gives a stopping point. This article covers what happens to a preserved bladder, how long the surveillance runs, how much function is actually left, and the figure nobody wants to hear but which has to be written down.

My position first: the radiotherapy inside trimodality therapy is the part I deliver myself, and this topic argues that Taiwan under-uses bladder preservation — an argument that suits me, and you have every reason to discount it accordingly. So every comparison here comes with its primary source, and I have written down the things bladder preservation cannot do as well. Take it to the urology clinic for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

Three guidelines, three cystoscopy schedules, and not one of them finishes

The EAU 2026 follow-up section says that most recurrences occur between two and five years after treatment; expert consensus is cystoscopy every three months for the first two years, every six months to year five, and annually thereafter, with urine cytology and cross-sectional imaging of chest, abdomen and pelvis (including the upper tract) every six to twelve months for the first three to five years and as clinically indicated after that[1]. AUA/ASCO/SUO 2024 uses different intervals: every three months in the first year, every four to six months in the second, every six to twelve months thereafter; for imaging it recommends every six months for the first two years — and in the same passage adds a sentence of its own, that no published data show this imaging frequency improves survival[2]. The NICE NG2 version is a rigid cystoscopy three months after the end of radiotherapy, then every three months for the first two years, every six months for the two years after that, annually thereafter, and upper tract imaging once a year for five years[3].

The intervals differ, but all three end on "annually after that" — none of them writes a stopping point. I am not going to supply one on their behalf.

A preserved bladder keeps growing new things

The most direct measure in this cell is the pooled data from six prospective RTOG trials: 468 patients with muscle-invasive disease, clinical stage T2 in 61%, T3 in 35% and T4a in 4%, median follow-up 4.3 years (7.8 years among survivors). Non-muscle-invasive local failure was 31% at five years and 36% at ten; muscle-invasive local failure 13% at five years and 14% at ten; distant metastasis 31% at five years and 35% at ten[4]. Two things go together there: recurrence is mostly non-muscle-invasive, more than twice the muscle-invasive rate; and very little is added between five and ten years (31% to 36%, 13% to 14%), so late recurrence is uncommon.

The meta-analysis restricted to matched cohort studies (87 studies, 28,218 patients) gives a pooled estimate for intravesical recurrence of 23.1% (95% confidence interval 19.0–27.7; a confidence interval means that if you ran the same study many times over, the answer would land in that range), with a complete response rate in the same analysis of 74.4%[5]. The SPARE authors observed the same shape and wrote this sentence: an irradiated bladder "remains at high risk of developing second primary disease", but most non-muscle-invasive recurrences can still be salvaged with local treatment, so bladder preservation rates hold up[6].

A recurrence is handled down one of two routes

Non-muscle-invasive recurrence: AUA's clause 29 says the options are local treatment (resection plus intravesical instillation) or radical cystectomy, and the same clause adds that anyone who has had one non-muscle-invasive recurrence is at higher risk of a further one, non-muscle-invasive or muscle-invasive[2]. Residual or recurrent muscle-invasive disease: AUA's clause 28 is a strong recommendation — patients fit enough should have a radical cystectomy with bilateral pelvic lymphadenectomy[2]; EAU says the same thing, that the indication for salvage cystectomy is non-metastatic muscle-invasive recurrence that has not responded to bladder-preserving treatment[1]. This is why the follow-up cannot be allowed to slacken: EAU states outright that close cystoscopic surveillance and early salvage cystectomy are key to long-term bladder preservation and to disease-specific survival[1].

Salvage cystectomy: about one person in five

Across 73 studies and 9,110 patients, looking only at series with more than five years of follow-up, the pooled salvage cystectomy rate is 19.2%; split by reason, it is 15.5% for failure to respond to the preserving treatment and 28.7% for local recurrence arising after it[7]. The same systematic review notes that only three studies reported complications in full: the overall complication rate falls between 67% and 72%, 30-day mortality between 0% and 8.8%, and this operation is "usually accompanied by an incontinent urinary diversion" — after a salvage operation, an orthotopic neobladder is usually not achievable[7].

Compared with having surgery first, two datasets point in different directions and both belong here. A single-centre comparison of 265 patients with a median follow-up of 65.5 months: no difference in intraoperative or early complications within 90 days, but more late complications beyond 90 days (any late complication hazard ratio 2.3, p=0.02; major late complication hazard ratio 2.1), while disease-specific and overall survival showed no difference[8]. A multinational propensity-score matched analysis across 13 high-volume centres (1:1 matched, 118 patients in total after matching, 59 in the salvage arm and 59 in the primary-cystectomy arm, median age 73): of the 59 in the salvage arm, 7 patients (11%) had a severe perioperative complication of grade 4 or 5, and in those same 59 the 30-day and 90-day survival were 93% and 91%; blood loss was 297 mL higher and the odds ratio for admission to intensive care was 2.8 (1.2–6.7) — an odds ratio is the odds of having something against not having it in each group, not a multiple of probability, and all it says here is that markedly more of this group went to intensive care; overall survival was worse than after primary cystectomy (hazard ratio 1.9, 1.2–4.1), and the authors' own explanation is that this may reflect tumour biology[9]. AUA adds two practical sentences: pelvic surgery after full-dose radiotherapy is more difficult and complications may be higher, and radiation limits both the choice of urinary diversion and the possibility of nerve-sparing surgery[2].

So salvage cystectomy is not an insurance policy. It is a road that exists, and it is narrower than the first one.

How much function the preserved bladder actually has

The most direct data come from Massachusetts General Hospital: of 221 patients with cT2 to T4a disease treated with trimodality therapy between 1986 and 2000, 71 were alive in 2001 with their native bladder and free of disease, and 69% of them took part in this study, a median of 6.3 years after treatment (range 1.6 to 14.9 years). In the urodynamic group of 32: 24 had normal bladder function, 7 had reduced bladder compliance, and 2 women had bladder hypersensitivity with involuntary detrusor contractions and incontinence — those three add to 33, one more than 32, and the source does not say whether the categories are mutually exclusive or where the overlap lies. On the questionnaires: urinary flow symptoms in 6%, urgency in 15%, control problems in 19%, with 11% of the women using pads; bowel symptoms in 22%, of whom 14% found them distressing; and most men retained sexual function[10]. That denominator needs labelling clearly — these are the people who were alive, disease-free and still had their bladder, which is by construction the group whose course went most smoothly, and they cannot stand for everyone who has had trimodality therapy.

The patient-reported quality of life data from BC2001 were collected inside a randomised trial: scores fell significantly at the end of treatment (bladder cancer subscale −5.06, 99% confidence interval −6.12 to −4.00), returned to baseline by six months and stayed there, with no difference at any time point between adding concurrent chemotherapy and not; the authors write that two-thirds of patients had stable or improved quality of life on long-term follow-up[11]. The other half of that has to be written too: when EAU quotes the same dataset, what it writes is that about 33% of patients still score below their pre-treatment level on bladder symptoms at five years[1]. And the response rate to that questionnaire fell from over ninety per cent at baseline to about half at 12 months[11] — who the missing half were, nobody knows.

Late toxicity: the rate is low, but you need to know where to look

In a pooled analysis of four RTOG trials, 157 patients who completed full concurrent chemoradiotherapy, survived at least two years and still had an intact bladder were followed for a median of 5.4 years: late grade 3 or higher pelvic toxicity was 7%, of which genitourinary 5.7% and gastrointestinal 1.9%; there was no late grade 4 toxicity and no treatment-related death; and of the 9 people who had grade 3 or higher urinary toxicity, only 1 had a problem that did not resolve. Age, sex, stage, presence of carcinoma in situ and completeness of resection all failed to predict who it would happen to[12]. A low rate does not mean you need not recognise them: visible blood in the urine, new or worsening difficulty passing urine, or a fever with pain on passing urine — any one of those is a same-day matter, so contact your treating team that day rather than waiting for the next scheduled cystoscopy. The late effects of radiotherapy and how to look after yourself are already written up on this site: "What you buy is side effects, not survival", "Late bleeding: treatments and their evidence", "Bowel reactions: when they come, how to get through" and "Those weeks of bladder and passing urine".

The cystoscopies themselves are this disease's long-term burden

I am not going to write this off as unimportant. Irradiated mucosa changes, and EAU states plainly that cystoscopy and cytology are harder to interpret after treatment because of it[1] — in plain words, you will meet "let's look again" and "we'll take a piece while we're in there" more often than other people do. AUA, for its part, writes directly that no published data show that the currently recommended imaging frequency improves survival[2]. Those two sentences sit together awkwardly, and both hold at once: the discomfort of surveillance is real, and surveillance is genuinely one of the conditions on which trimodality therapy stands — AUA's own explanation is that the survival results in bladder preservation series come partly from close monitoring plus early salvage surgery, and partly from careful selection at the outset[2].

What the next twelve months look like on a calendar

Write the date treatment ended on a piece of paper and count forward: a cystoscopy every three months (AUA's first year, and the first two years for both EAU and NICE all use this interval), urine cytology in between, and imaging of chest, abdomen and pelvis including the upper tract every six to twelve months[1][2][3]. Which schedule is yours, ask your doctor to point at one in front of you and write it down — the three differ, and blending them into one is not something I will do.

One more thing to note now. The items needed for follow-up all exist in the NHI fee schedule: 28019C cystoscopy at 1,800 points, 78008C endoscopic resection if there is a recurrence, and 50011C intravesical instillation at 260 points[13]; but the notes on these items record no frequency limit, and I am not going to infer from that that "however many you have are paid for" — how the number of procedures and any prior review are worked out is a question for the medical affairs office or your case manager. Patients with non-muscle-invasive disease have a different follow-up schedule of their own, written up in "How long the cystoscopies go on"; do not apply it to yourself.

From the physician

Write down the date treatment ended, and ask your doctor to specify which follow-up schedule you are on and to put it in the notes; the three guidelines use different intervals and I am not going to blend them into one for you. If visible blood appears in the urine, passing urine becomes difficult, or you get a fever with pain on passing urine, contact the team that day rather than waiting for the next scheduled cystoscopy.
