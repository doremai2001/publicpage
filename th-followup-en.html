FOLLOW-UP AND BEYOND

Follow-up is an ultrasound and one number

There is no universal line for that number: how much was removed and whether you had radioiodine decide which line it is read against, and after a lobectomy there is no box for it at all.

Thyroid Cancer Guide · Follow-up and beyond · 8 references
#Follow-up#Imaging#Biomarkers#Recurrence

People treat Tg as a number they can compare with somebody else's. It is not. And few are ever told that this follow-up has an end: this edition is the first to write down which year it can stop.

Once the surgery is done and radioiodine (I-131) is either finished or decided against, every follow-up visit looks the same: an ultrasound of the neck, one tube of blood, one number. Most people treat this as routine paperwork. It is not — how that number is read depends entirely on how much was taken out of you and whether you had radioiodine.

This article covers how follow-up is scheduled, how the number is read, and when it can stop. A number that climbs while imaging finds nothing is in 〈The number rises and nothing shows〉; how much of the tablet you take is in 〈The tablet you take for life, and how much〉; why people still under observation, not yet operated on, are not tested for it at all is in 〈Watching instead of operating, on conditions〉.

Follow-up is really only two things

The first is ultrasound, looking at the thyroid bed and the central and lateral neck node compartments. The 2025 American Thyroid Association guideline for differentiated thyroid cancer calls it the preferred method of imaging surveillance for most of this disease — Strong recommendation, moderate certainty evidence[1].

The second is thyroglobulin (Tg) in the blood. It is a protein only thyroid cells make, normal ones and cancerous ones alike. Take the gland out and it should fall very low; if it climbs again, something somewhere is still of thyroid origin. The guideline asks that anti-thyroglobulin antibodies be quantified with every single Tg measurement — which the guideline does not grade, listing it as good practice[1].

Before you read that number, ask how much was removed

This is the most important section in the article. The guideline splits the reading of response to therapy into three columns: total thyroidectomy plus radioiodine, total thyroidectomy without radioiodine, and lobectomy[1]. The lines are different in all three, and the lobectomy column is not even the same kind of thing.

After a lobectomy, the excellent response box does not look at Tg at all. It asks whether the opposite lobe is normal or carries only low-risk nodules, or whether any nodule there has a benign biopsy, and whether imaging is free of abnormal lymph nodes. And the two boxes for indeterminate response and biochemical incomplete response simply read N/A. Those categories do not exist for you.

That is not laziness. A 2022 systematic review reported three situations separately: 4 studies and 561 patients after lobectomy, 5 studies and 751 after total thyroidectomy without radioiodine, 28 studies and 7,618 after total thyroidectomy before radioiodine. Its conclusion on the first, word for word, is "Following partial thyroidectomy, Tg measurement was not accurate for diagnosing recurrence or metastasis, or estimates were imprecise" — and it rated the quality of evidence for every one of those situations as very low[2]. A 30 ng/mL line after lobectomy was indeed proposed in 2016, from a single-centre cohort of 507 patients, none of whom had radioiodine[3], but the 2025 edition did not adopt that cutoff.

So if you had a lobectomy, remember this: the mainstay of your follow-up is the ultrasound, not the blood tube. On routine Tg measurement after lobectomy the guideline says it is not recommended routinely — Conditional recommendation, very low certainty evidence[1].

After a total thyroidectomy, radioiodine or not is another line again

With radioiodine, an excellent response means a nonstimulated Tg — the one drawn while you are taking your tablet — below 0.2 ng/mL, or a stimulated Tg below 1, with negative imaging. Without radioiodine, the same box is drawn at below 2.5 ng/mL[1]. That is more than a tenfold difference, and it is not a typo.

The reason is plain. Radioiodine clears the normal thyroid tissue surgery leaves behind. If you did not have it, that tissue is still there, still making the same protein, and your baseline is simply higher. This is the misunderstanding I unpick most often in clinic: people holding somebody else's number against their own. It also drifts downwards for years. In a cohort of 299 patients, all of whom had a total thyroidectomy with remnant ablation and no treatment of any kind afterwards, only 58% had reached their nadir by six months, and 75% by twelve[4]. A first result that is not low enough does not settle anything. How long the rest wait, and whether waiting is reasonable, is in 〈The number rises and nothing shows〉.

Four response categories, each with its own recurrence rate

The guideline asks that response to therapy be described in four categories — Strong recommendation, moderate certainty evidence. Excellent response: no clinical, biochemical or structural evidence of disease. Indeterminate response: nonspecific findings that cannot confidently be called benign or malignant, which includes stable or declining antibody levels. Biochemical incomplete response: an abnormal Tg or rising antibodies with no localisable disease. Structural incomplete response: locoregional or distant metastases visible on imaging[1].

The guideline collects a range of structural recurrence rates for each box, in adults with differentiated thyroid cancer after total thyroidectomy and radioiodine: excellent response 1–4%, and among those assigned low risk at the start, 0.2–2%; indeterminate response 5% to 15–20%; biochemical incomplete response 20–53%[1]. The corresponding figures in the column without radioiodine run lower across the board.

What makes this system worth the trouble is that it overwrites the box you were put in at the beginning. The original paper followed 588 adults with differentiated thyroid cancer, all with total thyroidectomy and remnant ablation, median follow-up 7 years, using the three-tier stratification of the day: persistent structural disease or recurrence appeared in 3% of the low-risk group, 21% of the intermediate, 68% of the high. Those figures differ from the set in 〈Why the stage depends on your age〉: that set is the guideline's pooled structural recurrence in papillary carcinoma under the three-tier era — 1.5%, 5.4%, 25%[1] — while this one also counts persistent structural disease. The same risk box gives different percentages in different studies, which is itself the reason the opening box is not a verdict. Reclassify after an excellent response in the first two years and the three figures become 2%, 2% and 14%[5]. A second cohort of 506 patients, median follow-up 10 years, also total thyroidectomy and ablation, moved the same way: 13%, 36% and 68%, falling to 1.4% among those reaching an excellent response[6].

The one in five with antibodies takes a different road

Roughly 20% of patients with differentiated thyroid cancer have detectable anti-thyroglobulin antibodies in their blood[1]. Many are told "the antibodies interfere, so discount the number a bit". That is wrong. Current immunometric assays tend to read falsely low when antibodies are present, so the problem is not a discount — the problem is that a reassuring number may be false; mass spectrometry, meanwhile, lacks sensitivity. Hence a recommendation of its own for this group, who should not be monitored on Tg alone: "Imaging is the primary modality for monitoring in this population", Conditional recommendation, low certainty evidence[1]. The same passage is blunter still: it is not clear that any Tg assay system is fully accurate when antibodies are circulating.

The antibodies themselves become the marker instead: followed serially on the same assay, stable or falling levels put you in the indeterminate box, rising levels in the biochemical incomplete box. So if you are in that one in five, what changes is that your ultrasounds come closer together, not that the blood tube comes out more often.

How the ultrasounds are spaced, and when a node is left alone

A neck ultrasound is done six to twelve months after initial therapy is completed; timing and frequency after that follow your risk of residual or recurrent disease and your response to therapy — again, which the guideline does not grade, listing it as good practice[1]. A suspicious node does not automatically get a needle: under 8 to 10 mm in shortest dimension it can be followed instead, unless it grows or threatens the recurrent laryngeal nerve, trachea, oesophagus or great vessels (Conditional recommendation, low certainty evidence).

More scanning feels safer, and the guideline says the opposite in as many words: in low-risk patients with an excellent response, ultrasound has not been found to identify clinically significant disease, and does increase the number of interventions driven by false positives[1]. As for why nobody stops your tablet or injects you to stimulate the test any more: a meta-analysis of 10 studies and 1,796 patients found that when high-sensitivity Tg is undetectable on thyroid hormone therapy, the negative predictive value for excluding structural disease is 99.4% (95% confidence interval 98.9 to 99.9)[7].

This edition is the first to write down that it can stop

Every previous edition told you how to follow up and never told you which year you could stop. The 2025 one does. Patients who are low risk, had a total thyroidectomy and radioiodine, and have sustained an excellent response can discontinue routine neck ultrasound after five to eight years, with biochemical testing every one to two years thereafter (Conditional recommendation, low certainty evidence); and those who sustain an excellent response for ten to fifteen years do not require continued routine biochemical monitoring and should be considered to have achieved a complete remission — which the guideline does not grade, listing it as good practice[1]. Those two words, complete remission, are new to this guideline's text.

Hold the two conditions together: low risk, and sustained excellent response. The guideline notes that data are more limited for people treated with lobectomy; for them it writes that if the initial ultrasound is negative, subsequent ultrasounds should be done every one to three years for five to eight years.

One distinction has to stay clean. What can stop is routine cancer surveillance, not seeing a doctor. How the levothyroxine dose is adjusted, and whether that signal keeps being tracked, is a separate matter; I could not find a clause in the 2025 full text specifying how often TSH should be measured, so I am not going to supply one on its behalf. And a corrigendum was issued after publication, correcting two authors' affiliations, one middle initial and a table row heading — it did not touch this section[8].

The boxes on your follow-up sheet that are still blank

Spread the follow-up schedule out and go through these five, one at a time:

  • "With my extent of surgery and my radioiodine status, which line should my Tg be read against?"
  • "Are my anti-thyroglobulin antibodies positive or negative? If positive, how does my follow-up change?"
  • "Which response category am I in now?"
  • "When is my next ultrasound due? What would bring it forward?"
  • "On this trajectory, is there a day I get to stop routine surveillance? Roughly how far away?"

Please actually say the last one out loud: "If I do not meet the conditions for stopping yet, write down for me which one is missing and roughly how many more years."

From the physician

Read these out at your next appointment: "With my extent of surgery and my radioiodine status, which line should my Tg be read against?" and "Are my anti-thyroglobulin antibodies positive or negative?" Then the question almost nobody asks: "On this trajectory, is there a day I get to stop routine surveillance, and roughly how far away is it?"
