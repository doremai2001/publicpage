Two weeks for the report: what is it waiting for

That report is not one report, it is several layers stacked up. This article covers what each layer tests, which items can change the name at the top, and how much uncertainty the tests themselves carry.

Glioblastoma Guide · After the Diagnosis · 9 references
#Molecular Testing#Biomarkers#Staging#Treatment Pathway

The operation is over and the treatment has not started, and this is the hardest stretch of the whole course. I am not going to give you a number of days — for how long a pathology report takes, I could not find data I could cite. What I can give you is the shape of the thing you are waiting for.

The operation is over, and all the family have been given is the sentence "we're waiting for the pathology". The surgery is done, the treatment has not started, and you do not know what it is you are waiting for. This article takes that report apart for you: how many layers it has, what each layer is testing, and which items can change the name at the top of it.

One report is really several layers stacked on top of each other

The frozen section during surgery can give a broad direction the same day, but it is not the final answer. The real report takes time, because it is not one report — it is several layers stacked up: first fixing, embedding in paraffin, sectioning, staining, and reading the morphology; then immunostaining; and where that is not enough, molecular testing — some of it sequencing, some of it array-based, some of it sent out to another laboratory. Each extra layer is another wait, and each layer can overturn the conclusion of the one before. Which is why we will not hand you a halfway answer that may have to be taken back.

How many days, then? For pathology report turnaround times, international or Taiwanese, I could not find data I could cite, so I am not going to invent a number of days for you — ask your consultant directly, because he knows which layer your specimen is sitting in. The guidelines know about the tension in the middle of this: the 2021 EANO guideline on adult diffuse gliomas says that molecular diagnostic workflows in glioma should be standardised, and that they should not cause a delay in radiotherapy or in tumour drug treatment[1]. The waiting is for the sake of getting it right, but waiting too long is itself a problem.

IDH gets a stain first; only if the stain shows nothing does it go for sequencing

The routine first step is immunohistochemistry: looking for the IDH1 R132H mutant protein, and for loss of nuclear ATRX expression[1]. When can a negative stain settle it? EANO gives a very specific set of conditions: age over 55, histologically typical glioblastoma, no antecedent lower-grade glioma, tumour not in a midline location, and retained nuclear ATRX expression — when all of those hold, a negative IDH1 R132H stain is enough to classify the tumour as IDH-wildtype glioblastoma[1]. In every other situation, a negative stain should be followed by DNA sequencing of IDH1 and IDH2 to find atypical mutations; the recommendation field is blunter still: all grade 2 and 3 diffuse astrocytic and oligodendroglial tumours, and all glioblastomas in patients under 55, should have IDH1 codon 132 and IDH2 codon 172 sequenced[1].

Why the extra step? Because that stain recognises one mutation only. The distribution measured in the CATNON trial: among IDH1-positive patients, R132H accounted for 86.9% and 84.7% (two readings), with the rest being R132C, R132G, R132L and R132S; and about 3% of patients carried an IDH2 mutation instead[2]. The remaining bit over 10% is visible only to sequencing. Posterior fossa and brainstem gliomas need more care again; the guideline says it outright — IDH1 R132H immunohistochemistry alone tends to misclassify them[1].

How that MGMT line is actually measured

MGMT can be tested by methylation-specific PCR (MSP), by pyrosequencing, or on a methylation array; and immunocytochemistry is not an appropriate method for determining MGMT promoter methylation status[1]. EANO lists two of its own unresolved problems: establishing an assay with adequate consistency between laboratories, and estimating how the intermediate state of "limited methylation" affects outcome[1].

A 2019 Neuro-Oncology review devoted to this question lays out the detail: the MGMT promoter CpG island is 777 base pairs long and contains 97 CpG sites, and different methods measure different sites, which is where the inconsistency comes from[3]. Three concrete numbers. One group re-tested 465 glioblastoma specimens by MSP and found that repeat experiments on the same specimens were discordant 12% of the time, and that the survival curve of the patients with discordant results ran parallel to that of the unmethylated group. Quantitative MSP has what is called a grey zone, because uncertainty around the threshold is high. Pyrosequencing can quantify the percentage at each site, but there are different practices as to whether the cut-off should be 8% or 25%, and those thresholds have never been validated in a clinical trial[3].

There is a direct head-to-head too: CATNON tested MGMT in IDH-wildtype tumours with both MS-PCR and a methylation-array algorithm, and the two agreed 85% of the time, kappa 0.67[2]. Kappa is the agreement that is left once agreement by chance has been subtracted, and 0.67 is moderate-to-good, not an exact match. So this is how I put it in clinic: that MGMT line is not a blood glucose measurement. It is different laboratories measuring with different rulers, and the markings on the ruler are not yet agreed. What that line decides for treatment, and what it does not, is in "That MGMT line: what it decides and what it does not".

1p/19q: FISH cannot separate a whole-arm loss from a partial one

EANO recommends testing for 1p/19q codeletion in all IDH-mutant gliomas with retained nuclear ATRX expression[1]. What has to be demonstrated is loss of the whole chromosome arm, and the fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) traditionally used cannot distinguish a partial loss from a whole-arm loss; a partial loss called positive by FISH is, diagnostically, a false positive[4]. The Mayo Clinic checked FISH against chromosomal microarray in 223 adult diffuse astrocytic gliomas and put the estimated FISH false-positive rate at 3.6% (8 cases)[4]. An Italian series checked FISH against next-generation sequencing: of 60 comparable cases, 49 (81.7%) agreed, and the other 11 were interstitial gains or losses near the probes, which had made FISH overdiagnose 1p/19q codeletion[5].

3.6% means "be careful", not "cannot be trusted". It matters because this item decides whether the tumour is an oligodendroglioma or not, and the treatment recommendations for oligodendroglioma and astrocytoma differ. Get it wrong and you are on the other road — which is written up in "The report says IDH-mutant: you are on a different road".

Ki-67 comes as a percentage, and it is not on the criteria list

Plenty of people go looking for Ki-67 first when they get the report, because it is a percentage and so it looks like a mark out of a hundred. It counts the proportion of cells that are dividing, which gives the pathologist a feel for how active the tumour is. But it is not among the parameters the 2021 WHO classification uses to set the name or the grade: on the histological side those are microvascular proliferation and necrosis, and on the molecular side the TERT promoter mutation, EGFR amplification and +7/−10 in IDH-wildtype tumours, plus homozygous deletion of CDKN2A/B in IDH-mutant astrocytoma[6]. I could not find a citable clinical cut-off for reading Ki-67, so I am not going to tell you that "over such-and-such a per cent means" anything.

One technical limit belongs here as well, because it explains why some items simply have to go for molecular testing. What the EGFR box needs is focal high-level amplification; one extra copy of chromosome 7 does not reach that bar, and immunostaining is not sufficient to call EGFR amplification[7].

Methylation profiling can change the diagnosis, and it has boundaries

Of the DNA methylation-based classifier published in Nature in 2018, the authors write that compared with standard methods the approach may have a substantial impact on diagnostic precision, changing the diagnosis in up to 12% of prospective cases[8]. WHO CNS5 is restrained about where it belongs: it is a valid adjunct, to be used alongside standard techniques including histology[6]. Where it earns its keep is with morphologically unusual tumours, with rare entities that only it recognises, and when the specimen is very small; it can also stand in for certain genetic events, with one line drawn — it cannot do so where a targeted therapy or a clinical trial requires a specific mutation to be demonstrated first[6]. There are score thresholds for interpretation as well: a suggested diagnosis with a score below 0.84 should be treated with caution, and below 0.50 it should probably be discarded; and the method actually struggles with lower-grade diffuse gliomas, besides not being widely available at present[6].

Ontario's 2025 health technology assessment included 38 studies and wrote both sides: as an adjunct tool it may improve the classification of central nervous system tumours (GRADE moderate); it may improve subsequent patient outcomes, but the evidence is very uncertain (GRADE very low); and unclassifiable results may lengthen the time to treatment[9]. The costs in that assessment belong to the Canadian system and the Canadian currency and cannot be converted into New Taiwan dollars. Which of these tests are available in Taiwan, and whether the NHI reimburses them, I could not find an official provision I could cite — ask your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.

A changed name does not mean your disease got worse in these two weeks

A changed name, in a patient's ear, nearly always means "it has got more serious". So let me finish by setting out the mechanisms that change a name — all of them are changes in the classification rules:

  • A molecular condition can override the grade seen down the microscope. It can look like grade 2 or 3, and if a TERT promoter mutation, EGFR amplification or +7/−10 turns up, the name becomes glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, CNS WHO grade 4[6].

  • Homozygous deletion of CDKN2A/B makes an IDH-mutant astrocytoma grade 4 outright[6].

  • And one that is not a changed diagnosis but a completed one: the NOS suffix on a report means the molecular testing that should have been done is unfinished or failed, and NEC means it was done but will not assemble into a standard WHO diagnosis[6]. NOS does not mean "we cannot find out".

How new all this is can be judged from the fact that even national cancer registries are still catching up — that passage, with the full rules for how the name is settled, is in "Glioblastoma: what it is, and what it is not".

During these two weeks of waiting there is one thing you can do that requires no pathology at all: change the question. Do not ask "how much longer", because that only gets you a number of days nobody can promise. Ask "which of these items are finished, and which one are we still waiting on" — then you will know which layer your specimen is stuck at and how many are left. Waiting for a report does not put an emergency on hold; what should bring you back to hospital the same day is in "When to come back the same day: three tiers".

From the physician

The waiting is not empty: that report is being built one layer at a time, and each layer can overturn the one before it. So the most useful move in these two weeks is to change the question — not "how much longer", but "which of these items are finished, and which one are we still waiting on". Nothing about the illness pauses while the report is out; read the thresholds in "When to come back the same day: three tiers" first.
