After metastasis, genomic testing is about finding a drug

51% of patients have an ESCAT tier I or II alteration detected and only 7% actually receive a matched drug; and in Taiwan that gap has the layer of NHI provisions added on top of it.

It goes by the same name, "genetic testing", but the purpose of this one is almost the opposite of the other two: it is not to prove you can have less treatment, it is to find a target that can be hit. What each of the three lines is answering is set out in "Which lines of the report actually change treatment". One thing has to be settled first: this entire article is about metastatic breast cancer. If your notes say stage I to III and the aim is to cure it, this is not your subject — yours is "What this test is actually trying to prove". What germline BRCA means for your relatives is in "Family history: should you test for BRCA now?".

The two numbers this article turns on: 51% and 7%

The Breast International Group's AURORA programme analysed matched primary tumour and metastatic lesion samples from 381 patients with metastatic breast cancer: an alteration of ESCAT tier I or II was detected in 51% of patients, while 7% actually received matched therapy[1]. ESCAT is an ESMO scale that ranks genomic alterations by the strength of the evidence behind them, tier I being the hardest[2].

SAFIR02-BREAST is the same story: 1,462 patients with HER2-non-overexpressing metastatic breast cancer were genomically profiled, and only 238 went on to randomisation[3]. In a real-world molecular tumour board analysis (one European cancer centre, 2019 to 2023, 96 patients with metastatic breast cancer), 56 patients (58%) had at least one actionable target; of the 50 with follow-up data, 32 received matched therapy and 18 received standard treatment, and real-world progression-free survival was no different between the two groups (4.07 against 3.12 months, P=0.8)[4].

Thinking of sequencing as an act that "will find me a drug" is the commonest misunderstanding there is about it. What it does is lay the list of options open to be looked at, and most of the time there is no new line on the list.

Your subtype sets the odds that anything will be found

An analysis using cell-free DNA in plasma, with ESCAT tier I/II as the standard, gives the figures broken out by subtype: the proportion with an actionable alteration on the baseline blood draw was 63% in hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative disease (n=344), 38% in HER2-positive disease (n=42) and 25% in triple-negative disease (n=95)[5]. Among triple-negative patients, roughly three out of every four have no target of that class detected on the baseline blood draw.

Finding no usable target is a common result. It is not bad news, and it is not a failed test. All it means is this: the few positions that a drug can be aimed at happen not to be on your tumour, and treatment carries on along the road it was already following.

ESMO's position changed in 2024, and what changed is when to test and what for

The 2024 update from the ESMO Precision Medicine Working Group extended the recommendation to cover patients with advanced breast cancer, on top of the cancer types for which tumour next-generation sequencing was already recommended[6]. Reporting of the same update puts the breast cancer part more concretely: tumour next-generation sequencing is now recommended in advanced breast cancer, timed to after resistance to endocrine therapy has developed, with the purpose of increasing the chance of detecting an ESR1 mutation; ESR1 was reclassified as ESCAT tier IA on the strength of elacestrant's efficacy[7].

So the accurate way to put it is "recommended, with a specified timing and a specified purpose" — not "not recommended", and not "sequence everything the moment it spreads". The other half of SAFIR02-BREAST shows why the tiering matters: where the alteration fell into ESCAT tier I or II, matched targeted therapy improved progression-free survival (adjusted hazard ratio 0.41, 90% confidence interval 0.27–0.61, P<0.001); without ESCAT selection there was no improvement (0.77, 0.56–1.06, P=0.109); and for alterations outside tiers I and II, the matched-therapy group had no benefit at all (unadjusted 1.15, 0.76–1.75)[3]. The two sentences have to be read together: what helps is not "having sequencing done", it is "landing on one of those few positions".

The positions that really do connect to a drug, each with its preconditions and its costs

The five positions that follow are the places in breast cancer where a drug can genuinely be connected today. Each comes with explicit preconditions — what you have already had, which line of therapy, which subtype — and the cost is written into the same paragraph.

The first is germline BRCA1/2, which connects to PARP inhibitors. OlympiAD enrolled 302 patients with metastatic, HER2-negative breast cancer carrying a germline BRCA mutation, olaparib against single-agent chemotherapy of the physician's choice: median progression-free survival 7.0 against 4.2 months (hazard ratio 0.58, 95% CI 0.43–0.80; a hazard ratio is the ratio between the rates at which events occur in the two groups), with grade 3 or higher adverse events in 36.6% against 50.5%[8]. EMBRACA enrolled 431 patients with advanced breast cancer carrying a germline BRCA1/2 mutation, talazoparib against single-agent therapy of the physician's choice: median progression-free survival 8.6 against 5.6 months; the cost is in the same sentence — grade 3 to 4 haematological adverse events (mainly anaemia) in 55% against 38%[9].

The second is a PIK3CA mutation, which connects to alpelisib. SOLAR-1 enrolled patients with hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative advanced breast cancer who had had prior endocrine therapy, 341 of them with a confirmed PIK3CA mutation: alpelisib plus fulvestrant against placebo plus fulvestrant, progression-free survival in the mutated cohort 11.0 against 5.7 months (hazard ratio 0.65, 0.50–0.85); in the cohort without a PIK3CA mutation it was 0.85 (0.58–1.25). The cost is very concrete: grade 3 or 4 hyperglycaemia in 36.6% (against 0.7% in the control arm), and discontinuation of alpelisib for adverse events in 25.0% against 4.2%[10]. Final overall survival was 39.3 against 31.4 months, hazard ratio 0.86 (0.64–1.15, P=0.15) — it did not cross the prespecified boundary for significance[11].

An AKT pathway alteration (PIK3CA, AKT1 or PTEN) connects to capivasertib. CAPItello-291 enrolled 708 patients with hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative advanced breast cancer whose disease had progressed during or after an aromatase inhibitor (premenopausal, perimenopausal and postmenopausal women, and men, all eligible), 289 of whom (40.8%) had an AKT pathway alteration: in that group median progression-free survival was 7.3 against 3.1 months (hazard ratio 0.50, 0.38–0.65); grade 3 or higher rash 12.1% and diarrhoea 9.3%, with discontinuation for adverse events in 13.0% against 2.3%[12]. As of the date I checked, this trial has no published final overall survival paper, so I am not going to say it has been shown to prolong survival.

Two more cells: ESR1 and HER2-low, each with its own proviso

An ESR1 mutation connects to elacestrant, or to a switching strategy. EMERALD enrolled 477 patients with ER-positive, HER2-negative advanced breast cancer (1 to 2 prior lines of endocrine therapy, and a CDK4/6 inhibitor obligatory), 47.8% of whom had a detectable ESR1 mutation: in the mutated population the hazard ratio for progression-free survival was 0.55 (0.39–0.77, P=0.0005); treatment-related grade 3/4 adverse events 7.2% against 3.1%[13]. SERENA-6 tested a different approach — checking plasma ESR1 at intervals during first-line treatment and switching drug on detection, before progression shows on imaging. The screening denominators tell the story best: 3,325 people were screened, 3,256 were tested at least once, 548 were positive, and in the end only 315 were randomised; median progression-free survival in the extended analysis was 16.8 against 9.2 months (hazard ratio 0.45, 0.34–0.59)[14]. Neither trial has reported a significant improvement in overall survival, so "switching lets you live longer" cannot be said yet.

HER2-low connects to T-DXd. DESTINY-Breast04 enrolled 557 patients with HER2-low metastatic breast cancer who had had one or two prior lines of chemotherapy (HER2-low meaning immunohistochemistry 1+, or 2+ with negative in situ hybridisation), of whom 494 (88.7%) were hormone-receptor-positive: in that group median progression-free survival was 10.1 against 5.4 months and median overall survival 23.9 against 17.5 months; adjudicated drug-related interstitial lung disease or pneumonitis occurred in 12.1%, of which 0.8% were fatal events (the figure in the label, calculated across the whole monotherapy population, differs slightly)[15]. DESTINY-Breast06 enrolled a different group: 866 patients who were hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-low or ultralow, and chemotherapy-naive in the metastatic setting; in the HER2-low population median progression-free survival was 13.2 against 8.1 months (hazard ratio 0.62, 0.52–0.75), with overall survival data not yet mature; adjudicated interstitial lung disease 11.3%, three of them fatal[16]. The preconditions of the two trials differ and their numbers cannot stand in for each other; HER2-low is a treatment classification, not a new subtype on the report.

Keep the interstitial lung disease item very concrete: dry cough, breathlessness, fever. It is unremarkable early on, and it can kill — any one of them while on T-DXd means contacting the treatment team the same day. The same goes for hyperglycaemia on alpelisib (thirst, constant trips to the toilet, fatigue). Which symptoms to report the same day for each of the drugs above are listed one by one in "Which side effects mean phoning the same day".

In Taiwan, the positions where the gaps sit

NHI next-generation sequencing in breast cancer covers only the germline BRCA1/2 blood test in triple-negative disease; the PIK3CA, AKT1 and PTEN report that alpelisib or capivasertib requires is itself outside the reimbursed indications[17]. On the drug side: PARP inhibitors (olaparib, talazoparib) in metastatic breast cancer are restricted to triple-negative disease, requiring HER2, ER and PR all negative, a germline BRCA1/2 mutation, and prior chemotherapy or inability to have it, with one of the two to be chosen[18]. OlympiAD and EMBRACA enrolled HER2-negative patients, hormone-receptor-positive ones included — a metastatic patient who is hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative and carries a germline BRCA mutation does not qualify under this provision in Taiwan, even though those two trials enrolled exactly her.

Alpelisib and capivasertib are both restricted to use with fulvestrant in postmenopausal metastatic breast cancer that has progressed after a CDK4/6 inhibitor, requiring ER or PR above 30%, HER2-negative, and the corresponding alteration; for a patient with a PIK3CA mutation, only one of the two drugs can be reimbursed[18] — whereas CAPItello-291 enrolled premenopausal, perimenopausal and postmenopausal women and men, and prior CDK4/6 inhibitor was not required[12]. The T-DXd HER2-low indication requires ER and PR both negative and at least one prior course of chemotherapy[18]; the HER2-low cohort of DESTINY-Breast04 was 88.7% hormone-receptor-positive[15]. HER2-ultralow does not appear in the provisions at all; a full-text search for elacestrant, tucatinib and camizestrant returns zero hits — there is at present no reimbursement route corresponding to the SERENA-6 approach[18].

I set these gaps out not to accuse anybody. A reimbursement provision is a line drawn between the evidence and the budget, and it updates more slowly than trials do, as it always has. What you can do is know where it has been drawn, and then ask. The prices of self-paid tests and the requirements on their form are in "This test is self-pay. Is it worth it?".

Before the next blood draw or biopsy, get these things straight

  • Ask first what question testing now is meant to answer. The timing ESMO gives is after endocrine resistance, and the purpose is mainly ESR1[7]. If no purpose comes out, do not rush to book it.

  • Get clear which specimen is being tested. AURORA showed that the genomic make-up of metastatic lesions differs from that of the primary tumour, with intrinsic subtype switching in 36%[1]. Using a five-year-old paraffin block of the primary to answer today's question may not answer it accurately.

  • No usable target on the report makes that a day on which the information is complete, not a day on which something failed[5]; the calendar carries on with the treatment and the imaging already planned.

  • If there is one on the report, ask four things: what ESCAT tier the alteration is, whether the corresponding drug is marketed in Taiwan, what preconditions the NHI provision demands, and whether the form of this report is one the NHI will accept[18]. Only when all four are passed does it amount to "one more option".

From the physician

No usable target on the report is not bad news, and it is not a failed test. At your next appointment finish four questions: what ESCAT tier this alteration is, whether the corresponding drug is marketed in Taiwan, what preconditions the NHI provision demands, and whether the form of this report is one the NHI will accept.
