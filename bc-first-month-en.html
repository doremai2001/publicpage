AFTER THE DIAGNOSIS

What the first month after diagnosis looks like

What the first month has to nail down is the tissue diagnosis, the receptor results and the stage; along the way it sorts out the administration — the catastrophic illness certificate, the waiting for reports, and the scheduling of surgery.

Breast Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 9 references
#Getting Ready#Treatment Pathway#Tumour Board#NHI Coverage

A catastrophic illness certificate is valid for three years in stage I disease; stage II and above fall under a different clause and get five years. And the effective date is the day the National Health Insurance Administration accepts the application, not the day you were diagnosed. There is one more piece of paperwork in this month than most people expect.

What most people remember about those first thirty days is not what happened on which day. It is the waiting: waiting for the biopsy report, waiting for the HER2 box, waiting for a slot in theatre. In the Ministry of Health and Welfare's analysis of the 2022 (ROC year 111) cancer registry, 17,366 women were newly diagnosed with breast cancer, a standardised incidence of 92.0 per 100,000 women, and the median age at diagnosis was 57[1] — an age at which most people still have a job and a family to hold up, which is why "when will there be an answer" often presses down earlier than "what is the answer". This article deals only with the process and the paperwork: what happens in the first month, which bits of waiting are normal, and which forms have to be filed.

The first step is not an operation. It is a needle

A systematic review commissioned by the US Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, covering the literature from 1990 to 2009, compared core-needle biopsy with open surgical biopsy: the two were close in accuracy for telling benign from malignant (the authors graded their own strength of evidence as low, meaning the conclusion could still be overturned by later work), but the risk of a serious complication was under 1% with core-needle biopsy against 2% to 10% with open biopsy; and people diagnosed by core needle were more likely to need only one trip to theatre, random-effects odds ratio 13.7, 95% confidence interval 5.5 to 34.6[2]. An odds ratio divides one group's ratio of happened-to-did-not-happen by the other group's; it cannot be read as 13.7 times the probability. The interval of 5.5 to 34.6 says that the estimate itself swings about a great deal. The population here was women at general risk with suspected breast cancer, with no breakdown by subtype.

A complete core-needle report has to exist before anyone can usefully discuss how much surgery, or whether to give drugs before operating. That is why most teams will not accept "just take it out and we will see".

Why has nobody booked me for a whole-body scan

This is the commonest grievance of the first month. Pooling 22 studies and 14,824 breast cancer patients who had staging imaging (median age 53), the median prevalence of distant metastases found was 7.0%, with a range from 1.2% to 48.8%, and it rose with stage — low in stages I and II, higher the later the disease[3]. The same review notes that PET-based studies detected a higher proportion of asymptomatic metastases, partly because the people selected for that scan were at higher risk to begin with. No breakdown by subtype.

In early breast cancer, whole-body imaging has a low chance of finding something real and a not-low chance of finding something that looks like it and needs another round of chasing — and that round costs exactly the time the next section but one is about. This is not about saving money; it is the ratio of what you get to what you pay. For PET in the initial staging of breast cancer, I could not find a corresponding provision in the NHI fee schedule, so this is one to confirm with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.

The report arrives in two batches, and that is usually the process working

In the NHI fee schedule, item 25012B, immunohistochemical staining, is limited to five antibodies per case, relaxed to ten only for the pathology specimen of a bilateral breast cancer; item 12195B, in situ hybridisation for human epidermal growth factor receptor 2, is limited to breast cancers with a HER2 immunohistochemistry score of 2+, and the provision states in writing that it is an adjunct to the immunohistochemistry result and must not be used on its own[4]. In other words, the HER2 box waiting for a second round is a sequence the regulations designed, not somebody mislaying your specimen. What these terms actually mean is set out in full in "What ER, PR and HER2 are telling you"; here I am only dealing with timing.

The imaging side has its rules too: item 33145B, diagnostic mammography, is restricted to specialists in diagnostic radiology, its stated indications are a mammography report of BIRADS 0, 3, 4 or 5 together with specified imaging findings, and it is limited to two per person per year[4]. How many working days a report takes to come back, I could not find regulated in either the NHI or the ministry documents, so please confirm that one with your case manager as well.

The multidisciplinary meeting is not an administrative formality

The west of Scotland ran a very large natural experiment: 13,722 women with symptomatic invasive breast cancer diagnosed between 1990 and 2000. Before multidisciplinary teams were introduced in 1995, breast cancer mortality in the intervention area was 11% higher than in the non-intervention area (adjusted hazard ratio 1.11, 95% confidence interval 1.00 to 1.20); afterwards it was 18% lower (hazard ratio 0.82, 0.74 to 0.91), and all-cause mortality 11% lower (0.89, 0.82 to 0.97)[5]. A hazard ratio of 0.82 means that over that period women in the intervention area were dying of breast cancer at roughly eight-tenths the rate of those in the other area; it does not mean "survival improved by 18%". This was a retrospective, non-randomised comparison, with no breakdown by subtype.

On the Taiwan side there is a structure behind this. The breast cancer care quality improvement programme announced by the National Health Insurance Administration on 13 February 2026 (ROC year 115) covers newly diagnosed and first-recurrence cases at every stage (men included), aims to enrol 80% of newly diagnosed patients nationally, has a treatment team of breast surgeons, diagnostic radiologists and case managers providing a single point of contact, and pays incentives for integrated care of a new case and for one follow-up care episode per year, with follow-up to five years and disease-free status, the whole programme funded at about NT$40 million[6]. The case manager beside you is not customer service. She is the contact point this programme specifies.

Catastrophic illness certificate: three years for stage I, five for the rest

Under Article 2, Appendix 1 of the Regulations Governing Exemption of Insured Persons from Copayment under National Health Insurance (the version applying from 1 January 2025, ROC year 114), within catastrophic illness category 1, "cancers requiring aggressive or long-term treatment", the certificate for "malignant tumour of the breast, stage I" is valid for three years; stage II and above do not sit under that clause and instead fall under "other malignant tumours", where the validity is five years[7]. Same disease, different stage, different number of years — and plenty of people only discover this when they come to renew.

The application can go by post, be delivered in person to a regional division, or be submitted on your behalf by the hospital through the NHI information network service system. The effective date is the date the insurer accepts the application, not the date you were diagnosed. If the application is made during an admission, copayment is waived from the first day of that admission; if the diagnosis is confirmed after discharge on the basis of tests performed during the admission, that admission and the related outpatient visits after discharge are also exempt. Renewal follows the length of validity, and certificates valid for two years or more are renewed by applying in the three months before expiry[8]. How many working days approval takes is not written on the administration's page, so this one is for your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office — file a day earlier and the clock starts a day earlier.

From diagnosis to surgery, how long counts as long

Two large databases point the same way. Among 94,544 SEER-Medicare patients aged 66 or over with non-inflammatory, non-metastatic invasive breast cancer diagnosed between 1992 and 2009, each step further along the waiting time (graded in 30-day bands) went with worse overall survival, hazard ratio 1.09, 95% confidence interval 1.06 to 1.13, with stage I (1.13) and stage II (1.06) reaching statistical significance; 115,790 patients in the US National Cancer Database gave 1.10 (1.07 to 1.13) in the same direction[9]. Both are observational data with no breakdown by subtype, and the authors state plainly that preoperative assessment and thinking about reconstruction take time in their own right. The grading is one band per 30 days, not "a point lost for every day of delay".

So the goal of the first month is not to shove everything forwards; it is to have no days that turn over without moving. Two things affect the schedule, and each has its own article. A germline BRCA result can change the surgical decision, so it has to be discussed before surgery — that is in "Family history: should you test for BRCA now?". Whether to add a preoperative magnetic resonance scan is a contested test that also costs time — that is in "The size on the scan isn't the real size". Male breast cancer and breast cancer in pregnancy follow a different logic and this series does not cover them.

Four sentences worth saying out loud at the first follow-up

There is a lot of information in the first month and very little time to ask about it. These four can be read off the page:

  • "Is my core-needle report complete? Which boxes are still outstanding, and roughly when will they all be in?"

  • "At my stage, will you be arranging whole-body staging imaging? If not, what is the reason?"

  • "Do I file the catastrophic illness application myself, or does the hospital submit it for me? Is the day it goes in the effective date?"

  • "When will the multidisciplinary team discuss my case? Who will tell me the conclusion, and when?"

Write the answers on one sheet of paper. By the end of the first month that sheet should carry a tissue diagnosis, a set of receptor results, a stage, and a date for surgery or drug treatment. Whichever line is missing is where the next clinic visit starts.

From the physician

Everyone's order of tests is different, and what is written here is the common rhythm, not your timetable. One thing you can do today: take a sheet of paper and leave a line each for the core-needle report, the receptor results, the stage and the date the catastrophic illness application went in. Whichever line is blank is where the next clinic visit starts.
