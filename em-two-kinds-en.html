DIAGNOSIS AND STAGING

Two kinds of endometrial cancer, now four molecular groups

The old two-type split looked at the patient's constitution; the new four groups look at the tumour's genetics — what the extra MMR, p53 and POLE lines on the report each mean, in one go.

Endometrial Cancer Guide · Diagnosis and staging · 14 references
#Molecular Testing#Staging#Evidence#Decisions

From a 366-patient paper in 1983, through TCGA in 2013, to a few abbreviations in the corner of today's report, the classification has been through three generations. One thing survived all of them: for most people, knowing which group you are in will not change the direction of treatment.

That one line on the pathology report — "endometrioid adenocarcinoma, grade 1" — would have been more or less the whole answer twenty years ago. These days the same report often carries a few extra letters further down: MMR, p53, POLE. This article is the foundation the rest of the topic sits on: where the old two-type split came from, why it was replaced, what the four current groups are, and one sentence that matters a great deal — knowing which group you are in does not mean your treatment changes with it.

The two types of 1983 were worked out from 366 patients

The man who split endometrial cancer into two types was Bokhman; the paper appeared in 1983 and enrolled 366 patients prospectively. Type 1 accounted for 65%: the group with obesity, hyperlipidaemia and excess oestrogen, of whom 82.3% were grade 1 or grade 2, 69.4% had only superficial myometrial invasion, 80.2% were sensitive to progestin, and 5-year survival was 85.6%. Type 2 accounted for 35%: the endocrine and metabolic abnormalities above were absent or slight, 62.5% were grade 3, 65.7% had deep myometrial invasion, pelvic lymph node metastases occurred in 27.8%, only 42.5% were sensitive to progestin, and 5-year survival was 58.8%[1].

That split works well in clinic, because it ties what you look like and what else you have been diagnosed with to your outlook. The problem is that what it divides is a clinical and endocrine phenotype, not the biology of the tumour.

Why it stopped holding up

A Canadian cohort of 1,357 patients went back over every endometrial cancer newly diagnosed in a single year, 2016, and reclassified them; the result is hard to explain with two types. Among the 954 low-grade (grade 1 to grade 2) endometrioid carcinomas, all four molecular groups were represented: no specific molecular profile accounted for 61.7%, but 19 cases (2.0%) were p53-abnormal. Read the other way, 91.4% of serous carcinomas were p53-abnormal — yet only 51.8% (129/249) of the p53-abnormal tumours were serous[2].

In plain language: under one histological name there are four diseases behaving in completely different ways, and half of the group with the worst outlook does not look the way everyone assumes it looks. Those are exactly the people the two-type split misses.

In 2013, TCGA broke it into four

The Cancer Genome Atlas published its integrated analysis in Nature in 2013: 373 endometrial carcinomas, of which 248 had exome sequencing. They separated four groups, named after what the mutations looked like: POLE ultramutated, microsatellite instability hypermutated, copy-number low, and copy-number high[3]. Mutation density across the four spans two orders of magnitude: 232, 18, 2.9 and 2.3 mutations per megabase respectively[3].

One thing needs saying clearly, because it is often got wrong online: there is exactly one proportion visible in that paper — POLE ultramutated, 17 cases, 7% of those 248[3]. What share the other three groups take, I cannot see in the full text available to me, so when I give proportions below I am using later clinical cohorts, not TCGA.

What is done in clinic is a much cheaper set of surrogate tests

Nobody sequences a whole genome to assign a group. The FIGO 2023 staging document relays how the 2020 fifth edition of the World Health Organization classification does it: immunohistochemistry plus one analysis for pathogenic POLE mutations gets you close to the same set of answers in clinical practice, and the four groups are named POLE-mutated, mismatch repair deficient, p53-abnormal, and no specific molecular profile[4][5]. The detail of how the mismatch repair proteins are stained, and what that has to do with inheritance, is in "Lynch syndrome: what the test means for you and your family".

This surrogate approach goes by two names. The Canadian team's version is called ProMisE, validated in 2015 on an independent cohort of 152 cases, and the paper contains one rather pointed finding: half of the POLE and mismatch repair deficient groups sit inside the clinically defined "high-risk" group[6]. The European version is called TransPORTEC, which produced the same four groups from 116 high-risk endometrial cancers: the POLE-mutated group (14 cases) and the microsatellite-instability group (19 cases) had no distant metastases at all, with 5-year recurrence-free survival of 93% and 95%; the p53-mutated group (36 cases) had 50% distant metastases and 42% recurrence-free survival[7].

What if one tumour meets the criteria for two groups at once? FIGO 2023 sets out the rule: where a tumour is POLE-mutated or mismatch repair deficient and also carries a secondary p53 abnormality, it is classified as POLE-mutated or mismatch repair deficient[4]. The classification can be done on a biopsy specimen, and if it has been done there it need not be repeated on the hysterectomy specimen[4].

How big each group is, and which way each one tends to go

Proportions have to come from clinical cohorts. That Canadian cohort of 1,357: POLE-mutated 6.3%, mismatch repair deficient 28.0%, no specific molecular profile 47.4%, p53-abnormal 18.3%[2]. Another, pooled cohort of 2,472 gives 7.4%, 25.8%, 49.5% and 17.3%[8]. The two are close, but please remember that these are clinical populations from the 2010s onwards, not the 373 research specimens in TCGA.

There are numbers for the direction of outlook too, but look hard at their denominator. The PORTEC-3 trial enrolled a high-risk population; 410 cases had molecular classification, and 5-year recurrence-free survival was 98% for POLE-mutated, 72% for mismatch repair deficient, 74% for no specific molecular profile, and 48% for p53-abnormal[9]. These are patients enrolled into a high-risk trial cohort, and cannot be taken to represent endometrial cancer patients in general.

One further finding explains why the molecular groups get treated as the foundation: a study of 2,478 cases together with a systematic review showed that within the POLE-mutated and p53-abnormal groups, grade and histological type no longer carry prognostic meaning; within the mismatch repair deficient group they do not either, in early-stage patients; only in the no-specific-molecular-profile group do grade and histological type still carry significant prognostic meaning[10].

The histology column is still there, and carcinosarcoma sits inside it

Molecular classification has not replaced histological type; both columns appear on the report. The histological types of endometrial carcinoma listed by FIGO 2023 include endometrioid, serous, clear cell, mucinous and undifferentiated carcinoma, carcinosarcoma, and rare types such as mesonephric-like carcinoma[4]. Grade is assigned by the proportion of solid growth: 5% or less is grade 1, 6% to 50% grade 2, more than 50% grade 3; nuclear atypia excessive for the grade raises a grade 1 or grade 2 tumour by one[4].

Where carcinosarcoma belongs is often misunderstood. The same document says, in its own words, that serous, clear cell, mesonephric-like, gastrointestinal-type mucinous and undifferentiated carcinomas "and carcinosarcomas are considered high-grade by definition" — that is, in the current staging document carcinosarcoma is a high-grade histological type of endometrial carcinoma, not something filed on the sarcoma side[4]. The four-group classification applies to it just the same: a meta-analysis of 5 studies and 263 uterine carcinosarcomas showed no recurrences and no deaths at all among the POLE-mutated carcinosarcomas, while p53-abnormal and no-specific-molecular-profile carcinosarcomas did worse than endometrioid and serous carcinomas of the same molecular group[11]. As for leiomyosarcoma and endometrial stromal sarcoma, together they make up more than 80% of uterine sarcomas, and they are a different set of diseases with a different surgical and treatment logic[12]; this topic does not cover them.

What difference does knowing your group make

The honest answer is that for most people it does not change the direction of treatment. A review written ten years after TCGA puts it more bluntly than I would: after a decade, the only real contribution of TCGA to the clinical management of these patients appears to be limited to the uncommon group with early-stage, high-grade endometrioid carcinomas carrying POLE exonuclease domain mutations, because they may be candidates for de-escalating treatment; knowledge of the other three groups has not changed management significantly[13]. How molecular classification actually alters adjuvant treatment, which evidence has already read out and which has not, is a whole article — "How molecular classification changes your treatment" — and I am not opening it here.

The testing itself is not universal either. The Canadian population-wide data from 2016 show that in that year only 42% of cases had mismatch repair immunohistochemistry and 21.1% had p53 immunohistochemistry; looking back, 54.7% of the mismatch repair deficient cases were never tested at the time[2]. On the Taiwan side, what I found is this: the National Health Insurance fee schedule has a general item, 25012B, "immunohistochemical staining", at 1,354 points, but searching the full document I could not find a listed item that writes in the mismatch repair proteins or endometrial cancer[14]. Not finding a listed item means not finding one; whether it can actually be claimed is a question for the medical affairs office or your case manager.

So next time you get a report, look for two lines: the histological type, and whether there is a field for MMR, p53 or POLE. If there is, copy it onto the first page of your notebook; if there is not, you are not behind. Ask first: "In my situation, would adding those tests change what you are about to recommend?" When the answer is no, that is a complete answer.

From the physician

When the report arrives, look for two lines only: the histological type, and whether MMR, p53 and POLE appear anywhere. If those lines are not there, it does not mean you were overlooked.
