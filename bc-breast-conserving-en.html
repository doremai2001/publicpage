Conservation or mastectomy: is survival the same?

The randomised trials say "not worse", and the 2021 Swedish cohort even leans towards conservation — but that result has always hung on the two words "plus radiotherapy".

Breast Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 7 references
#Surgery#Decisions#Breast Reconstruction#Quality of Life

Both of the foundation trials enrolled in the 1970s, when HER2-targeted drugs did not exist. The other number that usually gets skipped: bilateral mastectomy all but abolishes contralateral breast cancer, and yet the twenty-year breast cancer mortality is 8.50% against 8.54%.

"Conserve it or take it off — do I live just as long?" I get asked this most weeks in clinic, and the person asking has usually already read the word "same" somewhere online. The more accurate answer is this: conserving surgery plus radiotherapy gives long-term survival that is not worse than mastectomy, and the recent observational data lean towards the conserving side. Those two words cannot be dropped.

That word "same" came out of two trials. Here they are

The first is Veronesi's Milan trial: 701 women with operable breast cancer and tumours no larger than 2 cm, enrolled between 1973 and 1980, randomised to quadrantectomy with radiotherapy to the affected breast, or Halsted radical mastectomy. At a median follow-up of 20 years, all-cause mortality was 41.7% against 41.2% (P=1.0) and breast cancer mortality 26.1% against 24.3% (P=0.8)[1]. One thing in that same trial was clearly not the same: the 20-year cumulative probability of a new cancer in the same breast was 8.8% (30 of 352 in the conserving arm) against 2.3% (8 of 349 in the radical arm), P<0.001[1]. Survival the same, local recurrence not the same — those two sentences have to be said together.

The second is NSABP B-06: 1,851 patients with stage I and II invasive breast cancer, enrolled from 1976, split into three arms — total mastectomy, lumpectomy, and lumpectomy plus radiotherapy. At 20 years the hazard ratio for death with lumpectomy plus irradiation against total mastectomy was 0.97, 95% confidence interval 0.83 to 1.14, P=0.74 — a hazard ratio divides the speed at which deaths occur in one arm by the speed in the other, so 1 means both sides are running at the same pace, and the confidence interval is the plausible swing around that estimate[2]. The real point is the third arm: among people who had a lumpectomy and no radiotherapy, the 20-year ipsilateral breast recurrence rate was 39.2%, against 14.3% in the arm that had radiotherapy, P<0.001[2]. Breast conservation is not the option that avoids radiotherapy. It is a package that makes the surgery smaller and puts the radiotherapy in.

Both trials enrolled in the 1970s, and that has to be said out loud

There was no HER2-targeted drug in that era, none of today's chemotherapy combinations, and the pathology report did not yet carry the ER, PR and HER2 lines. Answering a 2026 question with 1970s patients leaves a gap by construction. Closer data come from Sweden: a national cohort of 48,986 women operated on between 2008 and 2017 for T1-2, N0-2 primary invasive breast cancer. After adjustment for comorbidity and socioeconomic status, mastectomy without radiotherapy against conserving surgery plus radiotherapy gave an all-cause mortality hazard ratio of 1.79 (95% confidence interval 1.66 to 1.92) and a breast cancer mortality hazard ratio of 1.66 (1.45 to 1.90); mastectomy with radiotherapy against conserving surgery plus radiotherapy gave an all-cause mortality hazard ratio of 1.24 (1.13 to 1.37)[3].

The median follow-up in that dataset is only 6.28 years, and it is observational — who went for conservation and who went for mastectomy was never decided by drawing lots, and the residual selection bias cannot be removed. The authors' own wording is that where both are feasible, mastectomy should not be regarded as equivalent to conservation[3]. So this is how I put it in clinic: conserving surgery plus radiotherapy is not worse than mastectomy, and in the recent cohort data it may even be better — but that "better" does not yet have a randomised trial holding it up.

What the radiotherapy actually buys inside that package

The Early Breast Cancer Trialists' Collaborative Group pooled individual data from 17 randomised trials and 10,801 women who had breast-conserving surgery: radiotherapy after surgery brought any first recurrence within 10 years down from 35.0% to 19.3% (an absolute reduction of 15.7 percentage points, 95% confidence interval 13.7 to 17.7) and 15-year breast cancer death from 25.2% to 21.4% (an absolute reduction of 3.8 percentage points, 1.6 to 6.0)[4]. Taken apart: in the 7,287 node-negative women, 10-year recurrence fell from 31.0% to 15.6% and 15-year breast cancer death from 20.5% to 17.2%; in the 1,050 node-positive women, 10-year recurrence fell from 63.7% to 42.5% and 15-year breast cancer death from 51.3% to 42.8%[4]. The same treatment buys very different things in those two groups. These trials enrolled across the 1970s to the 1990s and again carry no HER2 typing; what can be read off them is the order of magnitude of what radiotherapy buys inside this package, not your own absolute probability today.

The sentence in the other direction has to be just as clear: choosing mastectomy does not mean radiotherapy will not be needed. When it is still needed after mastectomy is in "Should the axilla and supraclavicular area be treated too?". How many treatments, and who can leave radiotherapy out, are in "Five treatments, fifteen, or twenty-five" and "Omitting radiotherapy: every condition has to hold" — the title of that second one is its own conclusion: the conditions come as a string, and one missing item breaks it.

Margins: if the ink has not touched the tumour, that is enough

The 2014 consensus from the Society of Surgical Oncology and the American Society for Radiation Oncology reviewed 33 studies and 28,162 patients with stage I and II invasive breast cancer receiving whole-breast irradiation: a positive margin (the pathologist's ink touching invasive cancer or carcinoma in situ) roughly doubles the risk of ipsilateral breast tumour recurrence — that is a ratio, not an absolute probability, and the consensus document gives no matching absolute percentage, so this "double" has to be read on top of the baseline risk estimated from your own report; and cutting a wider margin does not push the recurrence rate any lower, which is why the consensus set "no ink on tumour" as an adequate margin[5]. The same document states separately that this doubling is not abolished by favourable tumour biology, by being on endocrine therapy, or by a radiotherapy boost[5]. So if your report says the margin is positive and you are advised to go back to theatre, that is an evidence-based recommendation, not a standard set too strictly.

"Why not just take the other one as well" — I understand that thought completely

It is not an irrational thought. In the SEER database between 1998 and 2012, among 496,488 women with unilateral stage I to III invasive breast cancer, the proportion having contralateral prophylactic mastectomy rose from 3.9% in 2002 to 12.7% in 2012 (P<0.001), while over the same period the proportion of those women having reconstruction rose from 35.3% to 55.4%[6]. A great many people are doing the same thing. In the same dataset, with conserving surgery plus radiotherapy as the comparator, contralateral prophylactic mastectomy did not improve breast-cancer-specific survival and the point estimate leaned slightly unfavourable (hazard ratio 1.08, 95% confidence interval 1.01 to 1.16; overall survival 1.08, 1.03 to 1.14), and this did not change with hormone receptor status or with age[6] — this is observational data, and the people who go for that operation start out with a different risk, so this "slightly worse" cannot be read as caused by the surgery.

The second breast cancer is all but abolished, and breast cancer mortality does not move. Here is why

A 2024 SEER matched cohort puts it most clearly: 661,270 women with stage 0 to III unilateral breast cancer, matched 1:1:1 into lumpectomy, unilateral mastectomy and bilateral mastectomy, 36,028 in each group, followed for 20 years. The 20-year risk of contralateral breast cancer was 6.9% in the first two groups (95% confidence interval 6.1 to 7.9; 766 and 728 cases respectively), against only 97 cases in the bilateral mastectomy group; and the 20-year breast cancer deaths numbered 3,077 (8.54%, lumpectomy), 3,269 (9.07%, unilateral mastectomy) and 3,062 (8.50%, bilateral mastectomy)[7]. The cancer in the second breast is very nearly wiped out, and the proportion dying of breast cancer does not follow it down.

The same paper goes on to explain why. Treating contralateral breast cancer as a time-varying variable, women who developed one had a 15-year cumulative breast cancer mortality afterwards of 32.1%, against 14.5% in those who did not, hazard ratio 4.00 (95% confidence interval 3.52 to 4.54)[7]. A contralateral cancer marks a constitution that was already prone to letting cancer cells travel; it is not the cause of death. Removing the second breast removes the marker, not the constitution — and the thing that actually decides survival has, in most cases, already happened by the time the first tumour is diagnosed.

Two limits have to be read with that paragraph. SEER does not record BRCA testing results, so this section applies to people with no known inherited variant; prophylactic surgery in carriers follows a different logic, and it is in "Family history: should you test for BRCA now?". And "no survival benefit" is not the same as "should not be done" — anxiety, difficult imaging follow-up and symmetry between the two sides are all real reasons; what they buy is simply not survival. On the Taiwan side, I could not find a formal provision on National Health Insurance cover for contralateral prophylactic mastectomy. Confirm this one with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office, and do not leave it until the operation is booked.

Get these papers together, then decide how much to remove

How much to remove is settled in the consulting room, in front of the film on the lightbox — not on this page. Before you walk in, gather these:

  • Tumour size and position on the imaging report, and whether there is more than one lesion. This decides whether conserving surgery is technically possible.

  • The ratio of your breast size to the tumour. Two tumours both 2 cm across can leave very different appearances behind.

  • Whether you can fit the radiotherapy schedule: the daily journey, work, caring responsibilities. That survival figure for conserving surgery rests on the radiotherapy having been completed.

  • If you are considering having the other side removed as well, write down your reasons and bring them. Is it to have one less imaging follow-up to worry about, or because you believe it will lengthen survival? Those two need separating, because the two datasets above can only answer the second one.

Then get this out of your mouth: "With my tumour where it is and the size it is, can conserving surgery get a clean margin? If not, which item is the obstacle?" How far the axilla has to go is a separate decision, and it is in "How much of the axilla has to go?".

From the physician

On the day, take the imaging report with the tumour's size and position, and get "can conserving surgery get a clean margin" out of your mouth. If you are considering having the other breast removed as well, write the reasons down first — what that operation buys is not survival. For the reimbursement conditions for contralateral prophylactic mastectomy, ask your case manager first.
