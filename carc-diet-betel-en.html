Group 1 is not a danger ranking

Processed meat, alcohol, betel quid and asbestos all sit in Group 1, yet annual attributable cancer deaths run from about 34,000 to about 1 million; the grouping describes the strength of the evidence.

Carcinogens at work and at home · At Home · 23 references
#Diet & Habits#IARC Grouping#Prevention#Screening#Head & Neck

Inside one classification box, some things take a million lives a year and some take thirty-odd thousand. This piece turns the 18% for processed meat into people, spells out the conditions attached to the 14.5-fold figure for drinkers who flush, and puts the numbers to call for stopping betel quid and tobacco in the last section.

A sausage and a sheet of asbestos really do hang under the same Group 1 heading. "So the two are equally dangerous" is the wrong part. This piece pulls apart two things that get tied together: how certain the evidence is, and how large the risk is. Pull them apart and the items in that one box differ by tens of times in how much they are worth worrying about.

Processed meat and asbestos are both Group 1. Equally dangerous?

On the official classification list of the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), consumption of processed meat is Group 1 (Volume 114), red meat Group 2A; alcoholic beverages Group 1; betel quid with tobacco, betel quid without tobacco and areca nut itself all Group 1; asbestos and smoking Group 1[1]. One box, that many different things.

IARC says it outright in its questions and answers on red and processed meat: processed meat sits in the same category as smoking and asbestos, but this does not mean they are equally dangerous; the classification describes the strength of the scientific evidence that something causes cancer, not the size of the risk[2].

The same document gives magnitudes. On Global Burden of Disease data, annual attributable cancer deaths run: about 1 million for tobacco smoking, about 600,000 for alcohol, over 200,000 for air pollution, about 34,000 for diets high in processed meat[2]. Same heading, roughly 30 times between top and bottom: the box answers "does it", not "how much". The eight agents set out side by side are clearest on the entry page, "Carcinogens at work and at home".

"Up 18%" — how many people is that?

Volume 114 concluded that each extra 50 grams of processed meat a day — about two rashers of bacon or one hot dog — raises colorectal cancer risk by about 18%[2][3]; the evaluation was made by 22 experts from 10 countries, summarised in The Lancet Oncology[4]. That 18% is an increase in relative risk, not a probability of cancer becoming 118%. It only becomes legible in absolute numbers: Cancer Research UK's explainer uses the UK lifetime bowel cancer risk, where of the group eating the least processed meat about 56 in every 1,000 develop bowel cancer in their lifetime, and of the group eating the most about 66 — a difference of 10 people[5].

I will not give a "no more than X grams a day" threshold, because IARC states the available data are not sufficient to determine whether a safe level exists[2]; and I found no Taiwanese study of processed meat and colorectal cancer, so all of the above is international data. What the evidence supports is a direction, about quantity and frequency: every day and once a week are not the same thing.

Alcohol is a different case: the evidence is settled and the scale is large

IARC Monograph 100E concludes that drinking alcoholic beverages causes cancer of the oral cavity, pharynx, larynx, oesophagus, colorectum and liver, and female breast cancer; the acetaldehyde associated with alcoholic beverages is likewise Group 1[6].

The scale: in 2020, 741,300 new cancer diagnoses worldwide were attributable to alcohol, 4.1% of all new cancers, most of them oesophageal, liver and breast. East Asia is among the regions with the highest attributable proportion, population attributable fraction 5.7% (95% uncertainty interval 3.6% to 7.9%, the plausible range for the estimate, as with a confidence interval)[7] — an attributable fraction means that if nobody in the region drank, new cancer diagnoses would fall by about 5.7%, not that 5.7% of people will get cancer from drinking. Set beside the 34,000 for processed meat, that is why alcohol weighs more with me than sausage.

The WHO Regional Office for Europe's 2023 statement is that no level of alcohol consumption is without effect on health, and that half of alcohol-attributable cancers in the European region arise from light and moderate drinking[8]. That is "risk never reaches zero", not a prohibition. In Taiwan, the 2021 National Health Interview Survey found a past-month drinking rate of 24.4% among adults 18 and over[9]; the Health Promotion Administration separately estimates about 4,500 deaths a year from alcohol harm — all alcohol-related deaths, not alcohol-attributable cancer deaths[10]. How many cancers in Taiwan are attributable to alcohol I found no official estimate for: take the 741,300 and the 4.1% as global magnitudes, not Taiwanese figures.

Flushing is not good metabolism. It is acetaldehyde accumulating

This section matters most for Taiwan. Ministry of Health and Welfare public information states that close to half the population of Taiwan flushes when drinking, from a lack of ALDH2[9]. A review co-authored from the National Health Research Institutes, National Cheng Kung University and Stanford reports East Asian ALDH2*2 prevalence running from 28% in Korea to as high as 45% in Taiwan[11] — that 45% is quoted in a review, not an official Taiwanese survey. The foundational international literature gives an East Asian average of about 36% with the flushing reaction[12]. The three figures differ because the denominators are the Taiwanese population, the Taiwanese carrier proportion and East Asia as a whole; the direction is the same, Taiwan among the highest-proportion places in the world. Adjusted for amount drunk, odds ratios for oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma in ALDH2 heterozygotes run 3.7 to 18.1[12]. IARC 100E writes the same sentence: people with a deficient capacity to oxidise acetaldehyde have a substantially increased risk of alcohol-related cancers[6].

Taiwan's own numbers are more specific. A multicentre case-control study from Kaohsiung Medical University took 406 patients with oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma and 656 matched controls. The inactive ALDH2*1/*2 genotype combined with drinking carries a risk far higher than either alone, the direction multiplicative: with light-to-moderate drinking of 0.1 to 30 grams of alcohol a day, the joint adjusted odds ratio is 14.5; with heavy drinking of more than 30 grams a day, 102.6[13]. Read the conditions: the 14.5 box means "flushes" plus "under two cans of beer a day", not alcoholism.

An odds ratio divides one group's happened-to-not-happened ratio by another's, so it is not a multiple of probability and needs an absolute magnitude beside it: oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma incidence in East Asia is roughly 11 cases per 100,000 per year[11]. That is annual incidence, not lifetime risk, and over decades it accumulates to considerably more; even so, multiplied by ten-odd, most people who flush and drink will not develop oesophageal cancer. For lifetime absolute risk in this group Taiwan has no citable estimate, and I will not calculate one.

The causal chain matters too: acetaldehyde accumulates only where the deficiency is multiplied by drinking, and someone who flushes but does not drink does not carry this risk. Worth having the gene tested? No — the same review calculates that at that incidence, even with sensitivity and specificity both at 99%, the positive predictive value is only 1.1%[11]; while asking in clinic "did drinking use to make you flush", in a two-item questionnaire, has sensitivity and specificity both around 90%[12].

Does cutting down help? IARC Handbooks of Cancer Prevention Volume 20A concludes that reducing or stopping alcohol consumption reduces the incidence of oral and oesophageal cancer, on sufficient evidence; for laryngeal, colorectal and breast cancer the evidence is limited[14]. The public health message is less is better, not that it only counts at zero.

Betel quid is the one I will not soften

IARC 100E: betel quid with tobacco causes cancer of the oral cavity, pharynx and oesophagus; without tobacco it causes cancer of the oral cavity and oesophagus, with a positive association observed for liver cancer[15]. "No tobacco, no inflorescence, so it is fine" is wrong — the areca nut itself is Group 1[1]. The same volume records the chewing pattern peculiar to Taiwan: taken with fresh Piper betle inflorescence, safrole and hydroxychavicol reach 10 to 15 mg per gram; urinary hydroxychavicol in Taiwanese chewers is 3 times that of non-chewers, and safrole-related DNA adducts are detectable in the oral cavity[15].

The Taiwanese figure comes from a 1995 case-control study at Kaohsiung Medical College: 107 oral cancer patients, 200 matched controls. Adjusted for education and occupation, people who smoked, drank and chewed betel quid had a joint odds ratio for oral cancer 123 times that of people doing none of the three[16]. That 123 is a case-control odds ratio comparing all three against none, and it cannot be multiplied by an incidence rate to give an individual's probability of falling ill. Age-standardised oral cancer incidence in Taiwanese men is 27.19 per 100,000 per year[17], an average across all men; for the cumulative lifetime probability of someone doing all three Taiwan has no citable figure, and I will not calculate one. What the multiple establishes is that the three stacked together come to far more than the sum of their parts. Chewing alone is significantly associated with oral cancer, and swallowing the juice or using unripe fruit adds further[16]. None of it amounts to "betel quid causes such-and-such a share of oral cancer": for that attributable fraction I found no citable figure, and the proportions circulating online have no source.

The absolute scale: the 2023 cancer registry recorded 138,051 new cancers, oral cancer (including oropharynx and hypopharynx) 6th across all cancers and 4th in men[18]. On registry data from 1980 to 2019, standardised incidence in men rose from 4.19 to 27.19 per 100,000 (annual percentage change 5.1%), in women from 1.16 to 2.8[17]. That is a curve that has genuinely grown on the bodies of Taiwanese men.

"I've already quit — do I still need screening?" Yes

The Department of Oral Health, Ministry of Health and Welfare sets out eligibility plainly: people aged 30 and over who chew betel quid (including those who have quit) or smoke, and Indigenous people aged 18 to under 30 who chew betel quid (including those who have quit), get a mucosal examination once every 2 years at a contracted clinic on presentation of an NHI card[19]. The words "including those who have quit" are the ones I most often repeat.

The examination has outcome data: an analysis of 2,334,299 smokers or betel quid chewers, adjusted for self-selection bias, gives a relative risk of 0.74 (95% confidence interval 0.72 to 0.77) for oral cancer mortality and 0.79 (0.76 to 0.82) for advanced oral cancer[20], the roughly 26% reduction in deaths and 21% in advanced cases the official material describes[19]. Oral mucosal examination is one of the four national cancer screenings; eligibility for the other three (colorectal, cervical, breast) is not in this piece.

Starting needs a phone number, not willpower

The formal entry point for stopping betel quid is the Department of Oral Health's search for medical institutions providing betel quid cessation counselling, with a downloadable national list, or referral through a local health bureau[21]; the Health Promotion Administration has funded hospital betel quid cessation classes since 2010[22].

  • Stopping smoking: the free quitline on 0800-636363, Monday to Saturday 9am to 9pm, voicemail outside those hours, and the official LINE account @tsh0800636363[23].

  • Alcohol comes down first, with no need to wait until it can be cut out altogether; both reduction and cessation were judged to lower the incidence of oral and oesophageal cancer[14]. For someone who flushes, saying "my ALDH2 can't handle it" when the round comes is more honest than matching the table.

  • Processed meat is a question of frequency: going from every day to once or twice a week has a continuous effect, with no pass mark to clear.

  • In front of a mirror: a break in the skin unhealed at two weeks, a red or white patch, a mouth that opens less far, numbness of the tongue or cheek — ear, nose and throat or oral surgery; and a mucosal examination every two years for anyone eligible.

Not one line above asks you to start by admitting you did something wrong. Every one of them starts with a phone call or a single appointment.

From the physician

I am not planning to talk you out of sausage; that is not the heaviest thing people in Taiwan carry. Flushing and still drinking a little every day, and chewing betel quid for decades without ever having a mucosal examination — those two are. People who have quit betel quid remain eligible for the publicly funded examination, once every two years, with an NHI card.
