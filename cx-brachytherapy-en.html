Brachytherapy: the scariest name, the decisive days

The tumour needs 85 to 95 Gy; the rectum and bladder pressed against it can take only 65 to 90 — a gap only brachytherapy, delivered from inside, can fill, and the price of skipping it is survival.

Cervical Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 14 references
#Brachytherapy#Decisions#Side Effects#NHI Coverage

This article does two things at once: it nails down the answer to "can I skip it?" with database evidence and the guideline's own words, and it tells every step — insertion, anaesthesia, the waiting, the pain — as it is, including under which protocol that 41% post-traumatic stress figure was measured. The fear, most of the time, is because nobody told the truth first.

My position first: definitive chemoradiation and brachytherapy are the treatments I deliver every day. So every sentence about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it — not more warmly.

The name "brachytherapy" pulls the same set of questions out of people in clinic, over and over: can I skip it? Can it be replaced with one of the newer "high-precision" radiotherapy machines? I am going to nail the answer down first and take the procedure apart afterwards — because the price of getting this one wrong is survival. Brachytherapy is a component of curative radiotherapy, on the same footing as the external-beam treatment and the weekly chemotherapy; it is not an optional boost, and no degree of precision in external-beam radiotherapy can substitute for it. The rest of this article explains why, and what those few days actually involve.

The dose the tumour needs and the dose its neighbours can take do not meet

Lay the numbers out and it explains itself. The 2023 update of the guidelines from three European societies (ESGO/ESTRO/ESP) is very concrete: external-beam radiotherapy gives the whole pelvis 45 Gy in 25 fractions, and brachytherapy has to add another 40 to 45 Gy of equivalent dose on top of that, pushing the tumour core to 85 to 95 Gy[1]. The problem is that the rectum and bladder sit pressed right against the cervix: the protocol of EMBRACE-II, the international prospective study, sets the dose limit for the rectum at 65 to 75 Gy and for the bladder at 80 to 90 Gy[2]. The tumour needs 85 to 95; the organs touching it are allowed only 65 to 90 — a beam fired in from outside the body cannot satisfy both of those at once. Put the radiation source inside the tumour, where dose falls away steeply with distance, and that gap can be built within a few millimetres. This is not a question of brand or technology tier. It is geometry.

The price of skipping it has already been measured, in thousands of patients

The 2024 updated analysis of the US SEER database took 8,500 locally advanced patients treated with external-beam radiotherapy between 2000 and 2020; after matching, those who had brachytherapy had four-year overall survival of 64.0% against 51.4% without it; the hazard ratio for death from cervical cancer was 0.70 (95% confidence interval 0.64 to 0.76 — a confidence interval is the range within which the estimate can reasonably swing, and the whole of it sits below 1, so the direction is steady), and for death from any cause 0.72 (a hazard ratio divides the speed at which events occur in one group by the speed in the other; below 1 means death arrived distinctly more slowly in the brachytherapy group)[3]. The other dataset is the one to be more wary of: among 7,654 stage IIB to IVA patients in the NCDB database treated from 2004 to 2011, those whose boost was delivered with "high-precision" external techniques such as IMRT or SBRT instead of brachytherapy had a hazard ratio for death of 1.86 (1.35 to 2.55) — a harm larger than leaving chemotherapy out of the entire course (1.61)[4]. These are observational databases, not randomised trials, and residual confounding can remain after matching; but the direction agrees completely with the prospective data and with the dose logic, and the guidelines have already closed the question.

There is a lesson in this history. An earlier analysis by the same team found that brachytherapy use in the United States fell from 83% in 1988 to 58% in 2009, dipping to 43% in 2003 — exactly the years the various new external-beam technologies came to market[5]. The field then turned round and corrected itself: in 2014 a dedicated article appeared whose title reads "Curative radiation therapy for locally advanced cervical cancer: brachytherapy is NOT optional"[6]; by 2018 to 2020, use had climbed back to 76%[3]. "Everyone is doing it less and less" is history, not the present; but the survival that history cost is the reason this article exists. The ESGO 2023 original I translate directly: image-guided brachytherapy is an essential component of definitive radiotherapy and is not to be replaced by an external-beam boost — not with photons, and not with protons; if a hospital cannot deliver it, the patient should be referred to a centre that can[1]. You do not need to interrogate any hospital by name. One question is enough: "How will my brachytherapy be arranged?"

What doing it well buys

EMBRACE-I enrolled 1,341 patients across 24 centres, treated with MRI-guided brachytherapy: five-year local control was 92%; severe (grade 3 or higher) late effects ran at 6.8% for the urinary tract, 8.5% for the bowel and 5.7% for the vagina — each under one in ten[7]. Note that "local control" means the tumour does not come back where it was — it is not a survival rate, and the two must not be swapped for each other. The stage-by-stage figures are in retroEMBRACE: five-year local control 98% for stage IB, 91% for IIB, 75% for IIIB[8] — a large tumour treated is still not one hundred percent, and I am not going to write it up as a guarantee; but this is the best the prospective data can currently show, and its precondition is that the brachytherapy was done, and done in full.

What actually happens on those days

I will go through the procedure step by step, without prettifying it. Under anaesthesia, the doctor passes a thin tube (an intrauterine tandem) through the vagina into the uterine cavity, and adds an ovoid or ring applicator at the vaginal end; when the shape of the tumour requires it, thin needles are inserted directly into the tissue. "Should be performed under anaesthesia" is the guideline's own wording, not my personal habit[1]; whether that means general, regional or sedation differs between hospitals, and it is a question you can ask in advance. After insertion comes imaging (the guideline's first choice is MRI, with CT or ultrasound also acceptable), and the dose is recalculated for every single insertion before treatment is given[1][9] — so after the applicator is in place there is still a wait, and what you are waiting for is not a queue: it is the physicist calculating your safety. The high-dose-rate (HDR) approach is usually delivered in 3 to 4 sessions, at least 6 to 8 hours apart, and for large tumours the brachytherapy is scheduled towards the end of chemoradiation or within one to two weeks after it[1]; the American Brachytherapy Society's commonly used schedule is 5 sessions[9] — the number follows each hospital's dose design, and neither 3 nor 5 means anyone did it wrong. The irradiation itself is very short: the source comes in and is withdrawn within minutes[10]. Across the whole day, what takes the time is the insertion, the imaging and the calculation — not the irradiation.

The discomfort is real, and I am not going to gloss it

Somebody has measured this honestly. A single-institution prospective study followed 50 patients — that hospital's protocol was a single insertion with the applicator left in place overnight, two fractions delivered through it, under spinal or epidural anaesthesia — and found acute stress symptoms in 30% at one week after treatment, and post-traumatic stress symptoms in 41% at three months[11]. The same study took the sources of that stress apart: it was mainly not the insertion itself but the stretch between the two fractions, immobilised with the applicator in place; what helped were explanation beforehand, pain that somebody was managing, and the team staying close[11]. So that 41% cannot be applied directly to a protocol where insertion and treatment are completed the same day — but what it warns of is universal: a systematic review of 19 studies found that 9 of the 10 addressing the psychological dimension described brachytherapy as a distressing experience, and that analgesia and anaesthesia practice varies widely between hospitals[12]. My conclusion is direct: it will be uncomfortable — that is the truth; but the pain can be managed, provided you know the procedure in advance and say so, at the time, when it hurts. The patients who are frightened into abandoning treatment halfway are mostly the ones nobody told the truth to first.

When it is over, there is no radioactivity in your body

In HDR brachytherapy the radiation source is inside you for only a few minutes at a time, and once it is withdrawn, no radioactive material of any kind stays behind[10]. Holding a child, sharing a bed with your partner — none of it needs isolation. The passage on this site in "Sex during treatment: who raises it?" about "radioactive seeds in the body" refers to the permanent implants used in prostate cancer, which is a different thing entirely from the remote-afterloading treatment here — please do not read that section onto yourself. The full list of situations during treatment that mean contacting the team the same day is in "Getting through five or six weeks of pelvic radiotherapy"; that article also explains why letting the whole course stretch out loses local control — and brachytherapy sits at the end of the course, exactly where the grip must not loosen.

Three questions you can have ready before the next visit

On the Taiwan side, one reassuring thing first: brachytherapy has explicitly listed payment items in the item file of the NHI fee schedule, including insertion of the brachytherapy applicator and remote-afterloading brachytherapy[13]; the detailed reimbursement conditions and costs are for the hospital's medical affairs office or your case manager to confirm. In passing: when SBRT was brought into the NHI, the announced scope was single primary early lesions of the lung and of the liver and biliary tract[14] — "replacing brachytherapy with SBRT" has no evidence behind it, and in Taiwan it is not within that scope either. Today you can write down three questions and ask them out loud at the next visit: How many brachytherapy sessions am I expected to have, and when do they start? Which anaesthesia will the insertion use, and who do I turn to when it hurts? While the applicator is in place, where will I be, and roughly for how long? Once those three are answered, the "scariest name" part is usually over — what remains is the team walking those few days through with you.

From the physician

Hospitals differ widely in the number of brachytherapy sessions, the anaesthesia and the day's routine; what I have written is the general shape. At your next visit, ask directly: how many sessions am I expected to have, when do they start, and who do I turn to when it hurts? Once those three are answered, the fear is usually half its size.
