HYPERTHERMIA: HOW IT WORKS

Heat helps chemotherapy too, by another route

With radiotherapy the mechanism is repair and hypoxia; with chemotherapy it is how much drug gets in. Three quite different things share one Chinese name.

Next-Generation Therapy Guide · Hyperthermia: how it works · 6 references
#Hyperthermia#Evidence

This article handles a confusion that is common in clinic: heated intraperitoneal chemotherapy, heated bladder instillation, and external heating alongside systemic chemotherapy are three different things. Get them mixed up and you will imagine one treatment's risks while consenting to another.

Warning 1 (what this displaces): Most of the treatments in this topic are paid out of pocket, and most sit outside standard treatment. Before you decide, you need to know that what they displace is usually not time but three things of real value: standard treatment you have not yet used up, money you will need for later lines, and your eligibility for clinical trials (many trials exclude people who have recently received other experimental treatments). And there is one more, harder to see — physical reserve. The strength burnt through in a course that does not work may no longer be there when a genuinely useful option appears. So the question is not whether this is worth trying — it is what you give up by doing it.

Chemotherapy with hyperthermia has a tighter schedule: the heating has to fall within a few hours of the drug. The extra trips are not ones whose timing you get to choose.

Warning 2 (finding the right doctor): Do not make this kind of decision alone, and do not make it on the word of the person offering the treatment. The doctor you are looking for is one willing to tell you "this is not right for you" — if they never once say who this is NOT for, that is not a consultation, it is a sale.

My position first: proton therapy and hyperthermia are both technologies within my own field, and the hospital I work at has the equipment for them. So I draw every line in this topic tighter, not looser.

The other half of the hyperthermia story is chemotherapy. In Chinese that half is compressed into three characters — “thermochemotherapy” — and those three characters cover at least three different things.

What heating changes is how much drug gets in

Unlike the radiotherapy line, heat's effect on chemotherapy lies mainly not in repair but in the drug itself. With cisplatin, heating increases the amount of drug entering the cell and the number of drug-DNA adducts formed, and the order in which heating and drug administration are given affects the resulting cytotoxicity[1]. Add to that the change heating makes to perfusion in the tumour region[2], and the conditions of drug delivery change with it. Overall, hyperthermia is classified both as a radiosensitiser and as a chemosensitiser[3].

Three things that all get called thermochemotherapy

  • External heating added to the systemic chemotherapy you are already having: the drug goes in as usual, and a machine heats the tumour region from outside. This is the version that appears most often in the cancer-site articles later, and it is the version my department does.

  • Intracavitary heated instillation: warmed chemotherapy is instilled directly into a body cavity. The abdominal version usually follows surgery; the bladder version instils the drug into the bladder and circulates it warm. The bladder cancer article, the fourteenth, is where you will meet randomised trials of this kind — some of the newer evidence in this whole set.

  • Regional perfusion: the blood supply of a limb or organ is temporarily isolated and perfused with warmed, high-concentration drug. This is invasive at the level of surgery.

The complication risks of the three differ by orders of magnitude. Using the safety record of one to imagine another is the commonest misreading on this subject, and the one most likely to cause harm.

The timing window is tighter than you would expect

Because the mechanism involves drug concentration and perfusion, the timing of the heating usually has to sit tight against the drug — the common clinical practice is to complete the heating within a few hours either side of administration. That means if your chemotherapy and your hyperthermia happen in different units, on different floors, or even in different hospitals, the scheduling itself is something to settle first. The order is not arbitrary either: change the sequence and the result may change[1].

Why intracavitary instillation is a different category of risk

External heating delivers energy from outside the body; its main adverse effects are pain in the heated region, pain and induration in the subcutaneous fat, blisters, and the postural burden of lying still for an hour. Instillation delivers warmed fluid directly into a cavity or organ, needs catheters and a circulation system, and its risks belong to another class altogether. Even the heating technology differs — getting heat into a cavity and pushing it through several centimetres of tissue from outside are two different engineering problems[4]. The clinical evidence for bladder instillation is in fact among the newer and better-founded parts of this set, and the fourteenth article handles it separately — but its conclusions cannot be lent to external heating, and the reverse does not hold either.

Thermal dose matters just as much on this line

Whether it accompanies radiotherapy or chemotherapy, “how hot and for how long” has to be converted into the same unit before anything can be compared, and that conversion has been in use since 1984[5]. So studies of chemotherapy with hyperthermia should also be read for whether they recorded thermal dose. A study that did not record it is not fraudulent; it is simply that the “hyperthermia was given” it reports and the “hyperthermia was given” another study reports may well not be the same thing. Article six sets out that unit properly.

Why the frequency is also once or twice a week

The limit on frequency is exactly the same as on the radiotherapy line, for the reason given in article four: once tissue has been heated it is less sensitive to heat for a period afterwards[6]. So chemotherapy with hyperthermia is not scheduled daily either; it fits the chemotherapy cycle at once or twice a week. If someone offers you a denser schedule, that is not what the existing studies did.

The strength of evidence is not the same as on the radiotherapy line

Better to say this up front than to have you disappointed later. There is far more randomised evidence for hyperthermia added to radiotherapy than for hyperthermia added to chemotherapy. Apart from bladder instillation, the clinical data for chemotherapy with hyperthermia are mostly single-arm, retrospective or small series. The sixteenth article lists several cancer sites where it has been tried but the evidence will not carry a conclusion; the seventeenth and eighteenth handle the more frontier combinations with immunotherapy and cell therapy — the evidence there is weaker still, and I write those two more conservatively.

So if I am already on chemotherapy, should I add it

This article does not answer that, because the answer depends entirely on cancer site and situation, which is the business of articles eleven to sixteen. But two things can be settled now. First, heating does not make chemotherapy side effects disappear, and no trial has used heating to reduce the chemotherapy dose — if someone suggests that, it is not something these studies did. Second, the cost you pay is concrete: one or two extra trips a week, over an hour each time, discomfort during it, and in most cases paid out of pocket. Those costs belong in the decision; they cannot be counted as zero.

The schedule is itself a checkpoint. Where in the drug cycle the heating is placed, and who is responsible for confirming it — whether there is a specific answer to that tells you more about how this treatment is regarded here than any efficacy summary will.

One more test. If the schedule for chemotherapy with hyperthermia is described as “very flexible, whichever day suits you”, be careful — because mechanistically it is not flexible. The order and interval between drug and heat affect the result, and that is visible even at the cellular level[1]. A team that takes the timing window seriously will have a rigid schedule; a place that says any time is fine may not be managing it as part of the treatment at all. That observation is useful for judging whether somewhere is delivering a treatment or selling sessions.

Facts checked: September 2026.

From the physician

If your team proposes “thermochemotherapy”, ask which one: instilled into a body cavity, or external heating added to the chemotherapy you are already having. The invasiveness, the complications and the indications are entirely different, and so is what the consent form actually covers.
