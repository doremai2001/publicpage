Steroids: the drug that saves you, and has to come down

What it repairs is the leaking blood vessels, not the tumour. The side effects are written out item by item, along with why the taper has to be slow and which two things coming down too fast runs into.

Glioblastoma Guide · During Treatment · 12 references
#Steroids#Side Effects#Daily Life

Only one group of side effects has been confirmed in a randomised trial to rise with both dose and time: the moon face and the ankle oedema — which is also the first thing a patient sees for himself. Every mg figure in this article is a study's grouping, not anybody's prescription.

Two or three days in, the headache has gone, the walking is steady again, and the family says "he is completely back". The same week another family asked me: if it works this well, can he not take a bit more of it, for a bit longer? This article answers that question — why this drug rescues an emergency, and why from the first day it is taken somebody is already thinking about how to get it back down.

What it repairs is the leaking blood vessels, not the tumour

What glioblastoma produces is vasogenic oedema: the tumour secretes factors such as VEGF, the new vessels it grows are described as "chaotic, tortuous, hyperpermeable and dysfunctional", the tight junction proteins that seal the gaps in the blood–brain barrier (occludin, claudin) are expressed less, and water seeps into brain tissue[1]. What a glucocorticoid does is seal those gaps again: in animal brain tumour models, dexamethasone reduces blood–brain barrier permeability by upregulating occludin[1]. Dexamethasone is chosen over other steroids because it has the lowest mineralocorticoid activity, a long half-life and high potency[1].

How fast does it work? The review's own sentence is that "symptomatic improvement is usually seen within 24 to 72 hours"[2]. So hold on to this one: the improvement of these few days is water being squeezed out, not the tumour getting smaller. How the tumour itself gets treated is the business of the operation, the radiotherapy and the chemotherapy.

The guideline is blunt: do not give it for oedema with no symptoms, and come down as soon as you can

The 2021 guideline on diffuse gliomas of adulthood from the European Association of Neuro-Oncology puts steroids in the section of discouraged treatments: they should not be given to treat oedema that is asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic, and they should be tapered as soon as possible, because the safety profile of long-term use is not a good one[3]. In the preoperative paragraph the same guideline uses "may be given", not "should be given", and adds a caveat: not where primary central nervous system lymphoma or an inflammatory lesion is suspected (why the drug is started early in the first place is in "The first few days after they find it")[3].

A clinical practice guideline for high-grade glioma from Alberta, Canada, puts the price into a single sentence: the side effects of dexamethasone are very common, and they occur more often and more severely as the dose goes up and the course runs longer; patients should be monitored for endocrine, muscular, skeletal, gastrointestinal, psychiatric and haematological complications[4]. Every mg figure that appears in this article is a group that patients were sorted into inside a study. It is not a dose or a schedule for you; your dose is decided by your doctor from your symptoms, and you must not raise it or lower it yourself.

When the face starts to round out, that is the moment to ask when it can start coming down

In the randomised trial done in patients with brain metastases (89 people, three daily doses), most of the side effects showed no statistical difference between the three arms: blood sugar, infection, proximal weakness — none of them. Only one group reached statistical significance: the cushingoid face and ankle oedema, increasing as the treatment ran longer and the dose went higher (P=0.02 at day 7, P=0.03 at day 28)[2].

It is the one item a randomised trial has confirmed to rise with dose and with time, and it is also the first thing the patient sees for himself. When the face starts to round out, that is when "when can I start coming down" goes on the list of questions for clinic — it is not the moment to quietly take one tablet fewer.

The weakness that stops you standing up is not the weakness the tumour causes

Steroid myopathy affects the proximal muscles: getting up out of a chair becomes hard work, the stairs need a handrail, the arms will not hold up long enough to comb the hair; while sensation and grip in the hands and feet do not drop away down one whole side the way they do in a stroke. In that 1982 retrospective cohort of 97 patients with brain metastases, myopathy was 4.5% (4/89) in the group taking 12 mg a day or more, and 0% in the group on 8 mg or less[2].

That trial also holds an observation that is rarely quoted: patients who developed proximal weakness, a cushingoid face or ankle oedema had, if anything, a smaller mean improvement on the performance scale; the authors' reading is that the higher dose was more effective, but the side effects cancelled the net benefit out[2]. This is the item that most needs telling apart from the tumour getting worse, and at home it cannot be told apart — which is why it has to be said out loud, not watched from home.

The real amplifier for an ulcer is a steroid plus an anti-inflammatory painkiller

A nested case-control study (1,415 patients with peptic ulcer disease against 7,063 controls) worked out a relative risk of 2.0 for current oral steroid users (95% confidence interval 1.3 to 3.0) — a relative risk of 2.0 means the probability of it happening is about twice as high; the bracket is the plausible range for the estimate, not your personal probability. But take it apart: people also taking a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory painkiller (NSAID) had a relative risk of 4.4 (2.0 to 9.7), and people not taking one only 1.1 (0.5 to 2.1); for those taking both together the risk was fifteen times that of taking neither[5]. These are 1991 data in a non-brain-tumour population; the direction is still clear.

Brain tumour patients are precisely the group who reach for a painkiller the moment the head hurts. As for whether a stomach drug should be added alongside, I could not find a guideline I could cite saying "everyone needs one"; rather than asking about a stomach drug, ask this first: which painkiller can I take when my head hurts? Black stools, vomiting something coffee-coloured, persistent severe pain in the upper abdomen are same-day contact with the team; the detail is in "When to come back the same day: three tiers".

Not sleeping, a temper that has changed, blood sugar going up

Insomnia has data behind it: in a prospective study of 68 patients receiving palliative whole-brain radiotherapy, the group taking 16 mg a day or more had "more difficulty falling asleep" (P=0.009)[2]. The mechanism behind the blood sugar is that glucocorticoids increase gluconeogenesis in the liver, reduce insulin sensitivity and damage the pancreatic β cells, with the oral route carrying the highest risk; older age, a higher BMI and a family history of diabetes raise it further[6]. In that same cohort of 97 people, hyperglycaemia was 6.7% (6/89) in the group on 12 mg a day or more, and 0% in the group on 8 mg or less[2].

On mood, the family's phrase is usually "he has become a different person". In a systematic review and meta-analysis covering 73 studies, people on steroids had higher depressive symptom scores (standardised mean difference 0.92 — the gap converted onto a single ruler across different scales); and in the acute phase, within eight weeks of starting the drug, manic symptoms were more common than depressive ones (odds ratio 2.42, from 6 studies and 1,086 people), psychotic symptoms about 2.4%, and anxiety showed no significant association; the authors say themselves that the certainty of the evidence across these studies is overall low to very low[7]. An odds ratio is not a multiple of the probability. Even so, irritability, talking too much and not sleeping in the first few weeks are worth reporting as a possible reaction to the drug, rather than being taken as his character having changed.

What only comes with time: infection, and a few pieces of observational data

The infection line is tied to the dose: when the 2018 guideline from the American Society of Clinical Oncology and the Infectious Diseases Society of America discusses prophylaxis against pneumocystis pneumonia, the example it gives is "the equivalent of 20 mg of prednisone or more daily for longer than a month"[8]. It shows itself as breathlessness, a dry cough, getting winded after a few steps, and it can progress to respiratory failure; in immunosuppressed patients who do not have HIV the course is more abrupt[9].

There is one more set of data to lay out, and please read it together with its limitations. The conclusion the 2016 Brain paper draws for itself is this: in retrospective analyses of three independent cohorts, steroid use during radiotherapy was an independent marker of shorter survival; the authors' other sentence uses the word "may"[10]. On the immunotherapy side there is a separate retrospective analysis of 181 IDH-wildtype patients, in which dexamethasone at baseline was the strongest adjusted predictor of poor survival (hazard ratio 2.16 in the under-2 mg group, 1.97 in the 2 mg-and-over group; a hazard ratio divides the speed at which deaths occur in two groups, and above 1 means they happened faster)[11]. All of this is observational data: people taking more steroid were the ones whose oedema was worse and whose condition was worse to begin with; and when even an illogical direction turns up — the under-2 mg group having the higher hazard ratio — you can see it is not a curve of dose against effect. So the correct reading is "come down when it is time to come down", not "taking less will let you live longer"; the survival question is written in "That number, and how I am going to tell you about it".

No tapering schedule was calculated for you

Coming down too fast runs into two things, and both of them look like the tumour turning worse again. The first is adrenal insufficiency: a meta-analysis of 74 papers and 3,753 adults, all tested by ACTH stimulation, shows the proportion rising with dose (2.4% on low doses, 21.5% on high) and with the length of the course (1.4% in an asthma population treated for under 28 days, 27.4% for over a year); the authors' closing sentence is that no form of administration, dose, length of course or underlying disease can rule adrenal insufficiency out with certainty[12]. It looks like no strength, nausea, dizziness, vision going black on standing up, low blood pressure. The second is rebound symptoms: the oedema that was being held down comes back, and the headache, the vomiting and the weakness that had improved return with it; in that trial, 3 patients had to have the dose put back up because of neurological deterioration[2]. These two point the opposite way from the tumour getting worse, and the cost of guessing wrong is asymmetric.

How slowly should it come down? The guidelines do not agree with each other: the same systematic review compared the practice guidelines and found starting doses from 4 mg to 24 mg a day and tapering schedules from three days to two weeks, and noted that some of these guidelines are citing one another; the evidence base of one of them is annotated as having "not a single peer-reviewed primary study that enrolled glioma patients"[2]. What you can do is this: do not stop it yourself, do not add to it yourself, and report every symptom that appears after a reduction. At the next appointment, get this sentence out: given my symptoms now, when are you planning to start bringing it down, and which three signs should I be watching for once we do?

From the physician

This drug repairs the oedema, not the tumour, so the benefit and the price go up together. When the face in the mirror starts to round out, that is when "when can I start coming down" goes onto your clinic question list — do not stop it yourself, do not add to it yourself. No strength, nausea, dizziness or vision going black on standing after a reduction is a same-day report; and ask your doctor before you take a painkiller for a headache.
