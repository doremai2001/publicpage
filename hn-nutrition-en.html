When a feeding tube is the right call

Half of head and neck patients lose over 5% of body weight during chemoradiation. When a tube earns its place, and why the evidence for early gastrostomy is weaker than you think.

Head & Neck Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 13 references
#Swallowing#Nutrition#Side Effects#Daily Life

A tube is not a verdict on how well you are coping; it is equipment, and equipment gets chosen on evidence. This page covers when it earns its place, when it can wait, and the swallowing you should keep doing while it is in.

"Doctor, can I get through this without a tube?" That question carries about as much weight in my clinic as "am I going to be all right". A nasogastric tube or a gastrostomy reads, to many people, as "I could not cope". I want to take that equals sign apart first: a tube is a tool that stops your nutrition collapsing and your course being interrupted in the weeks when swallowing is hardest. And because it is a tool, when to use it and whether to place it early are questions with their own evidence — evidence considerably weaker than what you will read online.

Start with the number: half of patients lose more than 5% of their body weight

In 910 patients receiving curative radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy for head and neck cancer, mean weight loss during the course was 5.1 ± 4.9%, and 50% reached what the researchers call critical weight loss — more than 5% during treatment[1]. The risk factors from the same study: nodal irradiation, a higher dose to the primary, 3D conformal radiotherapy rather than IMRT, and younger age.

So how many kilos before a tube? The answer is not a weight

5% and 10% are definitions in the research literature, not thresholds for action. 5% is the definition of critical weight loss[1]; 10% is the line the gastrostomy studies use for malnutrition. The triggers actually written into guidelines look at how much is going in, and for how long.

The UK national multidisciplinary guideline on nutritional management in head and neck cancer is specific[2]: screen nutrition at diagnosis and again at every stage of treatment; during radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy aim for at least 30 kcal per kilogram of body weight per day and at least 1.2 g of protein per kilogram; and every patient having radiotherapy should have weekly dietitian contact, not a referral once the weight has already gone.

The trigger conditions are listed below as written, but one principle carries most of it: what counts is intake and duration, not the reading on the scale.

Begin nutritional therapy if you are already undernourished, or if you are expected to be unable to eat for more than 7 days. Begin tube feeding if intake is expected to be under 60% of your energy needs for more than 10 days. Consider a gastrostomy only if tube feeding is expected to be needed for more than 4 weeks. Continue nutritional intervention for up to 3 months after treatment ends. The European ESPEN practical guideline on clinical nutrition in cancer takes the same flow-chart form, stringing 43 recommendations into a decision path[3].

"Should we put the gastrostomy in first?" The answer is weaker than you expect

A prophylactic PEG — placed before the course begins — is a popular request, and I understand why. Nobody wants to be wheeled off for an invasive procedure in their worst week. But I have to be honest: the evidence does not sit on the side of "everyone should have one in advance".

The Cochrane review of enteral feeding methods during radiotherapy and chemotherapy for head and neck cancer concluded that there is not sufficient evidence to determine the optimal method of enteral feeding. Only one randomised trial met the criteria, 33 patients, PEG against a nasogastric tube: less weight loss with PEG at 6 weeks, a difference gone by 6 months; no difference in complications or satisfaction; PEG used for longer and costing substantially more[4]. More important still, that review found not a single study comparing prophylactic placement with placing a tube when it becomes necessary.

What can be discussed is a systematic review of 22 studies — observational data, not randomised: prophylactic gastrostomy did reduce the number of patients who became malnourished (more than 10% body-weight loss), but average weight loss at the various time points was similar to reactive placement; it was associated with better quality of life at 6 months, and also with greater long-term gastrostomy dependence[5]. The risk factors for malnutrition without a prophylactic tube were advanced age, weight already lost before treatment, and radiation dose to the pharyngeal constrictors. The authors' own strategy is a prophylactic tube for the highest-risk patients and a reactive tube for everyone else.

Both things are true at once: place it early and fewer people starve into malnutrition; place it early and more people cannot get rid of it. Which makes this an individualised decision to take with your team — age, how much you have already lost, tumour site, dose to the swallowing muscles — not a rule that earlier is safer.

Use it or lose it: if you can swallow safely, keep swallowing

A study of 497 patients with pharyngeal cancer recorded this at the end of radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy: 26% had no oral intake at all, 74% kept some or all of their intake by mouth, and 58% did adhere to their swallowing exercises. Maintaining oral intake and adhering to the exercises were each independently associated with a better long-term diet and a shorter period of gastrostomy dependence. In the authors' words: patients who either eat or exercise fare better than those who do neither; patients who both eat and exercise have the highest rate of return to a regular diet and the shortest duration of gastrostomy dependence[6].

A randomised trial called "Pharyngocise", with 58 patients, pointed the same way: the arm doing active exercises during chemoradiotherapy showed less structural deterioration of the tongue musculature on MRI and less deterioration of swallowing function[7].

The mechanism makes sense and the signal is consistent. Now the other half: a Cochrane review of 6 studies and 326 patients with advanced head and neck cancer found the risk of bias generally high, graded the overall quality of evidence "very low", and found no evidence that therapeutic exercises were better than usual care — not for the safety of oral swallowing, not for its efficiency, not for any secondary outcome[8]. The same review notes no adverse events attributable to the exercises.

So here is how I put it. Swallowing exercises are safe, they cost nothing, the observational data point one way consistently, and they are well worth doing. They have not been proven to work in high-quality trials, so I will not promise you that practising means nothing will go wrong.

"Nil by mouth to rest the throat" is not safer — but it is not your call either

The data run the other way: a swallow that goes unused long enough deteriorates[6], and long-term tube dependence is itself a harm[5]. "The tube is in, so I have stopped eating" is a poor default.

There is an absolute condition attached: only if it is safe. The swallowing rehabilitation handout from the Department of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation at Taipei Veterans General Hospital lists the indications for tube feeding plainly: a surgical wound that has not healed, calorie intake that stays inadequate despite real effort, silent aspiration, or recurrent aspiration pneumonia[9]. Silent aspiration means what it says — material goes into the airway and you do not cough, and you feel nothing. That is precisely why "can I eat" is not settled by how you or your family feel about it, but by a speech pathologist and a physician after assessment.

If the speech pathologist says you are not currently safe to eat by mouth, please do not quietly try anyway. Aspiration pneumonia can bring down the whole treatment plan. The other way round: if the assessment says a particular texture is safe, eat that texture, and treat the tube as a way of topping up calories rather than replacing eating. Chang Gung's handout works from the same principle — a nasogastric tube temporarily, when pain in the mouth or throat makes intake inadequate along with clear weight loss, or when choking is frequent, with the aim of holding your pre-treatment weight[10].

Feeding or swallowing: any of these, phone the same day

  • Fever, coughing or breathlessness after eating or drinking — this can be aspiration pneumonia.

  • Choking on every mouthful, or a wet, gurgly voice after you swallow.

  • A whole day in which you cannot get even water down, or dizziness, near-fainting, very little urine.

  • Losing more than about a kilogram in a week.

  • The tube site red, painful or leaking, or the tube loose or displaced.

  • Vomiting after a feed.

And one more: any nutritional supplement, home blenderised feed or Chinese herbal medicine should be cleared with the dietitian and the physician before it goes down a tube.

How this actually works in Taiwan

Swallowing therapy at Taiwanese medical centres is delivered by speech-language pathologists (語言治療師), usually inside a multidisciplinary team led by rehabilitation medicine. The team at Taipei Veterans General Hospital includes rehabilitation physicians, swallowing and speech therapists, dietitians and physical and occupational therapists, with the home exercise programme set at 2 to 3 sessions a day[9]; the head and neck cancer team at Shuang Ho Hospital emphasises early intervention, with a speech-language pathologist giving individual instruction on safe swallowing technique, positioning and food texture[11]. If your course has started and nobody has raised swallowing rehabilitation, ask for the referral yourself.

Gastrostomy is routine here. The gastroenterology and hepatology division at Tri-Service General Hospital lists long-term inability to swallow from a head and neck tumour as one indication, and sets out the advantages over a nasogastric tube: local anaesthesia only, about 25 to 30 minutes, a stoma of roughly 0.8 cm that hides under clothing, and avoiding the sinusitis and nasal erosion a nasogastric tube can cause[12].

On money: I could not verify from any official National Health Insurance (健保) Administration document whether gastrostomy placement, nasogastric tube care, or swallowing and speech therapy sessions are covered — ask your case manager (個管師), or the hospital's medical affairs or billing office, who can look up what you will actually be charged. To check yourself, the NHI Administration publishes the medical service benefit and reimbursement schedule[13].

Ask these while the weight is still on you

First: given my tumour site and dose, am I in the high-risk group that would justify a prophylactic gastrostomy? Ask for the reasoning, not just a yes or a no.

Second: when will I see the dietitian, on a fixed schedule? If the answer today is "referral if needed", ask whether it can be weekly for the duration of the course.

Third: what texture can I eat right now, and when should my swallow be reassessed? Write the answer down and stick it on the fridge — in the hardest fortnight of the course, memory is the first thing to go.

From the physician

These numbers are population averages. Whether you need a tube depends on where the tumour sits, on the dose reaching the swallowing muscles, and on how much you are actually managing to eat this week. The most important line: when the speech therapist says nothing by mouth, do not quietly try anyway, because silent aspiration does not make you cough.
