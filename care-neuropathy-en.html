THE BODY DURING TREATMENT

Numb hands and feet: when to speak up

Say it before the next cycle: dose adjustment is the one thing guidelines are sure of, and it is the doctor's to do.

Patient Education · The Body During Treatment · 20 references
#Side Effects#Chemotherapy#Quality of Life

Numbness does not send anyone to an emergency department, so it gets carried to the last cycle in silence. The prevention shelf is emptier than it looks, and the thing that works has a deadline.

Numb hands and feet is what I most often hear "I thought I could put up with it" about. It does not force you to an emergency department the way a fever does, and it is not visible the way hair loss is, so people carry it to the last cycle before saying anything. One point here: the earlier you say it, the better the chance of being left with less. The one intervention the guidelines are certain about can be done by a doctor and nobody else, and it works better the earlier it happens.

Will it get better? There is a curve, but nobody can work out yours

The order of magnitude first. A meta-analysis of 31 studies and 4,179 patients found peripheral neuropathy in 68.1% at one month after chemotherapy, 60.0% at three months, 30.0% at six months or beyond[1]. Meaning: most people recover part of it, and not everyone recovers all of it.

By drug it is more precise, and these curves are not interchangeable:

  • Adjuvant oxaliplatin in colorectal cancer: a systematic review of 27 studies gives any-grade symptoms in 58% at six months, 45% at one year, 32% at two years, 24% at three years, falling roughly 26% a year[2].

  • Taxanes in breast cancer: among 296 postmenopausal breast cancer survivors, 58.4% still had numbness or tingling — 30.7% mild, 27.7% moderate to severe[3].

  • Intravenous bortezomib, common in myeloma and lymphoma: 34 trials and 6,492 people pooled give any grade 33.9%, high grade 8.1%[4].

Worth flagging: these studies used ten different scales to measure the same thing[2], and all are population averages. Nobody can work out before treatment where you personally will land. So I will not say "it will definitely get better", and I will not say "it never will".

Why saying it early is the most useful sentence in this piece

ASCO's 2020 guideline on chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN) states one recommendation plainly: when a patient develops intolerable neuropathy or functional impairment, the clinician should assess whether to delay, reduce, substitute or stop the drug[5]. It is the most certain thing in the guideline, and the doctor carries it out, not you.

In practice it happens more often than people expect. One hospital reviewed 123 early breast cancer patients on taxanes: 40% had a dose reduction for some reason, 17% because of neuropathy; diabetic patients were at higher risk (p=0.01)[6]. So if you already have diabetes or neuropathy, say so before chemotherapy starts, not once symptoms appear.

The signals are concrete: you cannot do up a shirt button, cannot hold a cup steadily, cannot feel the floor, or the numbness keeps you awake. When those appear, say so before the next cycle. What I least want to see is the other response — it gets unbearable, so you decide alone not to go this time. The total length of a course is part of whether it works, and skipping alone costs twice: the treatment is discounted, and the dose that should have been adjusted is not. On the day you cannot go on, ring, and hand the decision back to the team.

The prevention evidence is much weaker than what you have heard

ASCO's 2014 guideline already said no agents are recommended for preventing CIPN; the 2020 update reconfirmed it and added one: the use of acetyl-L-carnitine should be discouraged[7][5]. Worth remembering as a worked example of a supplement making things worse: SWOG S0715 was a 24-week randomised double-blind trial giving acetyl-L-carnitine during adjuvant taxane treatment[8]; a later meta-analysis pooling three trials found no preventive effect at 12 weeks and, at 24 weeks, an increase[9].

Calcium and magnesium infusions are the same story told to the end. A trial stopped early, 102 patients analysed, saw benefit in 2011[10]; three years later a phase III randomised trial in 353 patients (N08CB) closed it: no difference between arms, and the study does not support calcium and magnesium for protecting nerves[11].

Oral glutamine has a Taiwanese appraisal of its own: three randomised trials plus two quasi-experimental studies, a difference in the incidence of pain (relative risk 0.26) but none in neuropathy grading, numbness or muscle weakness; adding in the self-paid cost of roughly NT$500 a day, the authors conclude against routine use[12]. This site has a piece called "Should I take L-glutamine?" — the detail is there.

Frozen gloves and compression gloves: worth a try or not?

The commonest self-help in a chemotherapy suite, and its evidence has to be told straight. Methodologically the most complete is Cropsi, which randomised 200 patients to cryotherapy or cryocompression: grade 1 in 30.1% against 32.8%, grade 2 or above in 13.7% against 17.2% — no difference between arms, and the trial had no "do nothing" control arm, so it cannot prove either works[13]. The two positive results both used the patient's own other side as control: a randomised self-controlled trial in 91 gynaecological cancer patients gave an odds ratio for sensory neuropathy of 0.54 (0.31–0.94, P=.03; an odds ratio divides one side's odds of the event happening against not happening by the other's, and below 1 means less likely, though not "a set percentage less"), but monofilament testing showed no difference[14]; ELEGANT gave 94 breast cancer patients two surgical gloves on one hand and a frozen glove on the other; its primary endpoint was in fact satisfaction (9 points against 6), grade 2 or above neuropathy 18% against 28% being a secondary outcome — and 22% still needed a dose reduction[15]. Taiwan's own systematic review of 11 studies concludes it is promising but the evidence insufficient[16].

So my line: wanting to try it is not a bad idea, and the main side effect is being uncomfortably cold — but it is not a guideline recommendation. If you try it, ask your chemotherapy nurse how to do it without a cold injury, and do not bring your own ice packs to the chair to add to it.

If it already hurts, the guideline recommends one drug

ASCO 2020 puts it this way: duloxetine is the agent with appropriate evidence to support its use in established painful neuropathy — and the same sentence continues that the benefit is limited[5]. The randomised crossover trial behind it enrolled 231 people: average pain fell 1.06 points against 0.34 on placebo (P=.003), an actual gap between arms of 0.73 points on a 0–10 scale, any improvement in 59% against 38%[17]. Less than a point on average — and this is the best-evidenced option we have.

Taiwan has a layer to know first. In section 1 of the National Health Insurance (健保) drug reimbursement regulations, the neuropathic pain indications listed for duloxetine are diabetic peripheral neuropathy (requiring a neurologist's diagnosis or nerve conduction confirmation) and fibromyalgia; for gabapentin, post-herpetic neuralgia; for pregabalin, post-herpetic neuralgia, fibromyalgia, diabetic peripheral neuropathy and neuropathic pain after spinal cord injury[18]. CIPN is not in those entries. That is nobody's fault, and it does not mean nothing can be done — it means this is worth asking out loud in clinic: in my situation, is there a drug worth considering, and if so, is it self-paid or is there another route?

Falling is the price actually paid

Numbness is unpleasant; falling is the part that changes a life. In those 296 breast cancer survivors, falls occurred in 23.8% of those without neuropathy, 31.9% with mild, 41.5% with moderate to severe (P=0.028)[3]. Things to do when you get home today: a small light on the route from bed to bathroom, a non-slip mat in the bathroom, a stair rail, no slippers on a wet floor, cables and rug edges tidied away.

Exercise has preliminary evidence: a meta-analysis of 15 randomised trials and 1,124 people found better total symptom scores in the exercise groups (standardised mean difference −0.62; more negative means a larger gap, and 0.6 counts as moderate)[19], but the trials differed widely, and the 2019 American College of Sports Medicine consensus puts peripheral neuropathy in the "effect still uncertain" box[20]. How to move when your footing is unreliable, and when to stop, is in "Move, or rest, during treatment".

Which numbness is not chemotherapy's numbness

Chemotherapy neuropathy is typically symmetrical, starting in the fingers and toes and creeping upwards. Anything not of that shape means contacting the team the same day, not filing it under chemotherapy: sudden weakness, symptoms on one side only, a band-like ring of numbness around the trunk, a change in bowel or bladder control, numbness spreading upwards quickly over a few days. On an immune checkpoint inhibitor, do not judge it yourself at all — report a new neurological symptom rather than treating it as a cold or a trapped nerve.

Three questions for your next appointment: which of my drugs can cause neuropathy? At this level, is it enough to consider a dose adjustment? If it worsens between cycles, which number do I ring, and can it wait until the next visit? Keep the answers on your phone — numbness changes slowly and memory lies about it.

From the physician

Which drug is causing it, and whether your level warrants a dose adjustment, can only be judged against your own regimen and history — so bring the specific losses (buttons, cups, footing) rather than a word like “numb”. If a drug for the pain is worth considering in your case, ask in clinic what the route is. And any numbness that is one-sided, band-like or fast-moving is a same-day call.
