HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

What this test is actually trying to prove

It asks whether adding chemotherapy would bring any further benefit — sometimes the answer is that it would not, sometimes it is not; it applies only to people who are hormone-receptor-positive and HER2-negative, and the same score means different things under different ovarian function.

Breast Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 14 references
#Biomarkers#Chemotherapy#Decisions#NHI Coverage

RxPONDER's 2026 AMH analysis rewrites "premenopausal women get a chemotherapy benefit" into "the ones with normal ovarian reserve do". And in Taiwan, the road of using ovarian suppression in place of chemotherapy has a different shape in the provisions from the one it has in the trials.

"I had that genetic test — so I don't need chemotherapy, right?" There is a very dangerous equals sign inside that sentence. This article is about the first of the three genetic-testing lines: taking tumour tissue and working out whether adding chemotherapy would bring any further benefit. Sometimes the answer is that it would not, and sometimes it is not that at all. What question each of the three lines answers is set out in "Which lines of the report actually change treatment".

First, check whether this test was designed for you at all

ASCO's 2022 biomarker guideline puts it bluntly: not one of these multigene assays is recommended for treatment decisions in HER2-positive or triple-negative breast cancer[1]. If your report says HER2-positive, or if ER, PR and HER2 are all negative, then none of the numbers below are yours, and your route is in "Three breast cancers, three different roads".

The guideline draws the lines finer than that: Oncotype DX, MammaPrint, Breast Cancer Index and EndoPredict for postmenopausal women or those over 50 with early-stage ER-positive/HER2-negative disease that is node-negative or has 1 to 3 positive nodes; Prosigna and Breast Cancer Index restricted to postmenopausal, node-negative women; and among premenopausal women, only Oncotype DX, and only if node-negative. The guideline also states in writing that premenopausal women with 1 to 3 positive nodes benefit from chemotherapy whatever their score, and that there are no data at all on using a genomic assay to guide chemotherapy in women with 4 or more positive nodes[1].

Node-negative: TAILORx's three score bands, and the age split buried inside them

TAILORx enrolled 10,273 women with hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative, axillary node-negative disease and divided the 21-gene recurrence score of 0 to 100 into three bands, 0–10, 11–25 and 26–100[2]. The 1,626 women in the 0–10 band (15.9% of the total) had endocrine therapy alone, and 99.3% were free of distant recurrence at 5 years[3]. The 6,711 in the middle 11–25 band were randomised, and the result was that endocrine therapy alone reached "non-inferiority" against chemotherapy plus endocrine therapy. That phrase is the one most easily misread, so let me open it up first: a non-inferiority trial is not asking whether having less chemotherapy is better, nor whether the two sides are equally good; it is asking whether the amount by which the lesser treatment falls behind exceeds a line agreed in advance. TAILORx's answer is that it did not: the hazard ratio for invasive disease-free survival was 1.08 (95% confidence interval 0.94 to 1.24, P=0.26), and at 9 years invasive disease-free survival was 83.3% against 84.3% and overall survival 93.9% against 93.8%[2]. A hazard ratio divides the speed at which things go wrong in one group by the speed in the other, and 1.08 means things went wrong slightly faster on the no-chemotherapy side; an interval running from 0.94 to 1.24 has room inside it for both "a bit better" and "a bit worse", so this is a failure to see a difference, not proof that there is none.

Put age into it and the same score changes meaning. The chemotherapy benefit for invasive disease-free survival varied with score multiplied by age, interaction P=0.004 — an interaction means the same treatment works differently in different groups; women aged 50 or under with scores of 16 to 25 did show a chemotherapy benefit[2]. Add in the clinical risk built from tumour size and grade (9,427 women), and among women aged 50 or under receiving endocrine therapy alone, still within the middle 11–25 score band, the 9-year distant recurrence rate was 4.7±1.0% for those at low clinical risk and 12.3±2.4% for those at high clinical risk[4]. The same "middle score" is a different situation in those two groups of people.

One to three positive nodes: RxPONDER split it twice

RxPONDER enrolled 5,083 women with hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative disease, 1 to 3 positive axillary nodes and a recurrence score no higher than 25; 33.2% were premenopausal. The chemotherapy benefit varied with menopausal status (interaction P=0.008), so it is reported separately: postmenopausal women had 5-year invasive disease-free survival of 91.9% (endocrine therapy alone) against 91.3% (chemotherapy plus endocrine therapy), hazard ratio 1.02 (0.82 to 1.26, P=0.89), with no chemotherapy benefit visible; premenopausal women had 89.0% against 93.9%, hazard ratio 0.60 (0.43 to 0.83, P=0.002). And within that premenopausal group, the relative benefit did not increase as the score rose[5].

A 2026 biomarker sub-analysis splits "premenopausal" once more. In pretreatment serum from 1,556 participants under 55, anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH, a marker of how many follicles are left in the ovary) had a significant interaction with chemotherapy benefit (adjusted P=0.0034): the 64% with an AMH of 10 pg/mL or above — normal ovarian reserve — had a chemotherapy benefit (invasive disease-free survival hazard ratio 0.46, 95% confidence interval 0.33 to 0.65); the 36% with an AMH below 10 did not (hazard ratio 1.27, 0.81 to 1.99). The authors conclude that AMH points to who will benefit from chemotherapy better than menopausal status or age does, and they note that most premenopausal participants in that trial did not receive ovarian function suppression[6]. The variable that is actually moving is ovarian function, not the age on your identity card.

MINDACT: the same split, and the authors offer their own explanation for it

MINDACT enrolled 6,693 women with early breast cancer, assessing clinical risk and 70-gene risk at the same time. The primary population was the 1,550 (23.2%) at high clinical risk and low genomic risk: those who had no chemotherapy had 5-year distant metastasis-free survival of 94.7%, above the pre-set non-inferiority boundary of 92%[7]. In the updated analysis at a median follow-up of 8.7 years, that group's 8-year distant metastasis-free survival was 92.0% with chemotherapy against 89.4% without (hazard ratio 0.66, 95% confidence interval 0.48 to 0.92)[8].

Within the hormone-receptor-positive/HER2-negative subgroup of 1,358, women aged 50 or under (464) had 8-year distant metastasis-free survival of 93.6% with chemotherapy against 88.6% without, a difference of 5.0 percentage points (95% confidence interval −0.5 to 10.4); women over 50 (894) had 90.2% against 90.0%, a difference of 0.2 percentage points (−4.0 to 4.4). The authors write themselves that this may come from chemotherapy-induced ovarian function suppression; and stratified by nodal status, the benefit did not grow larger with node positivity either[8]. These are exploratory, underpowered analyses, and every one of those confidence intervals crosses 0.

"So could ovarian suppression replace the chemotherapy?" — in Taiwan, read the provision first

Provision 5.5.1 of the National Health Insurance drug reimbursement regulations, covering GnRH drugs in premenopausal early breast cancer, reads as follows: used in combination with tamoxifen, as adjuvant therapy replacing chemotherapy after surgery, with a hormone receptor of 2+ or 3+, HER2 FISH-negative or IHC 1+, no more than 3 involved nodes, GnRH for 3 years and tamoxifen for 5 years, with prior review required and the reason the patient cannot have chemotherapy stated in the application[9]. And the strongest-performing combination in the SOFT and TEXT trials did not use tamoxifen; it used exemestane plus ovarian function suppression[10] — and that combination is outside the range the provision above permits[9]. What each of the three combinations buys and what it costs is in "Five years of endocrine therapy, or ten?".

So the sentence "you could have ovarian suppression instead of chemotherapy" is false reassurance in Taiwan: in the provision it is a road for people who cannot have chemotherapy, and the combination is pinned down as well. Whether to take that road, and how to ask about paying for it yourself, has to be confirmed with your own doctor and case manager.

A low score is not zero risk, and scores from different platforms cannot be swapped

Start with this article's own number: in TAILORx's middle score band, among those on endocrine therapy alone, 9-year invasive disease-free survival was 83.3%[2] — roughly one person in every six had an event within nine years, and those are people whose score fell in the band that does not call for adding chemotherapy. What a low score buys is a lower probability, not zero. How the risk across the fifteen years after year 5 divides into six boxes by tumour size and nodal status, differing by more than threefold, is the table in "Five years of endocrine therapy, or ten?"[11].

Nor can one platform be converted into another. The British OPTIMA prelim study sent 313 tumours for five assays at once, and only 119 (39.4%) were classified consistently by all five, with 183 (60.6%) assigned to different risk categories[12]. The main OPTIMA trial still has no journal publication of its primary results, so this section can only stop at that comparison study.

Taiwan: this one is self-pay, and the NHI's NGS is a different line entirely

I searched the National Health Insurance Administration website for "21-gene", "multigene", "Oncotype" and "MammaPrint", and got zero hits — I could not find a reimbursement provision for this kind of test. The official information and consent document approved by National Taiwan University Hospital in July 2024 records that Oncotype DX is self-pay, at NT$170,000, with the specimen sent to a laboratory in the United States[13]. That is one hospital's figure from one version of one document; confirm the actual amount with your case manager or the medical affairs office.

What often gets mixed in with it is the next-generation sequencing that the NHI has reimbursed since 1 May 2024: in breast cancer it covers one situation only — germline BRCA1/2 testing on a blood sample in triple-negative breast cancer, which belongs to the inherited-risk line, and is used for family members' risk and for eligibility for certain drugs[14]; that is a different thing from the "proving you can do less" this article is about, and a different thing again from "finding a target to hit on the tumour". The full arithmetic of whether a self-pay test is worth it is in "This test is self-pay. Is it worth it?".

Which reports to take into clinic for this decision

I will not decide for anyone whether to have chemotherapy, because both directions carry real costs: missing chemotherapy that was needed may cost a chance that could have been cured; having chemotherapy that was not needed buys the risk of possibly lifelong numbness in the hands and feet, cardiac toxicity and secondary leukaemia. Have these ready before the appointment:

  • ER, PR, HER2, grade and tumour size on the pathology report, plus the number of nodes retrieved and the number positive. Without those lines, even whether to test at all cannot be judged.

  • Your menopausal status; and if you have not been through the menopause, ask whether there is any usable indicator of your ovarian function.

  • If you already have a score, take the complete report, including which platform it came from — platforms cannot be converted into one another.

  • A list only you can fill in: work and caring responsibilities, plans for children, how much long-term numbness in the hands and feet you could accept.

Then get this out of your mouth: "Given these items on my report, which features are you weighing? If I don't have chemotherapy, what range do you put my distant recurrence risk in?" You are entitled to ask the person opposite to say out loud what they are weighing. The answer may be "both are defensible" — but at least you will know which lines of the report it came from.

From the physician

This article does not make the decision for anyone. On the day, take the complete pathology report and your menopausal status, and if you already have a score, take the platform along with it. Get "which items are you weighing, and what range does my recurrence risk fall in without chemotherapy" out of your mouth. Confirm the cost with your case manager.
