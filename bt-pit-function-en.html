PITUITARY ADENOMA

First, find out whether it is secreting

Three studies give three different sets of proportions for the types, and the guideline cut a hole for prolactinoma in its own "if it secretes, operate" rule; triage comes before any conversation about size.

Benign Brain Tumour Guide · Pituitary adenoma · 9 references
#Pituitary#Endocrine Therapy#Biomarkers#Decisions

The thing I most want to say first, having looked all of this up: a high prolactin is not the same thing as a prolactinoma. Drugs, thyroid function, a stalk being pressed on, and an assay artefact called the hook effect all distort the number. Get this box wrong and the whole road afterwards runs off line.

The report says there is something in the pituitary, and the first thing most people ask is how many centimetres it is. In this particular box, that is the second question. The first one is whether it is secreting a hormone and which hormone it is — what it secretes decides which road first-line treatment takes: a drug, an operation, or leaving it alone for now; the size decides who it is pressing on. Those two things travel separately, and they often arrive at opposite conclusions.

Secreting and non-secreting: how much of each, and why three studies disagree

An intensive case-finding cross-sectional survey in the province of Liège, Belgium, found 68 clinically significant pituitary adenomas in 71,972 people, a prevalence of 94 per 100,000 (95% confidence interval 72.2 to 115.8; a confidence interval is the range the true value plausibly sits in, not a band in which every point is equally likely). The breakdown was prolactinoma 66%, non-secreting 14.7%, somatotroph adenoma 13.2%, Cushing's disease 5.9%[1]. A community-based study in Banbury, England, found 63 cases in 81,149 people, and the same four types came out at 57%, 28%, 11% and 2%[2]. A workshop hosted by the Pituitary Society with delegates from several societies put the ranges at prolactinoma 50 to 60%, non-functioning 20 to 40%, somatotroph adenoma 10 to 15%, corticotroph adenoma 5 to 8%[3]. The three sets do not line up, because who was scanned, how the cases were hunted for and how the denominator was counted all differ. Thyrotroph adenomas do exist, but they are too rare to appear in that ranking; I could not find a proportion I could cite, so all I can say is that it is rare.

Here is something else that gets skipped at this point: a mass in the sellar region is not necessarily an adenoma. The Endocrine Society's guideline on pituitary incidentalomas says that in a surgical series of sellar masses, 91% were pituitary adenomas and about 9% were not of pituitary origin, that non-adenoma tenth being mostly craniopharyngioma and Rathke's cleft cyst[4].

While we are here, the naming row that frightens people. In 2022 the World Health Organization's 5th edition renamed well-differentiated adenohypophyseal tumours pituitary neuroendocrine tumours (PitNET), and the ICD-O behaviour code moved from 0, which means benign, to 3, which means primary malignant[5]; what was actually used in publication was the dual form "pituitary adenoma/pituitary neuroendocrine tumor", and the society delegates who objected to the change left their reason on the record: when patients hear the word "tumour" they generally read it as malignant, and that can bring over-treatment and anxiety with it[3]. What WHO changed is where these tumours sit in a classification; it did not declare pituitary tumours malignant. The full argument is in "Three tumours, three different logics".

What the one-centimetre line is actually drawing

The guideline is blunt about it: by convention, under one centimetre is called a microincidentaloma and one centimetre or more a macroincidentaloma[4]. That is an agreed dividing line, not a biological break point. In the Liège cohort, 42.6% were already macroadenomas at the moment of diagnosis[1].

The line does not decide first-line treatment. What it decides is who gets pressed on: pushing up into the optic chiasm gives you a visual field defect, growing sideways into the cavernous sinus gives you double vision or a drooping eyelid. How position maps onto symptoms is in "Where it sits matters more than how big it is". Those 63 people in Banbury left one more set of numbers worth saying out loud: from the first symptom to the diagnosis, the median was a year and a half for prolactinoma and 0.8 years for non-functioning adenoma, against 4.5 years for acromegaly and 7 years for corticotroph adenoma[2]. The people who walk into clinic already exhausted from having "been looking for this for years" are mostly those last two types.

The guideline cut a hole in its own "if it secretes, operate" rule

The incidentaloma guideline lists a string of conditions that should be referred for surgery, and the last item reads, word for word, "Hypersecreting tumors other than prolactinomas"[4]. Those three words are the foundation of the whole triage in this group: as a rule, tumours that secrete go to surgery, and prolactinoma is explicitly singled out as the exception.

Follow that line down and it sorts like this: the first line for prolactinoma is a drug, and the first line for somatotroph adenoma and for Cushing's disease is surgery, not a drug; a non-functioning one that is not yet pressing on the optic pathway goes to surveillance; and one that has already caused a visual field defect or an eye-movement palsy, or that is abutting or compressing the optic nerve and chiasm, is referred for surgery[4]. The prolactinoma box needs one more sentence added to it, because it is the one most easily read too far: the 2025 prolactinoma consensus (a Chinese one, not the Pituitary Society's) draws its line on the shape of the tumour — for Knosp grade 0 to 1, well-circumscribed micro- and macroadenomas, surgery is also one of the first-line options, with a multidisciplinary team setting out both sides of it and then deciding together with you; it is only at Knosp grade 2 and above, in invasive macroadenomas and giant adenomas, that the consensus writes drug-first for prolactinoma[6]. That box is "Prolactinoma: the drug comes first, not the knife".

Taiwan's reimbursement clauses say the same thing from the side. In the National Health Insurance drug reimbursement regulations, pasireotide is restricted to "patients with Cushing's disease who cannot undergo pituitary surgery or in whom that surgery has failed to treat the disease"; octreotide and lanreotide are restricted to "patients with acromegaly not controlled by surgery, radiotherapy or dopamine agonist therapy"[7] (the clauses are in Chinese; the wording here is my translation of them). Putting the drug after the operation is itself an admission that the operation is the step that comes first.

A high prolactin is not the same thing as a prolactinoma

This is the box most often skipped in clinic. The 2025 consensus uses hard words for it: before your number is put down to that tumour, physiological, drug-related and other pathological causes all have to be ruled out first, and pregnancy has to be ruled out before any of them[6]. At least these have to be cleared alongside.

  • Drugs: a good many commonly used medicines push prolactin up. I did not obtain a complete list of drug names I could cite, so I am not listing one here; take every drug you are on — psychiatric, gastrointestinal and anything you bought yourself included — in as one list.

  • Thyroid function: an underactive thyroid also lifts prolactin, so it gets looked at at the same time.

  • Macroprolactin: prolactin bound to immunoglobulin into a large molecule, which the assay picks up but which has no biological activity. The consensus recommends screening for it in people whose clinical picture and whose number do not match each other[6].

  • The stalk effect: when your prolactin is only mildly to moderately raised, the reason may not be that this tumour is secreting, but that it is pressing on the pituitary stalk. The guideline points out specifically that in that situation a drug will not shrink the tumour, and it may still go on growing[4] — so the imaging carries on as normal.

  • The hook effect: the item missed most often. What the same consensus writes is that when a patient with a macroadenoma looks clinically like hyperprolactinaemia but the prolactin is normal or only slightly raised, the serum should be diluted and measured again. The mechanism is that prolactin so high it saturates the capture antibody of the assay produces a falsely low reading. The same paragraph of the consensus also writes the other half: this has become uncommon with the newer chemiluminescent immunoassays[6]. Whether to dilute and repeat is the doctor's judgement.

The non-functioning road has a timetable on it

The 2011 incidentaloma guideline puts the schedule down very concretely (and this is a document more than a decade old): a macroincidentaloma gets a repeat MRI six months after the first scan, a microincidentaloma at one year; then over the next three years, yearly for the macro ones and every one to two years for the micro ones, lengthening after that. The blood tests split in two as well — a macroincidentaloma is reassessed for hypopituitarism six months after the initial work-up and then yearly, while for a microincidentaloma with no change in clinical picture, history or MRI, what the guideline says word for word is that no further testing for hypopituitarism is needed[4].

As for natural history: up to 25% of untreated non-secreting adenomas grow during the observation period, and the growth is mostly very slow, 0.4 to 1.0 mm a year[3]. That is a maximum-diameter measurement; switch to a volume measurement and you get a different answer — the fact that the measuring method decides the conclusion is in "Watching is a form of treatment".

There is a second direction to ask about: has it left the pituitary short

As well as possibly over-secreting, an adenoma can press the normal pituitary into under-secreting. In the Liège cohort, 20.6% already had hypopituitarism at the moment of diagnosis[1]. What the incidentaloma guideline recommends for every incidentaloma patient is that both directions, hormone excess and hormone deficiency, are assessed at the same time[4]. As for which tubes actually get taken, the list I can quote word for word comes from the UK Society for Endocrinology's emergency guidance: cortisol, TSH, free T4, prolactin, IGF-1, LH, FSH, with testosterone added in men and oestradiol in women[8] — that is the emergency-department combination, and which of them to take in clinic is your own doctor's decision. How far pituitary deficiency can go, and which situations count as an emergency, is in "Pituitary apoplexy, and not enough hormone".

These tests all have listed items in Taiwan's NHI fee schedule: prolactin 09120C at 150 points, insulin-like growth factor 24023B at 480 points, cortisol 09113C at 240 points, ACTH 09119B at 450 points, along with the stimulation tests for the individual axes[9]. A listed item is not the same as your particular claim going through; how that actually runs is a question for your own doctor.

The prolactin line matters more than the centimetres line

There are a lot of lines on that report, and the ones that decide your next step are the hormone lines, not the "how many centimetres" line. So the order to ask in goes like this. Which hormones were tested, and where did each result land? Is this one a microadenoma or a macroadenoma, and is it touching the optic chiasm? If the prolactin is high, have drugs, thyroid function and the stalk effect been cleared off the list? And last: is my pituitary producing enough, and does anything need replacing?

Write the answers down, together with the month of the next scan and the month of the next blood test. Stand in the wrong box here and every step afterwards stands in the wrong place too — you have to know which type you have been put into before there is anything to discuss about whether to treat, or when.

From the physician

Which hormone it secretes decides which road first-line treatment takes; the size only decides who it is pressing on. Those two travel separately, and the order cannot be reversed either — anyone whose prolactin is raised has to have drugs, thyroid function and the stalk effect cleared off the list before that tumour is even in the conversation. Skip that step and everything discussed afterwards rests on a false premise.
