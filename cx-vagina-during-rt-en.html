DURING TREATMENT

Changes already under way during treatment

Prospective data show the vaginal changes appear early in treatment: the severe ones are few, the mild ones near-universal. The handover point is the day radiotherapy ends, not months later.

Cervical Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 6 references
#Side Effects#Daily Life#Sexual & Fertility

This article's job is to put the timing right: what is happening now, what to report, and why dilator education has to be connected up the moment the course ends. How to use the dilator belongs to the next article, hormone replacement to the next stage — your part is to take the question "when do we start?" to your follow-up visit.

My position first: definitive chemoradiation and brachytherapy are the treatments I deliver every day. So every sentence about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it — not more warmly.

What this article covers is something most education pathways put off until months after treatment ends, on the day the follow-up schedule finally gets round to it. The trouble is that the body does not wait for the schedule: the changes in the vagina and in your hormones begin during radiotherapy. I have placed this piece in the "care during treatment" group precisely to put the timing right — while you are in treatment, now, you should know what is already happening, and what has to be connected up on the day the course ends.

The changes do not wait for treatment to finish

EMBRACE, an international prospective study, followed 588 patients treated with definitive radiotherapy (most with chemotherapy) and image-guided brachytherapy. The cumulative figures within two years: severe vaginal complications of grade 3 or above in only 3.6%, but changes of grade 1 or above in as many as 89%, and grade 2 or above (not limited to stenosis — vaginal changes of all kinds) in 29%, with most appearing within six months of treatment; the commonest was stenosis, with dryness next[1]. A substudy of the same series drew the timeline finer: among 113 patients whose vaginal involvement did not exceed 5 millimetres, the physician-assessed vaginal changes already differed significantly between baseline and the first follow-up, and after that largely stopped changing — the change happens at the earliest stage; in this group most changes were mild[2]. Carry the population label as you read: that is a group with very little tumour in the vagina, and its numbers cannot be pushed onto everyone. What I want you to remember is two sentences: the severe complications are genuinely few; the mild changes are near-universal. "Not severe" and "not felt" are two different things — and "not felt" does not mean "nothing to do".

During treatment: what is expected, and what to report

During irradiation the vaginal mucosa is irritated and can become red and sore, with more discharge — the American Cancer Society's patient page also lists these as expected reactions[3]. On washing I could not find an official guideline to cite, so what follows is my usual practice in clinic, not any society's provision: warm water on the vulva is enough; do not flush douching solutions inside, and do not insert medication on your own. Discharge with a clearly abnormal smell or a change in colour, or accompanied by fever, gets reported to the team — that could be infection, not the radiotherapy itself. As for bleeding: spotting during treatment can come from the tumour or the mucosa, and mentioning it at review is enough; but heavy bleeding that cannot be stopped within a short time belongs with the same-day situations, and the threshold, together with the whole emergency list, is in the master table in "Getting through five or six weeks of pelvic radiotherapy".

Why the dilator conversation belongs on the day treatment ends, not three months later

The European guideline puts the timing very clearly, and I translate the original directly: after completion of chemoradiation and brachytherapy, patients should be educated in sexual rehabilitation measures, including the use of vaginal dilators; topical oestrogen is indicated[4]. Note the time word: it starts "after completion" — not at a clinic visit months later. The reason is in the data one section up — the change happens at the earliest stage, and waiting until the symptoms have taken shape means starting the race already behind. Then should dilators start during treatment itself? The answer is short: currently no[5]. Where the evidence stands and where there is only consensus — including the numbers in the EMBRACE series' 2025 report on regular use and vaginal change[6] — I have written out in full in "Dilators and sex: the question nobody raises first". Your homework now is one item only: at the last few visits before radiotherapy ends, ask "when does my dilator education start?". Ask it, and the handover happens.

On hormones, one sentence is enough for now

The dose of pelvic radiotherapy is enough to stop the ovaries working, and anyone not yet through the menopause will be brought into it early — the full account, including hot flushes, bone, and how the replacement formulation is chosen, belongs to the next stage's article, "Early menopause: hormone therapy is not forbidden". Here I will place just one sentence against the false taboo: hormone replacement is not contraindicated in cervical cancer — the European guideline says so in writing[4]. The guideline also writes that topical oestrogen is indicated[4]; for its NHI reimbursement status in Taiwan I could not find a provision I could cite — confirm products and coverage with your gynaecologist and pharmacist.

There is no radioactivity in your body — this has to be said clearly

With high-dose-rate (HDR) brachytherapy the radiation source is inside you for only a few minutes at a time, and after treatment there is no radioactive material of any kind in your body[3] — sharing a bed, hugging, holding a child need no protective measures. The passage on the site in "Sex during treatment: who raises it?" that mentions "radioactive seeds" is describing the prostate cancer practice of leaving radiation sources permanently in the body — a different thing from the treatment you are receiving, and those rules have nothing to do with you.

The general questions about sex already have an article on the site — I am not rewriting it

Whether sex is actually possible during treatment (mostly yes, and what matters are specific conditions — blood counts, bleeding, infection — one of which is avoiding the weeks the pelvis is under irradiation), why the medical team should be the one to open the conversation, how common changes in desire and in the body's responses are, whether chemotherapy drugs can affect a partner through body fluids, where the "48-hour condom" advice comes from, why dryness and stenosis are not psychological problems, and which situations after sex cannot wait until the next day — all of that is already written in "Sex during treatment: who raises it?", including a Taiwanese single-centre study showing that this is something education can reach. That article is the general layer; this one and "Dilators and sex: the question nobody raises first" cover only the timing and practice specific to cervical radiotherapy. The three do not repeat one another.

What you can prepare today: write the question down

Today's preparation is small, but it decides whether the handover happens: write two questions into your phone's notes and take them to the visits before radiotherapy ends — "When does my dilator education start, and who teaches me?" "Do I need topical oestrogen — and do I get it from gynaecology or from here?" These are not easy questions to say out loud, I know; that is exactly why the next article's title calls it "the question nobody raises first". Write the words down first, and opening your mouth is reduced to reading them out. The changes have already started; the moment to catch them is the day the course ends.

From the physician

At the last few visits before radiotherapy ends, ask two questions yourself: when does my dilator education start, and who teaches me? Do I need topical oestrogen? Nobody raising it first does not mean it is unimportant — write the words into your phone's notes first, and speaking is reduced to reading them out.
