BEFORE AND JUST AFTER DIAGNOSIS

I never smoked. Why me?

In Taiwan more than half of lung cancer patients never smoked. The things you are blaming yourself for have weaker evidence than you think.

Lung Cancer Guide · Before and Just After Diagnosis · 14 references
#Lung#Prevention#Biomarkers#Screening & Nodules#Decisions

Nobody arrives at this diagnosis without an explanation already prepared — the kitchen, the traffic, the bad-air years. One of those was tested by Taiwan's own cohort study, and the result was not what anyone expected.

"Doctor, I have never touched a cigarette in my life and nobody at home smokes. Why me?" In clinic that question usually comes with a list attached: was it the kitchen, was it living near the industrial park, was it those years when the air was particularly bad.

This piece will not answer in the order of that list. In Taiwan, getting lung cancer without having smoked is not a freak event, it is the majority case; and the strength of the evidence behind the things you are blaming is not what you think it is. One of them has been tested properly, and the result went the other way.

More than half of Taiwan's lung cancer patients never smoked

Taiwan's national lung cancer database reviewed 371,084 patients diagnosed between 1995 and 2015. The proportion of men who had ever smoked as adults fell from 59.4% to 29.9%; in women it stayed between 3.2% and 5.3%; and 53% of all patients had never smoked (defined as fewer than 100 cigarettes in a lifetime). Over the same period the incidence of adenocarcinoma rose from 9.06 to 23.25 per 100,000 in men and from 7.05 to 24.22 in women — the female adenocarcinoma incidence has overtaken the male[1].

The Ministry of Health and Welfare puts it more briefly: "only 5.9% of female lung cancer cases in Taiwan have smoked"[2].

So "I don't smoke" has never been a reason to rule lung cancer out in Taiwan. Who should be scanned and how often — the thresholds and the eligibility criteria — is in "A nodule you neither cut out nor forget".

If you did smoke, please don't walk away yet

This article is easy to read as "the ones who never smoked are the innocent ones". I don't accept that reading, and clinically it does not hold up either.

The national database covered 11,678 patients with advanced lung adenocarcinoma tested for EGFR between 2011 and 2014 (the denominator is advanced lung adenocarcinoma, not all lung cancer). Of these, 7,179 (61.5%) carried an EGFR mutation. Split by smoking: 41.9% in smokers, 70.0% in never-smokers; and by amount smoked, from under 15 pack-years to over 45 pack-years, the mutation rate ran 51.1%, 44.1%, 40.9%, 35.5%[3].

What those figures mean is that the two groups do not have quite the same disease. In the same dataset, median overall survival was 20.3 months with an EGFR mutation and 9.6 months without[3]. That is not to say a mutation is good luck; it says there is a drug that matches. How the testing is done and how long it takes is in "Those two weeks of waiting are not wasted".

Data from across Asia point the same way. PIONEER prospectively enrolled 1,482 newly diagnosed advanced lung adenocarcinomas across seven Asian regions; 1,450 were evaluable and 746 (51.4%) were EGFR-positive, 60.7% among those who had never smoked, falling to 31.4% above 50 pack-years. On multivariable analysis, sex was no longer significant once smoking status was adjusted for[4].

Holding behaviour from thirty years ago against a frightened person has no clinical use whatever. Looking forward, the official line is this: "for smokers, quitting for 10 to 15 years can halve the chance of developing lung cancer"[5].

Cooking fumes were put to the test by the cohort study built on that very idea

Start with where the hypothesis came from. A study in Kaohsiung in 1992 to 1993 enrolled 117 women with lung cancer (106 of them non-smokers) and an equal number of individually matched hospital controls. Among the non-smoking women, those whose kitchen had no extractor fan during the cooking years between ages 20 and 40 had an odds ratio of 8.3 (95% confidence interval 3.1–22.7); that factor, together with a history of tuberculosis and low fresh vegetable intake, accounted for 78% of the attributable risk[6]. An odds ratio divides one group's odds of the exposure by the other's; it does not mean the probability becomes 8.3 times as large.

A later meta-analysis in non-smoking Chinese women pooled 13 studies, 3,596 women with lung cancer and 6,082 controls: relative risk 1.74 (1.57–1.94) for cooking fume exposure and 2.11 (1.54–2.89) for cooking without an extractor fan[7]. That looks solid, but all 13 were case-control studies and not one was a prospective cohort; asking people to recall how they stir-fried thirty years ago is biased by construction.

Then came TALENT: a prospective cohort of 12,011 people across 17 Taiwanese medical centres, and the study Taiwan's national lung cancer early detection programme was designed around. Cooking was written into its entry criteria from the start — cumulative cooking exposure index, cooking without an extractor fan, along with second-hand smoke and a history of tuberculosis or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. The hypothesis was tested by its own cohort.

The result: on multivariable analysis, second-hand smoke exposure, cumulative cooking exposure, cooking without ventilation and a previous history of chronic lung disease were not significantly associated with lung cancer, even after stratification by family history; what was associated was female sex, a family history of lung cancer, and age over 60[8].

I am not going to rescue that with "it may still play a part". The honest statement is that this hypothesis was not reproduced in Taiwan's largest prospective dataset. I am not telling anyone to rip out their extractor fan either, but I cannot let you believe that a new one closes the lung cancer question — no study has shown that changing kitchen equipment lowers the incidence of lung cancer.

Air pollution: the association holds, the size is not what you think

The classification is not in doubt. The International Agency for Research on Cancer published Monograph Volume 109 on outdoor air pollution in 2015[9]; members of that working group set it out plainly in a review: outdoor air pollution and the particulate matter in it were classified as carcinogenic to humans, based on sufficient evidence of carcinogenicity in humans and experimental animals with strong support from mechanistic studies[10].

But classification answers "can this cause cancer", not "will you get it". The European ESCAPE project pooled 17 cohorts from 9 countries, 312,944 people, mean follow-up 12.8 years, 2,095 new lung cancers: for each 10 µg/m³ of PM10, hazard ratio 1.22 (1.03–1.45); for each 5 µg/m³ of PM2.5 it was 1.18, with a confidence interval of 0.96–1.46 that crosses 1 and therefore did not reach significance. The effect was clearer for adenocarcinoma (1.51 for PM10, 1.55 for PM2.5). Nitrogen oxides and traffic volume on the nearest road to the home showed no association[11].

Here is a translation that usually gets skipped: 1.22 is a relative risk, not "22% of people living there will get lung cancer". It multiplies a probability that was already low by 1.22.

Taiwan has local data too. Using residential address to estimate 30 years of PM2.5 exposure (a 30-year mean of 33.7 µg/m³ across participants), each 10 µg/m³ increase between 2011 and 2020 corresponded to an incidence rate ratio of 1.044 for lung adenocarcinoma relative to non-adenocarcinoma lung cancer[12]. Note that its comparison group is "all patients without lung adenocarcinoma" rather than healthy people, and the same journal has published a challenge to this paper with the authors' reply. It is a contested dataset, not a settled finding.

That national database also ran a north-south comparison: since 1993, atmospheric visibility in the north improved from 7.6 km to 11.5 km while in the south it deteriorated from 16.3 km to 4.2 km; the annual percentage change in stage IB to IV adenocarcinoma was 0.3% in the north from 2009 (−1.9 to 2.6, not significant) and 4.6% in the south from 2007 (3.3–5.8)[1]. That is an ecological observation about trends in two regions, and it cannot be carried across to you as an individual.

The factor that stands up best right now is family history

Back to TALENT. On multivariable analysis the factors associated with increased lung cancer risk were female sex, a family history of lung cancer, and age over 60; risk rose with the number of affected first-degree relatives, and was also increased for those whose mother or sibling had lung cancer[8].

What was detected leans the same way: of 12,011 participants, 318 (2.6%) were diagnosed with lung cancer and 246 of those (77.4%) were stage I; invasive lung cancer occurred in 161 of 6,009 (2.7%) with a family history against 96 of 6,002 (1.6%) without[8]. A health-check cohort of East Asian never-smoking women points at the same thing: family history was independently associated with ground-glass nodule growth within 10 years, hazard ratio 2.14 (1.03–4.44)[13].

Policy followed that result. The Health Promotion Administration (國民健康署) words it this way: "Taiwan's TALENT study shows that, for lung cancer in people who have never smoked, a family history of lung cancer is the most important risk factor currently known"[5]. Taiwan's publicly funded screening runs on two routes, family history and heavy smoking, and cooking fumes were not written into the eligibility criteria. That is not an oversight. The evidence did not support it.

TALENT also wrote a sentence that has to be read alongside all of this: overdiagnosis could have occurred, especially in participants diagnosed with adenocarcinoma in situ[8]. How to weigh that is in "A nodule you neither cut out nor forget".

Three things worth asking, instead of re-running a kitchen from thirty years ago

First, get the family history straight: have any parents, children or siblings had lung cancer, and at what age were they diagnosed. This is one of the two routes written into Taiwan's publicly funded screening; the criteria, quoted word for word, are in "A nodule you neither cut out nor forget".

Second, symptoms do not check your eligibility. A persistent cough that does not improve, blood in the sputum, unexplained weight loss — none of these depend on whether you qualify for screening. If you need to be seen, be seen.

Third, if the diagnosis is already made, find out which genes are being tested and when the report comes back. That matters far more than establishing the cause, and it is in "Those two weeks of waiting are not wasted".

Official advice on lifestyle stops at the general: "stop smoking, stay away from air pollution, keep a healthy diet and routine, to reduce the risk of lung cancer"[14]. I quote it as it stands, and I keep its measure too — that is advice, not a proven preventive effect.

From the physician

Nothing here is a verdict on how you have lived, and that applies whether or not you ever smoked. If the diagnosis is already made, what genes are being tested and when the report comes back will change your treatment; establishing the cause will not. Bring the family history to your next appointment — that is the part that carries weight.
