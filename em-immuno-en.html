TREATMENT AND THE EVIDENCE

Immunotherapy: who does it actually help

All four phase 3 trials report split by MMR status: dMMR and pMMR are too far apart to be discussed together, and the trial in the adjuvant setting did not meet its primary endpoint.

Endometrial Cancer Guide · Treatment and the evidence · 13 references
#Immunotherapy#Biomarkers#NHI Coverage#Evidence

The same drug is two different stories in two groups of women. This article puts an MMR label and a number of patients on every figure, including a counter-example that usually gets skipped: immunotherapy added to adjuvant chemotherapy after surgery, primary-endpoint hazard ratio 1.02.

"Does immunotherapy work in endometrial cancer" cannot be answered as it stands, because it is missing the one condition that decides the answer. That condition is a single line on the pathology report: are the MMR (mismatch repair) proteins deficient (dMMR) or proficient (pMMR)? All four phase 3 trials report their results split on that line, and the same drug performs so differently in the two groups that they cannot be discussed together. The mechanism is in "Why immunotherapy works for some people and not others". Every figure below follows that line on your report: who it works for, how well, and at what cost.

One more thing to say first: these trials are set in different places. RUBY, NRG-GY018 and DUO-E ask about first-line treatment of advanced or recurrent disease; KEYNOTE-775 asks about women who have already had chemotherapy; KEYNOTE-B21 asks about adjuvant treatment after surgery. Read them as one thing and you will reach the wrong conclusion.

First line: RUBY lays the gap between the two groups open

RUBY was a global double-blind, placebo-controlled randomised phase 3 trial in primary advanced (FIGO 2009 stage III or IV) or first recurrent endometrial cancer. Dostarlimab or placebo was added to carboplatin plus paclitaxel and then continued as maintenance; 494 women were randomised, of whom 118 (23.9%) were dMMR/MSI-H. Progression-free survival at 24 months in the dMMR/MSI-H group (n=118) was 61.4% (46.3 to 73.4) against 15.7% (7.2 to 27.0), hazard ratio 0.28 (0.16 to 0.50); in the overall population (n=494) it was 36.1% against 18.1%, hazard ratio 0.64 (0.51 to 0.80)[1]. A hazard ratio below 1 means that events occurred more slowly in that group; the brackets hold the 95% confidence interval.

The survival read-out shows more clearly where the difference lies. At the second interim analysis (494 patients, 51% maturity), the hazard ratio for death in the overall population was 0.69 (0.54 to 0.89), P=0.0020; in dMMR/MSI-H it was 0.32 (0.17 to 0.63) and in pMMR/MSS 0.79 (0.60 to 1.04). The words the paper uses are "a trend in favor of dostarlimab" — a trend, not an established survival benefit[2].

While we are here, the denominators. Of RUBY's 494 women, 118 (23.9%) were dMMR/MSI-H[1]; of NRG-GY018's 813, the dMMR cohort was 225. So in the advanced or recurrent population roughly one in four is dMMR; the other three-quarters have to read the pMMR column, and that column is a completely different shape.

NRG-GY018 split the two groups into two trials outright

NRG-GY018's design is more decisive: patients were separated by dMMR or pMMR status into two independent cohorts, each with progression-free survival as its own primary endpoint, with pembrolizumab or placebo added to chemotherapy. The trial registry's results section gives the numbers box by box: dMMR cohort 225 (placebo 113/pembrolizumab 112), pMMR cohort 588 (295/293)[3]. The results were, in the dMMR cohort, 12-month progression-free survival 74% against 38%, hazard ratio 0.30 (0.19 to 0.48); and in the pMMR cohort, median progression-free survival 13.1 months against 8.7 months, hazard ratio 0.54 (0.41 to 0.71)[4].

The survival page has to be written as it stands: as of 2025, overall survival data remain immature and neither cohort has reached statistical significance. pMMR hazard ratio 0.79 (0.53 to 1.17, one-sided nominal P=0.1157), dMMR 0.55 (0.25 to 1.19, P=0.0617)[5]. So what stands up at present is "progression delayed", not yet "survival prolonged".

DUO-E: once olaparib is added, the two groups get different answers

DUO-E was a three-arm trial (718 women) randomised with stratification by MMR status: chemotherapy; chemotherapy plus durvalumab; chemotherapy plus durvalumab followed by olaparib maintenance. Overall, the durvalumab arm's hazard ratio for progression-free survival was 0.71 (0.57 to 0.89, P=0.003) and the durvalumab plus olaparib arm's was 0.55 (0.43 to 0.69, P less than 0.0001). Split up, the dMMR subgroup was durvalumab 0.42 (0.22 to 0.80) and durvalumab plus olaparib 0.41 (0.21 to 0.75, median 31.8 months against 7.0 months); in that group, adding olaparib on top of durvalumab alone gave no extra benefit (hazard ratio 0.97, 0.49 to 1.98). The pMMR subgroup went the other way: durvalumab alone moved median progression-free survival only from 9.7 to 9.9 months (hazard ratio 0.77, 0.60 to 0.97), and it took olaparib on top of that to reach 15.0 months (hazard ratio 0.57, 0.44 to 0.73)[6]. The number of patients in each of those two subgroups I could not find, either in the public full text or in the trial registry's results section, so I am not giving them here. Interim overall survival (about 28% maturity): durvalumab arm 0.77 (0.56 to 1.07), P=0.120, not significant; durvalumab plus olaparib arm 0.59 (0.42 to 0.83), P=0.003[6].

For women who have already had chemotherapy: another combination, another price

Study 309/KEYNOTE-775 enrolled women with advanced disease who had had at least one prior platinum-containing regimen, comparing lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab with the physician's choice of chemotherapy, 827 women in all (pMMR 697, dMMR 130). In the pMMR group, median overall survival was 17.4 months against 12.0 months (hazard ratio 0.68, 0.56 to 0.84), and in the whole population 18.3 against 11.4 months (hazard ratio 0.62, 0.51 to 0.75); median progression-free survival in pMMR was 6.6 against 3.8 months[7]. This is the most solid set of figures we currently have in pMMR patients.

The price has to go in the same paragraph: grade 3 or worse adverse events 88.9% against 72.7%[7]. By the fifth year (median follow-up 68.8 months) there is a figure that sits closer to daily life: the proportion who stopped either of the two drugs because of a treatment-related adverse event was 32.3% against 5.9%. Five-year overall survival was 16.7% against 7.3% in pMMR, 36.5% against 9.8% in dMMR, and 19.9% against 7.7% overall[8]. A third of people did not get through the whole course — that is worth knowing before the decision, not after.

Single-agent figures need the words "single-arm" in front of them

There are data for these drugs used on their own, but without a control group. KEYNOTE-158 was a non-randomised, open-label phase 2 trial giving single-agent pembrolizumab to previously treated women with advanced MSI-H/dMMR disease, efficacy population 79, median follow-up 42.6 months. Objective response rate 48% (37 to 60), median progression-free survival 13.1 months, median overall survival not reached; treatment-related adverse events 76% (grade 3 to 4, 12%)[9]. GARNET is a single-arm phase 1 trial of single-agent dostarlimab in two cohorts: dMMR/MSI-H (n=129) response rate 43.5% (34.0 to 53.4), MMRp/MSS (n=161) response rate 14.1% (9.1 to 20.6); grade 3 or worse treatment-related adverse events 16.6%, with 5.5% stopping treatment because of them[10]. Single-arm means there is nothing to compare against, so these response rates cannot be lined up alongside the hazard ratios of the randomised trials above.

"Effective in advanced disease" is not "effective added after surgery"

This is the counter-example I most want in this article. KEYNOTE-B21 added pembrolizumab to adjuvant chemotherapy after surgery, in women with no residual disease, randomising 1,095 (545/550). The primary endpoint was disease-free survival in the whole population, and the result was a failure: hazard ratio 1.02 (0.79 to 1.32), P=0.570, 2-year disease-free survival 75% against 76%. The pre-specified subgroups by MMR status were dMMR (n=281) 0.31 (0.14 to 0.69) and pMMR (n=814) 1.20 (0.91 to 1.57); grade 3 or worse adverse events 71% against 63%[11]. The primary endpoint was not met, and the dMMR box is a subgroup analysis with few events and short follow-up; it cannot be lifted out on its own as "adjuvant immunotherapy works".

The 2025 update of the European three-society guideline (ESGO/ESTRO/ESP) keeps these apart. For first-line advanced or recurrent disease, "Patients with MMRd tumours should be offered an immune checkpoint inhibitor…in combination with carboplatin-paclitaxel chemotherapy"; for immune checkpoint inhibitor-naive women whose mismatch repair is proficient, what is recommended is pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib (I, A). For adjuvant treatment, all it goes as far as is that MMRd disease at stage IIIm to IVAm "should be considered (II, B)" — should be considered, not should be done[12].

Taiwan's clause, copied out word for word

Clause 9.69 of the NHI drug reimbursement regulations (immune checkpoint inhibitors), item 2.(10), is the place in the whole document where endometrial cancer appears; it takes effect on 1 August of Republic of China year 115. It lists dostarlimab only: "restricted to dostarlimab used in combination with carboplatin and paclitaxel…for up to 6 cycles, followed by dostarlimab as a single agent, for the first-line treatment of adult patients with mismatch repair deficient (dMMR) or microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) primary advanced or first recurrent endometrial cancer (EC)"; the conditions of use include good performance status (ECOG ≦1) and thresholds for cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic and renal function, prior approval is required, and the reimbursement period runs "2 years from the date of the first prescription"[13]. That is my translation of the clause.

Three other searches came back at zero. Pembrolizumab for endometrial cancer: zero hits in the whole text of clause 9.69. Lenvatinib's clause 9.63 carries only two indications, radioactive-iodine-refractory differentiated thyroid cancer and advanced hepatocellular carcinoma — endometrial cancer is zero hits there too. Paclitaxel's clause 9.5.1 names ovarian cancer, non-small-cell lung cancer, breast cancer and Kaposi's sarcoma; carboplatin's clause 9.2 mentions endometrial cancer only in that item 9, the one about combination with dostarlimab[13]. The clauses say only what they say: I could not find a listed item, which is not the same as not being able to have the drug and not the same as a sum you would have to pay yourself — ask your medical affairs office or your case manager.

So back to that first line. Before your next appointment, confirm one thing: has my MMR test been done, and is the result deficient or proficient? That line decides which column of all the figures above is yours; it also decides whether your next question is "which combination suits me" or "let's get the chemotherapy road right first".
