LUNG CANCER

Can adding a drug up front push resistance further away?

Survival data are now out for both FLAURA2 and MARIPOSA. Is the extra time worth the extra toxicity?

Insights · Lung Cancer · 5 references

About half of lung adenocarcinoma patients in Taiwan carry an EGFR mutation, and third-generation targeted therapy has become the default first-line answer. The trouble is that it stops working. "Bring more firepower from the start" sounds intuitive, but it has a price, and the price is not small.

Roughly half of lung adenocarcinoma patients in Taiwan carry an EGFR mutation, and the third-generation inhibitor osimertinib has become the default first-line answer. The trouble is that it stops working. On osimertinib alone, median progression-free survival runs about 16.7 months, resistance is close to inevitable, and the options left afterwards get thinner. So why not put more firepower on the table at the start and push resistance further out? A 2025 review in Cancers called this direction "beyond single agent osimertinib" and mapped two routes: add chemotherapy to the targeted drug, or replace it with a bispecific antibody plus a targeted partner[1].

Final survival data for both routes landed across 2025 and 2026. In the FLAURA2 trial, adding platinum and pemetrexed to osimertinib lifted median overall survival from 37.6 to 47.5 months among 557 patients, a hazard ratio for death of 0.77, published in the New England Journal of Medicine[2]. The MARIPOSA trial took the chemotherapy-free route, pairing the bispecific antibody amivantamab with lazertinib: three-year survival of 60% against 51%, hazard ratio 0.75, with National Taiwan University Hospital's James Chih-Hsin Yang as first author[3]. The cost is stated plainly. Grade 3 or higher adverse events ran 70% versus 34% in FLAURA2 and 80% versus 52% in MARIPOSA, with more skin toxicity, venous thromboembolism and infusion reactions than targeted therapy alone. The two combinations have never been compared head to head, so nobody can say today which one is better.

Since October 2024, Taiwan's National Health Insurance has covered first-line osimertinib for stage 3B to stage 4 lung adenocarcinoma with an EGFR exon 19 deletion or L858R mutation, no longer restricted to patients with brain metastases[4]. The intensified combinations sit differently: amivantamab is reimbursed here only for first-line EGFR exon 20 insertion disease given with chemotherapy, a different population from the one MARIPOSA enrolled[5]. Good numbers do not mean everyone should add a drug. Age, performance status, brain metastases, and whether someone can tolerate chemotherapy or regular infusion visits all change the answer. Write down these two trial names and ask your oncologist directly at the next visit: in my situation, is the extra survival time worth the extra side effects? Until your doctor changes the prescription, stay on the treatment you are already taking.
