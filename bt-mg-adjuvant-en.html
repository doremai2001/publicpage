MENINGIOMA

After the operation, do you add radiotherapy

Of the two randomised trials, one has finished recruiting without publishing and the other is still recruiting; the phase II data and the observational data are not answering the same thing. How to hold this conversation.

Benign Brain Tumour Guide · Meningioma · 13 references
#SBRT / SRS#Evidence#Clinical Trials#Decisions

Two randomised trials are asking the same question from different states: one closed recruitment in 2021 and its results are still unpublished, the other is still recruiting and will not have a primary result until 2027. What fills the gap in between is phase II and observational data — and they do not entirely agree with one another.

After an operation for a grade 2 meningioma, the next appointment often produces this sentence: "shall we add some radiotherapy while we are at it?" The person asking has usually already had one fright from the "2" on the report. I will put the conclusion at the top: as of today there is no randomised answer to this question. Not because nobody wanted one — because it has been running for nearly ten years and still cannot be read.

My position first: stereotactic radiosurgery and fractionated radiotherapy are the part I deliver myself; the other side of every comparison in this topic is my neurosurgical colleagues' operations, my neuro-otology colleagues' hearing assessments, and my endocrinology colleagues' drugs. That is why every comparison here comes with its primary source, so you can take it to neurosurgery, to neuro-otology and to endocrinology for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

One has finished recruiting and is waiting, the other is still recruiting — and they are not recruiting the same people

The two randomised trials on this question are in different states, and must not be read as "both still running". ROAM/EORTC-1308 is a randomised phase III trial allocating 1:1 to 60 Gy in 30 fractions or to active monitoring, recruiting people with a newly diagnosed solitary grade 2 meningioma, a Simpson 1 to 3 resection, and the ability to start radiotherapy within twelve weeks of surgery. Recruitment ran from 28 April 2016 and closed on 26 May 2021, with a target of 190 and 157 actually recruited, and an overall trial end date of 31 March 2026; the publications field on the registry entry still holds only the 2015 protocol and a 2020 qualitative analysis, and the main results have not been published[1].

NRG-BN003 is a different randomised phase III trial, recruiting people whose grade 2 tumour has been "completely resected", with an estimated 163 participants; it is still recruiting, with primary completion on 15 June 2027 and overall completion in 2032[2]. The entry criteria differ: ROAM's Simpson 1 to 3 is the surgeon's intraoperative judgement and covers both coagulated dura and invaded dura left behind, whereas BN003 requires a gross total resection. How those two boxes divide is in "How much came out, and whether that still counts". Even when both trials do read out, they cannot be stacked on top of one another as answers to the same question — they are not recruiting the same people.

Why a question asked for ten years is still waiting

ROAM's own radiotherapy quality assurance analysis (an August 2026 preprint, not yet peer reviewed) sets out the reason: 56 centres submitted benchmark cases and 13 of them (23%) had a major deviation; over the trial period 28 centres irradiated 64 patients between them, a median of 2 per centre, and 25 cases (39%) needed contours or plans resubmitted[3]. That is what a complex radiotherapy trial in a rare tumour looks like: slow accrual and quality control at every step. Nobody is dragging their feet — a hospital meets two or three of these in a year. The blank you are facing in clinic today is not going to be filled in by waiting one more appointment; what can be filled in is reading the few documents you already hold properly. How to find and read a trial is on the site in "Clinical trials: how to find one, how to read it".

What has been read out is phase II data

RTOG 0539 is a non-randomised, risk-stratified phase II trial in three groups, and its ten-year analysis appeared in 2026[4]. Low-risk group (60 people; grade 1, observation after resection; median follow-up 12.1 years): ten-year progression-free survival, meaning the proportion still free of tumour growth on imaging or death, 85.2%, ten-year overall survival 94.1%. Intermediate-risk group (52 people; recurrent grade 1, or newly diagnosed grade 2 after gross total resection; 54 Gy; median follow-up 12.0 years): ten-year progression-free survival 72.2%, overall survival 84.7%, with grade 3 or higher treatment-related toxicity in 5 people (9.6%). High-risk group (53 people; newly diagnosed grade 2 after subtotal resection, newly diagnosed grade 3, or recurrent grade 2 or 3; 60 Gy; median follow-up 11.1 years): ten-year progression-free survival 42.5%, overall survival 51.1%, with grade 3 or higher toxicity in 8 people (15.1%)[4].

Those three group names read very easily as a ranking of severity, and what they actually are is a crossing of two separate things — the grade, and whether it was taken out cleanly. The same grade 2 disease sits in the intermediate group after a gross total resection and in the high-risk group after a subtotal one. So you have to know which box you were put in before this row of numbers means anything, and what decides that box is the pathology report plus the operation note, not your age and not how you feel.

The intermediate group's original report gave three-year progression-free survival of 93.8% (p=0.0003), but that p value was against a historical comparison — roughly 70% for gross total resection alone — and not against a concurrently randomised control arm[5]. A separate trial, EORTC 22042-26042, was a single-arm phase II: 56 people with grade 2 disease, Simpson 1 to 3, treated to 60 Gy, median follow-up 5.1 years, estimated three-year progression-free survival 88.7%, with grade 3 or higher late signs and symptoms in 14.3%[6]. All of those figures look good, and none of them answers this question — not one is a randomised comparison of irradiating against not irradiating.

Which way the observational data point: fewer recurrences, not longer lives

A meta-analysis of 30 studies and 2,904 people with grade 2 disease after gross total resection (737 irradiated, 2,167 not): local recurrence fell significantly, odds ratio 0.50 (95% confidence interval 0.36 to 0.68, p<0.0001) — an odds ratio divides one group's ratio of happening to not happening by the other group's, and is not a multiple of probability, while a confidence interval is the range within which the estimate plausibly sits; progression-free survival favoured radiotherapy at every time point; and overall survival showed no significant difference at any time point. Everything included was observational[7].

A second meta-analysis reconstructing time-to-event data (23 studies, 3,822 people with grade 2 disease) points the same way: a hazard ratio for progression-free survival of 0.849 (95% confidence interval 0.730 to 0.988, p=0.035), which is significant — a hazard ratio divides the rate at which events occur in one group by the rate in the other; and a hazard ratio for overall survival of 0.79 (0.57 to 1.11, p=0.173), favouring radiotherapy but not significant[8]. So what the observational data will hold up is "recurrence later", not "life longer".

The larger dataset finds no benefit after a gross total resection

An international multicentre database collected 1,452 people with grade 2 meningioma (1989 to 2019), of whom 276 (19.0%) had adjuvant fractionated radiotherapy[9]. In the gross total resection group, the uncorrected five-year recurrence rate was 25.5% in those irradiated and 22.8% in those not; after G-computation adjusting for age, sex, Simpson grade, Ki-67, location, national health system and year of treatment, what the authors write is that there was no evidence that adjuvant fractionated radiotherapy reduced the risk of recurrence, with a maximum difference of −2.7% (95% confidence interval −5.6 to 0.2). The interval does not rule out a small benefit, but it does not demonstrate one either. The same dataset holds a finding the authors themselves flag as unexpected: mortality was higher among those irradiated after a gross total resection, driven mainly by an older subgroup with a low Ki-67 who were irradiated anyway[9].

One paper that runs further against the grain has to be listed too: a single centre with 318 people with grade 2 disease (1990 to 2023, 104 of them irradiated, median follow-up 5.1 years) found adjuvant radiotherapy associated with markedly worse progression-free survival (p<0.0001 after both gross total and subtotal resection) and worse disease-specific survival after gross total resection (p=0.0237); the authors write that "These findings contradict some current treatment paradigms" and call for prospective work[10]. This is retrospective single-centre data with what is almost certainly indication bias — the nastier tumours are the ones sent for radiotherapy. I am not going to use it to say radiotherapy does harm, and I am not going to hide it either.

Subtotal resection is a different box, and grade 3 another one again

In the same 1,452-person analysis, the direction in the subtotal resection group was more consistent: radiotherapy was associated with a lower risk of recurrence, both before and after adjustment[9]. RTOG 0539's low-risk group offers a hint in the opposite direction: ten-year progression-free survival was 87.6% in those confirmed by central review as gross total resections and 72.7% in subtotal ones, and the authors' concluding sentence is that this makes the management of subtotal resection a live question again[11]. So "it was taken out cleanly, do I irradiate" and "it was not taken out cleanly, do I irradiate" were never the same question.

For grade 3 disease the review's description of current practice is that it is managed uniformly with surgery followed by radiotherapy[12] — that is a consensus position rather than randomised evidence, because grade 3 has no randomised trial. The prospective figures that can be cited are RTOG 0539's high-risk group, ten-year progression-free survival 42.5% and overall survival 51.1%, and that group did not contain grade 3 disease only[4]. What else can be tried in recurrent high-grade meningioma is already covered on the site: "Meningioma: the big trial that is never coming" deals with boron neutron capture therapy, and I am not repeating it here.

While there is no answer, what you can ask for

To discuss this question at all, three things have to be on the table: the pathology report (grade, mitotic count, whether brain invasion is mentioned, Ki-67), the Simpson grade on the operation note, and the baseline magnetic resonance scan taken after the operation. Without those three, whoever is talking is talking about the general case, not about you. With them assembled, the first thing to ask is whether I count as a gross total or a subtotal resection, and whether that is based on the intraoperative judgement or on the postoperative scan. Then ask two questions pointing in opposite directions: if we do not irradiate now, how often do I get scanned and what change would alter the decision; and if we do irradiate now, how are the dose and the volume decided, and what is most likely to be damaged in my particular location[4]?

There is not much to write about the Taiwan end here: adjuvant radiotherapy is claimed under the general radiotherapy items, and I could not find a clause of its own for "postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy for grade 2 meningioma" anywhere in the NHI fee schedule[13]; how it is claimed is a question for the medical affairs office or your case manager. Whether to irradiate is not decided by cost, and it is not something I am deciding for you here — it is a decision to be talked out with neurosurgery and radiation oncology together. How follow-up is arranged is in "How follow-up is scheduled, and when it stops"; what can be done if it does come back is in "If it comes back, what can be done".

From the physician

On this question I am the side that delivers the radiotherapy, which is exactly why I would rather you heard the surgical view first. At your next appointment, find the Simpson grade on the operation note and ask one sentence: if we hold off for now, what time point and what change on imaging would alter the decision?
