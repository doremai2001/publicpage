WHAT THE EVIDENCE SAYS

That MGMT line: what it decides and what it does not

Methylated is a favourable prognostic signal; in the unmethylated cell no survival difference could be seen, progression-free survival does differ, and the randomised trial aimed at that group has never been done by anyone.

Glioblastoma Guide · What the Evidence Says · 13 references
#Molecular Testing#Biomarkers#Chemotherapy#Evidence

About one person in ten falls into the grey zone between methylated and unmethylated; when a trial is designed that omits the drug, those people are specifically excluded, precisely for fear of harming them. This article is about what that line really decides in a treatment discussion.

There are two words that line on the report can carry: methylated, or unmethylated. In clinic it gets read as the same sentence almost every time — "if it's unmethylated, does that mean I don't have to take the drug?" I will put the conclusion at the front: that line decides what you and your doctor need to talk about; it does not decide your prescription. And the reason it cannot decide your prescription is more complicated than most people assume.

First separate two things: a signal of better news, or a signal that the drug does something

Hegi's analysis, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2005, was done backwards from the tissue collected in the EORTC 26981-22981 / NCIC CE.3 trial: of 573 patients only 206 samples were assessable, the method was methylation-specific PCR, and 45% of them were methylated[1].

The first finding has nothing to do with treatment: whichever treatment was given, MGMT promoter methylation was in itself an independent favourable prognostic factor, hazard ratio 0.45 (95% confidence interval 0.32 to 0.61)[1]. Both terms want translating once here: a hazard ratio is not "the probability becomes 0.45 times as big", it says that within the same stretch of time, events happened more slowly; and the confidence interval in the bracket is the plausible range for that estimate, not your personal probability. The second finding is the one that involves the drug: in methylated patients, adding temozolomide moved median overall survival from 15.3 months to 21.7, P=0.007[1]. How to read numbers like these is in "That number, and how I am going to tell you about it".

In the unmethylated cell, survival and progression-free survival are not saying the same thing

Hegi's abstract describes the unmethylated group with the words "a smaller and statistically insignificant difference"[1]. The actual figures only became visible in a later systematic review: for the unmethylated patients in that same trial, overall survival was 11.8 months with radiotherapy alone against 12.7 months with radiotherapy plus temozolomide (54 and 60 patients respectively), with heavily overlapping confidence intervals; but in the same people, progression-free survival was 5.9 against 10.3 months (46 in each arm)[2].

Put those two lines side by side and there is only one honest way to say it: in unmethylated patients, temozolomide holding the tumour back longer is something you can see; the same trial could not see it making people live longer. And each arm held only fifty or sixty people. "No difference could be seen" and "proved to make no difference" are not the same thing — that is the step most often swapped out in statistics.

The trial that would cut this open has never been done

The 2020 systematic review in Neuro-Oncology Advances took phase III trials only, pooled 655 unmethylated patients treated with radiotherapy plus temozolomide, and got a median overall survival of 14.11 months; the methylated group (753 people) came out at 24.59 months[2]. But the most important thing in that paper is not a number, it is this sentence from the authors: no one has ever compared radiotherapy alone against radiotherapy plus temozolomide prospectively and at random in that group[2]. They go on: on the question of omitting temozolomide in unmethylated tumours, the historical data are too sparse to support any conclusion, and that holds for older patients in good condition too[2].

The closest thing to a direct answer, and the only randomised one, comes from an older population. In CCTG CE.6, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2017, both arms had exactly the same radiotherapy (40 Gy in 15 fractions) and differed only in whether temozolomide was added. In the unmethylated subgroup of 189 patients: 10.0 months against 7.9, hazard ratio 0.75, P=0.055 — the direction favours benefit, and it just failed to cross the line for statistical significance[3]. The methylated subgroup of the same trial was 13.5 against 7.7 months, hazard ratio 0.53, P<0.001[3].

There is one reference point running the other way, too. CheckMate-498 enrolled exactly this population — newly diagnosed, MGMT-unmethylated — and swapped temozolomide for nivolumab (not added to it, in place of it). In 560 patients, the arm that had temozolomide taken away did significantly worse[4] (the design and the full figures are in "Fields, immunotherapy, vaccines, protons, BNCT: where each one stands"). That is not a demonstration that temozolomide works. It is a reminder that in this cell, the price of replacing the standard drug has already been measured once.

What "prognostic" and "predictive" actually look like, side by side

These two words are hard to explain in clinic, and the Nordic trial happens to draw the picture. It randomised patients over 60 to three regimens. Among those who had radiotherapy, no difference could be seen between methylated and unmethylated (hazard ratio 0.97, P=0.81); among those who had temozolomide, the two groups separated (9.7 against 6.8 months, hazard ratio 0.56, P=0.02)[5]. The same marker splits the two groups apart only in people who received an alkylating agent — that is what "predictive factor" means.

In a population where both arms took the drug, it is still a strong prognostic marker. RTOG 0525 gave temozolomide to both of its 833 patients and compared standard with dose-dense scheduling: methylated against unmethylated overall survival was 21.2 against 14.0 months, progression-free survival 8.7 against 5.7[6]. Incidentally, the question that trial actually asked — does packing the drug in more densely help — got the answer no: survival was no better, while grade 3 or worse toxicity rose from 34% to 53%[6].

One word on the report, a continuous scale underneath it

A 2019 paper in Clinical Cancer Research modelled 4,041 valid MGMT results from four clinical trials and used a quantitative method to cut the "unmethylated" side once more. Among people called unmethylated there is a group sitting in a grey zone between truly unmethylated and methylated, about 10% of all cases; relative to the truly unmethylated, the hazard ratio was 0.35 for the methylated and 0.58 for the grey zone[7]. The authors' conclusion is blunt: people with low-level methylation may retain some sensitivity to temozolomide, so when a trial is designed that omits the drug, these people are specifically excluded, precisely for fear of harming them[7]. The same paper knocks down another intuition: a higher degree of methylation does not correspond to a better outcome[7].

The method matters too. The EANO 2021 guideline is hard on this: MGMT status should be determined by methylation-specific PCR, pyrosequencing or a methylation array, and immunohistochemistry is not a valid method[8]. A meta-analysis built on a Cochrane systematic review (32 cohorts, 3,474 people) also found both molecular methods more prognostic than immunohistochemistry for MGMT protein, with pyrosequencing slightly ahead of PCR; but the same paper states that the evidence on which CpG sites to read and where to set the cut-off is not sufficient for a strong recommendation[9]. So if there is a percentage on your report, that is a laboratory parameter, not a scale you can convert into "will it work or not".

What methylation really opens is a different door

If that line says methylated, it decides one more thing: you are more likely to be eligible for certain regimens or trials. CeTeG/NOA-09 took only MGMT-methylated, newly diagnosed patients aged 18 to 70 in good performance status, and compared standard temozolomide chemoradiotherapy at random against lomustine plus temozolomide plus radiotherapy; in 129 patients median survival went from 31.4 months to 48.1, hazard ratio 0.60, P=0.0492[10]. That looks handsome, but the authors themselves ask in the abstract for cautious interpretation, on the grounds that the trial is small[10]; EANO's comment is blunter — use of this regimen is "largely confined to certain centres in German-speaking countries"[8]. It is not routine in Taiwan. Whether it is worth discussing is a question for your neuro-oncology team.

One other thing: that line is not carved in stone. A review covering 476 patients in total found 115 of them (24%) had a different MGMT status at recurrence from the one at diagnosis, possibly because of technique, tumour heterogeneity, or selection pressure from treatment; but the authors also state that the clinical meaning remains unclear and is not enough to change current practice[11]. So please do not take this paragraph in and demand a repeat test — that is the team's decision.

What that line decides is the conversation you have

There is a Taiwanese reality on top of all this: clause 9.25 of the NHI drug reimbursement regulations, the temozolomide clause, makes no mention of MGMT or of any molecular marker at all — only the disease name, the concurrent and adjuvant stretches, and prior approval every three months[12]. Which is to say that this line does not decide whether you get the drug; it decides how you and your doctor discuss the drug. Whether the test itself is paid for by you and how much it costs, I could not find an official provision I could cite — ask your case manager or the medical affairs office.

The field's own position on this question has gone round in a circle over the past decade. The EANO guideline writes the history down: for a period, up to roughly 2016, most of the field held that omitting temozolomide in unmethylated tumours did no harm — that view was later overturned. After a small survival gain from radiotherapy plus temozolomide was observed in older unmethylated patients, and after the negative CheckMate-498 result, "this has become controversial again"[8]. Elsewhere the same guideline writes that the activity of temozolomide in unmethylated tumours "may be marginal"[8] — those two sentences next to each other are what this question honestly looks like now. Even the group who developed the MGMT test, writing a commentary in Neuro-Oncology in 2015, still ended their title in a question mark: "Withholding temozolomide in glioblastoma patients with unmethylated MGMT promoter — still a dilemma?"[13]

What that line on the report is really good for is changing the question you ask. Don't ask "I'm unmethylated, can I skip it?" Take these three into the room instead: which method was my test done by? How far is my result from the grey zone? If I do take the drug, what are you going to use, and when, to judge whether it is doing anything? Whether to take it should not be decided one-sidedly by a line on a report, and it should not be decided by an article.

And one sentence to nail down: if you are taking this drug now, do not stop, reduce or skip doses of this drug on your own because you read this article. What this line changes is the content of a conversation with your doctor; whether anything is adjusted afterwards is prescribed by him, not decided by you. As for which situations during treatment cannot wait for the next appointment and mean coming back the same day, that is in "When to come back the same day: three tiers".

From the physician

That line does not decide your prescription, it decides what you and your doctor discuss — so change the question: which method was my test done by, how far is my result from the grey zone, and if I take the drug, when will you judge whether it is doing anything? And one thing nailed down: if you are taking this drug, do not stop it, cut it or skip days on your own because you read an article.
