HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

The oesophageal cancer treatment map: which box are you in

From endoscopic resection to systemic treatment after spread, every oesophageal cancer treatment option on one map, each box pointing to the article that holds the detail.

Oesophageal Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 8 references
#Decisions#Staging#Surgery#Treatment Pathway

The map is drawn only once. It does not choose your road for you; it lets you know, when you hear the words "chemoradiotherapy", "surgery" or "immunotherapy", which box you are standing in, how many roads lead out of it, and which article holds the evidence for each. The other articles each speak only for their own box.

What patients hear about oesophageal cancer treatment is usually a string of loose terms: endoscopic resection, surgery, chemoradiotherapy, immunotherapy, stents. They are in fact different boxes on the same map. The map is drawn only here, once, so that you know which box you are standing in, how many roads lead out of it, and which article holds the evidence for each road; I give each box one paragraph and leave the detail to the articles themselves.

Two coordinates first: histology, and stage

The map's horizontal axis is squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) or adenocarcinoma; the vertical axis is stage. The adenocarcinoma column is short: for resectable locally advanced adenocarcinoma, ESMO's 2025 interim update writes perioperative FLOT chemotherapy plus surgery, level I evidence, grade A recommendation[1] — why is in "Squamous and adenocarcinoma are two different diseases". The map below follows the SCC column, because over 90% of cases in Taiwan are SCC. The vertical axis uses the clinical stage before treatment (cTNM); after surgery it is replaced by the pathological stage, and the two tables cannot be swapped, as explained in "The tests that decide whether surgery is possible". Where do Taiwanese patients fall? The registry-NHI linkage study (2008 to 2014, ages 40 to 79), looking at SCC only, gives the stage distribution: stage 0 0.9%, I 5.3%, II 16.5%, III 48.5%, IV 28.8%[2] — more than three-quarters of people start out in the lower half of the map (the 77.1% quoted in "Squamous and adenocarcinoma are two different diseases" is SCC plus adenocarcinoma combined; the 77.3% here is SCC only, and the 0.2 percentage point gap is the different denominator).

Box one is an early lesion confined to the mucosa, and endoscopy can handle it

When the cancer has not yet crossed the mucosal layer (cT1a), the chance of lymph node metastasis is low, and endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) can lift it out in one piece for a complete pathological reading. The Japan Esophageal Society guideline has drawn the boundary clearly (every question in this guideline is put to an expert vote, and "weak recommendation" means the experts themselves were not sure; for the boxes that follow I give only strong or weak, not the vote counts): if the pathology after endoscopic resection shows pT1a-MM with lymphovascular invasion, or has reached the pT1b submucosa, both additional oesophagectomy and additional chemoradiotherapy have evidence behind them, and the guideline cannot choose between them[3]. The key to this box is "look at the depth after it comes out"; resection is not the end. Indications, depth and lymph node risk, and what to add after a non-curative resection are in "Can endoscopy alone deal with an early lesion".

Box two is cStage I: surgery, or organ-preserving chemoradiotherapy

Tumour into the submucosa but nodes clear (T1bN0): two roads. The Japanese guideline gives this box two weak recommendations: surgery, or, for those who want to keep their oesophagus, definitive chemoradiotherapy with closer follow-up and salvage if something happens[3]. Both are weak recommendations; the guideline does not favour either side. In a Taiwan Cancer Registry retrospective study of 4,931 SCC patients from 2008 to 2014 that levelled the two groups' conditions as far as possible (propensity score matching), clinical stage I patients treated with definitive chemoradiotherapy and those treated with surgery plus adjuvant chemoradiotherapy showed no difference in survival; only in stages II and III did the surgery group do better[4]. What surgery takes out and where the stomach ends up is in "What surgery removes, and where the stomach goes"; the dose and chemotherapy combination for those five to six weeks of chemoradiotherapy is in "Radiation dose: why higher is not better".

Box three: locally advanced and resectable, three roads the guidelines recognise

This is the box with the most people in it and the most argument: the tumour has gone through the muscle layer or spread to lymph nodes, but has not reached the trachea or a major vessel (cStage II/III). All three roads have guideline backing. One: chemoradiotherapy then surgery. The Dutch CROSS trial at ten years of follow-up: ten-year overall survival 38% with preoperative chemoradiotherapy, 25% with surgery alone[5]; China's NEOCRTEC5010 in SCC, five-year overall survival 59.9% against 49.1%[6]. Two: chemotherapy then surgery, the mainstream in Japan: the Japanese guideline strongly recommends preoperative triplet chemotherapy with docetaxel, cisplatin and 5-FU; in the JCOG1109 trial three-year survival was 72.1%, against 62.6% for the doublet[3]. Three: definitive chemoradiotherapy without surgery, with salvage surgery if needed. ASCO's 2020 guideline wording for SCC is "preoperative chemoradiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy without surgery should be offered" — the two side by side[7]; ESMO's 2025 footnote writes operating and not operating as equivalent in survival[1] (the original wording is in "After chemoradiotherapy, do you still need surgery"). Same box: Europe and America go by chemoradiotherapy, Japan by chemotherapy, and the guidelines put operating and not operating side by side. Why our clinic recommends surgery in this box and what the international trials are testing is the whole of "After chemoradiotherapy, do you still need surgery"; whether protons can help in this box is in "Protons for oesophageal cancer: the case and where the trials stand".

More than half of Taiwan's SCC patients stand in the definitive chemoradiotherapy box

The most crowded box on the map deserves its own paragraph. The actual treatment of 7,637 resectable SCC patients in the registry from 2008 to 2016: more than half (54%) had definitive chemoradiotherapy, 26% had chemoradiotherapy then surgery, and 20% went straight to surgery[8]. The survival figures for this box, why the Taiwanese data appear to favour surgery, and why the randomised trials say adding surgery "does not add survival" are all in "After chemoradiotherapy, do you still need surgery" and not repeated here. Patients in this box will meet oesophagitis and painful swallowing during treatment, covered in "Getting through radiation oesophagitis"; how follow-up is arranged afterwards is in "How follow-up is scheduled, and the second cancer people forget".

Box four is a tumour touching the trachea or a major vessel: no resection first

Tumour invading the trachea, bronchus or aorta (cT4b): this box does not get resection. The Japanese guideline gives a weak recommendation for definitive chemoradiotherapy in unresectable cStage IVA; for those whose tumour shrinks to resectable after chemoradiotherapy, the guideline also gives only a weak recommendation on whether to operate[3]. How contact with the trachea is judged, and why bronchoscopy has to be done, is in "The tests that decide whether surgery is possible"; what patients in this box most need to watch for is a tracheo-oesophageal fistula, and the warning signs are in "When to come back the same day".

Box five: after spread, systemic treatment and palliation of swallowing

After distant metastasis the lead role passes to systemic treatment: chemotherapy, and immunotherapy under conditions. Who has evidence for immunotherapy and how the NHI clauses are worded is the whole of "Who does immunotherapy actually help"; here I mention only one thing that gets misread: postoperative immunotherapy is not for everyone who has had surgery — the footnote to ESMO's 2025 algorithm restricts it to "With residual vital tumour in the resection specimen"[1], and the Japanese guideline likewise restricts it to those who did not achieve a pathological complete response after preoperative chemoradiotherapy and surgery[3]. For salvage surgery after residual or recurrent disease, the Japanese guideline gives a weak recommendation, and its explanatory text states plainly that the operative mortality is high[3]. Palliative measures for swallowing, clinical trials and hospice care run in parallel in this box, not in sequence; they are in "After recurrence or spread, what roads remain".

At your next visit, ask your doctor to point to your box

Every box moves. An ESD specimen that turns out deeper than expected moves you from box one to box two; a PET after chemoradiotherapy that finds distant spread moves you from box three to box five; a T4 patient whose tumour shrinks after chemoradiotherapy may be reassessed. So this map is not a one-time fix; it has to be looked at again every time an important test comes back. Bring this article to your next visit and put the question like this: "With my current results, which box am I in? How many roads does this box have? Which one do you recommend, and why?" If the answer is box three, add one more: "After chemoradiotherapy, when is the decision on surgery made, and by whom?" The full answer to that question is in "After chemoradiotherapy, do you still need surgery". The things everyone should discuss before treatment — teeth, quitting, nutrition, physical training — are in "Things to settle before treatment starts".

From the physician

Your box moves with your test results, and every time an important test comes back this map is worth another look. At your next visit ask your doctor to point to your box, say how many roads it has, and name the one he or she recommends and why; if you are in box three, add the question of who decides on surgery after chemoradiotherapy, and when.
