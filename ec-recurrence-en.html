AFTER TREATMENT

After recurrence or spread, what roads remain

Salvage surgery, re-irradiation, local treatment for oligometastatic disease, immunotherapy with chemotherapy, stents and brachytherapy, early palliative care — the numbers and the NHI reimbursement conditions for every road laid out, including the ones that do not look good.

Oesophageal Cancer Guide · After Treatment · 22 references
#Recurrence#Decisions#Clinical Trials#Quality of Life

Salvage surgery: in-hospital mortality about one in ten, three-year survival about 40%; re-irradiation: five-year survival 15%; oligometastatic disease with local treatment: progression-free survival data only, so far; palliative radiotherapy with chemotherapy added: only more toxicity. These numbers are not there to make you give up; they are there so you know what each road is asking, and what it costs.

After the words "it has come back", the clinic room usually goes quiet for a few seconds. Then I say: there are several kinds of recurrence, and a different road runs behind each one. Local disease that was never cleared, disease in only one or two places, and disease already spread to many sites have very different options; even in the last case there are still roads to walk, and one of them is widely misunderstood — palliative care. I give the numbers for every road, including the ones that do not look good.

Not cleared by chemoradiotherapy but still operable: salvage surgery, both ends of the numbers

When tumour is still there after definitive chemoradiotherapy, or grows back later, and the patient's fitness can still bear it, salvage oesophagectomy is one road. Its numbers have to be read at both ends. Data from 30 European centres, 2000 to 2010: 308 salvage operations against 540 planned operations (chemoradiotherapy followed by surgery — trimodality treatment); in-hospital mortality 8.4% against 9.3%, no statistical difference; anastomotic leak 17.2% against 10.7%, more in the salvage group; matched three-year overall survival 43.3% against 40.1%; but within the salvage group, "persistent disease" (never cleared by chemoradiotherapy) had three-year survival of 40.9%, worse than the 56.2% for "recurrence" (cleared, then grew back)[1]. An earlier pooled analysis of 8 studies looked less good: in-hospital mortality 9.5% against 4.1%, leak 24% against 14%, pulmonary complications 30% against 17%[2]; MD Anderson's early series of 13 cases had 30-day mortality of 15%, leak 39%, five-year survival 25%[3]. The spread comes from era and centre experience, so what I say is this: in-hospital mortality about one in ten, varying by centre — that is true; in an experienced centre it is a road with 40% three-year survival — that is also true. A meta-analysis pooling 4 squamous cell carcinoma datasets gave salvage surgery a hazard ratio for death of 0.42 against second-line chemoradiotherapy (a hazard ratio divides the speed at which events occur in one group by the speed in the other), at the price of 10.3% surgery-related mortality[4]. The ESMO 2022 guideline lists it as an option, and warns that patients whose radiation dose exceeded 55 Gy have higher postoperative mortality and complications[5]; the 2025 interim update adds that it should only be performed in selected patients, at experienced centres[6]. Japan's JCOG0909 designed salvage surgery in as part of the treatment plan; the whole strategy is discussed in "After chemoradiotherapy, do you still need surgery".

Irradiating what has already been irradiated: a last resort, not routine

People with a local recurrence who cannot be operated on ask whether it can be irradiated again. It can, but know the price. A meta-analysis of 9 retrospective studies and 573 squamous cell carcinoma patients with locoregional recurrence after radiotherapy: survival after re-irradiation at one, two, three and five years was 59%, 25%, 25% and 15%, complete response 54%, but 62% recurred locally again; grade 3 or higher oesophageal perforation, tracheo-oesophageal fistula and radiation pneumonitis were all clearly more frequent[7]. Every one of these numbers is retrospective, which means the people chosen for re-irradiation were the ones in relatively better shape to begin with. Before re-irradiating I always talk to the patient about perforation and fistula — those are complications that kill. A different situation is nodal recurrence after surgery: in 193 Chinese squamous cell carcinoma patients recurring after surgery, three-year survival was 35.4% with salvage chemoradiotherapy, 23.8% with radiotherapy alone, 2.9% with no radiotherapy[8]. That is irradiating a region never irradiated before — a different matter from re-irradiation, and the numbers must not be read together.

Only one or two metastases: how far local treatment for oligometastatic disease has got

"Oligometastatic" is the name doctors gave to "only one or two sites of spread": the usual definition is three or fewer present from the outset, or two or fewer appearing later[9]. The question of recent years is: on top of systemic treatment, does dealing with every metastatic site by radiotherapy, surgery or ablation do any good? There is only one set of randomised data so far, ESO-Shanghai 13 from six Chinese hospitals (randomised phase II, 104 squamous cell carcinoma patients, primary controlled, 1 to 4 metastases): median progression-free survival with added local treatment 15.3 months against 6.4 months for systemic treatment alone, hazard ratio 0.26; grade 3 or higher adverse events 47% against 41%, no difference; overall survival was not reported in the paper[10]. A meta-analysis of 27 studies (only 1 of them randomised) pooled an overall survival hazard ratio of 0.61[9], but that is almost entirely retrospective data, and the people who could be selected for local treatment were better off to begin with. So the most that can be written is this: one phase II trial showed progression-free survival stretched from six months to fifteen, and overall survival has not read out. It does not amount to "oligometastatic disease can be cured"; the ESMO 2022 guideline has no specific recommendation for oesophageal oligometastatic disease either[5]. If you are in this box, the question worth asking radiation oncology is: "Are my metastatic sites technically treatable with local therapy? And how does systemic treatment follow on afterwards?"

Systemic treatment: three numbers, and the NHI clauses

The full evidence for systemic treatment of metastatic squamous cell carcinoma is in "Who does immunotherapy actually help"; here are just three numbers and Taiwan's reimbursement. First line: in CheckMate 648 (970 squamous cell carcinoma patients), among those with PD-L1 TC of 1% or above, nivolumab plus chemotherapy gave a median overall survival of 15.4 against 9.1 months — about six months more[11]. Second line: ATTRACTION-3 (419 squamous cell carcinoma patients, progressing on or intolerant of platinum- and fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy), nivolumab against chemotherapy 10.9 against 8.4 months, two and a half months more, with grade 3 to 4 treatment-related adverse events 18% against 63% — far fewer[12]. On the NHI side: two nivolumab clauses, second-line monotherapy (from April 2024, ROC year 113) and first-line with chemotherapy (from February 2026, ROC year 115), both conditional on PD-L1 TC≧1%, performance status ECOG≦1, prior approval, and a maximum of two years from the first prescription[13][14][15]; I have translated the clauses word for word in "Who does immunotherapy actually help" and will not repeat them here. The pembrolizumab entry for oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma still reads "this drug is not yet reimbursed for this indication"[14]. These clauses presuppose a PD-L1 test result and a performance status; not every patient with recurrence qualifies. Immune-related adverse reactions — pneumonitis, colitis, hepatitis, myocarditis — are uncommon but do happen; any new breathlessness, diarrhoea, jaundice or chest tightness, speak up early.

When you cannot swallow: stent, radiotherapy and brachytherapy each have a trade-off

The most tormenting part of recurrent or metastatic disease is being unable to eat, and this section is my own field. A Dutch nine-hospital randomised trial of 209 inoperable patients, stent against single-dose brachytherapy: the stent improved swallowing faster, but brachytherapy gave better long-term relief of dysphagia; complications 33% against 21%, mainly late bleeding in the stent group, 13% against 5%; no difference in survival[16]. So the two roads each have a trade-off — if you need to be eating within days, the stent is faster; if you can wait a week or two and want the effect to last longer, brachytherapy lasts longer; but not every hospital has brachytherapy, so ask your radiation oncologist. Should palliative radiotherapy have chemotherapy added? TROG 03.01 (220 patients) added one course of cisplatin/5-FU: dysphagia relief 45% against 35%, no statistical difference; overall survival 6.9 against 6.7 months; grade 3 to 4 acute toxicity 36% against 16%[17] — adding chemotherapy added only toxicity. After a stent, should radiotherapy be added? The British ROCS trial (220 patients): deterioration of swallowing at 12 weeks 49% against 45%, no difference, no difference in survival, only fewer bleeding events[18]. Doing more does not mean doing better. The ESMO guideline writes that stenting should be avoided in patients under treatment with curative intent[5], which is why no stent goes in before chemoradiotherapy and only in the palliative phase; the full discussion is in "Can't swallow, weight falling: a feeding tube first?". After a stent, if you vomit blood, pass black stools, or get sudden severe chest pain with fever, come back the same day — see "When to come back the same day". Reimbursement of the stent as a special material — I could not find a clause I could cite, so ask the medical affairs office.

Palliative care is not giving up; it starts at the same time

In many families, "referred to palliative care" means "the doctor has given up". The evidence says something else. Temel's randomised trial, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2010 — note, this was 151 patients with metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer, not oesophageal cancer — brought in palliative care at diagnosis, alongside treatment, against standard care: better quality of life at 12 weeks, depressive symptoms 16% against 38%, aggressive end-of-life treatment 33% against 54%, median survival 11.6 against 8.9 months[19]. Oesophageal cancer has no randomised trial of its own like this, but the ESMO 2022 guideline writes directly: care of advanced oesophageal cancer should include early palliative care referral and nutritional support, evidence level [I, A][5] — the level I is borrowed from randomised trials in other cancers, and I say so as it is. The point is "early" and "at the same time": the palliative team deals with pain, breathlessness, not being able to eat, not sleeping, and the strain on the family, running alongside chemotherapy and immunotherapy, not referred to after treatment ends. Taiwan's NHI palliative home care takes terminal cancer patients as its service population; its items include symptom control, replacement of indwelling tubes and tube-feeding instruction, psychosocial and spiritual care, preparation for a good death and bereavement counselling; those holding a catastrophic illness certificate are exempted from part of the co-payment[20]; the NHI Administration also widened the eligible population from June 2022 (ROC year 111)[21]. I did not obtain the full text of the clauses for inpatient palliative care and shared palliative care; ask the hospital's palliative team for the details.

At the clinic visit after recurrence is confirmed, bring these four questions

One, which kind of recurrence is mine: local, oligometastatic, or multiple sites? Two, if it is local and my fitness is still adequate, how many salvage operations a year does this hospital do, and how does the surgeon see my chances? Three, what is my PD-L1, and which NHI clause do I meet? Four, when can the palliative team come in alongside? Beyond these there is one more road: clinical trials. The Taiwan FDA's "Taiwan Drug Clinical Trial Information" site lists the oesophageal cancer trials currently recruiting in Taiwan[22]; taking your disease details to your treating doctor and asking "is there a trial I qualify for" is a reasonable question. The references in this article are there so that you have something in hand when you go for a second opinion.

From the physician

After recurrence, two things worry me most: being dismissed with "there is nothing more to do", and being led by "there are still many options" into every treatment there is. Take this article's four questions to the clinic, and ask for the palliative team to come in early — that is not giving up, it is one more team looking after your symptoms.
