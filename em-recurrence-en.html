AFTER TREATMENT

If it comes back, can it still be treated

Recurrence is not one disease; it is three situations. Where it is, and whether that area has been irradiated before, decide completely different numbers — and the three situations have denominators of very different sizes.

Endometrial Cancer Guide · After treatment · 16 references
#Recurrence#Decisions#Brachytherapy#Clinical Trials

About one in ten stage I patients recurs, and the isolated vaginal recurrence with the best chance of being controlled is only 2% of the whole cohort. In the same trial, 3-year survival after vaginal, pelvic and distant relapse was 73%, 8% and 14%. Both of those have to be known together; drop either and the reading goes crooked.

The word "recurrence" arrives in clinic carrying a conclusion already attached, and that conclusion is usually wrong. Recurrent endometrial cancer is not one disease; it is three situations, and their survival figures are too far apart to sit in the same sentence. Which box you are in is worked out by gynaecological oncology, radiation oncology and imaging together — not by reading an article and matching yourself to a paragraph.

The denominators first: one in ten recurs, and the kind most likely to be controlled is only 2%

Two centres, in New York and Houston, went back over 2,815 patients with FIGO 2009 stage I disease treated between 2009 and 2017: 278 recurred, 10%. Isolated vaginal recurrence accounted for only 61 of them — 2% of the whole stage I cohort — with 42 (69%) at the vaginal vault; recurrence outside the vagina accounted for 217, 8% of the whole cohort[1]. Among the 960 who had adjuvant vaginal brachytherapy, 19 had an isolated vaginal recurrence, again 2%[1]. In time, isolated vaginal recurrence came at a median of 11 months, extravaginal at 20.

These numbers have to be read both ways. Most stage I patients will not recur; and the box marked "recurrence at the vault, still salvageable" is real, but it holds only 2% of stage I patients — it is not what recurrence typically looks like, it is the most favourable version of it.

Where it comes back matters far more than the word itself

The cleanest comparison comes from PORTEC-1: a 1990s stage I trial (enrolment used a FIGO version predating 2023), 715 patients randomised to pelvic radiotherapy or none, with no lymphadenectomy in either arm. Three-year survival after first relapse, split by site of relapse: vaginal 73%, pelvic 8%, distant 14% (P<0.001); the authors state plainly that survival after pelvic relapse and after distant metastasis did not differ[2]. The same data cut another way: three-year survival after relapse was 51% in the control group who had never been irradiated and 19% in the group who had (P=0.004)[2].

That last sentence is easily read as "so don't have adjuvant radiotherapy". It isn't. In the same trial, adjuvant radiotherapy pushed 8-year locoregional recurrence from 15% down to 4%, and the price is less room to salvage if it does come back; that trade-off belongs to "After the operation, do you still need radiotherapy". A caution as well: PORTEC-1's salvage figures come from the 1990s, without image-guided brachytherapy, and can serve only as a historical comparison.

Isolated vaginal recurrence with no previous radiotherapy: here adding chemotherapy was a failed intervention

There is exactly one modern randomised trial. GOG-0238 randomised 165 patients to radiotherapy alone or radiotherapy plus weekly low-dose cisplatin; the cohort labels have to be memorised alongside the numbers: 82% low-grade (grade 1 or 2) endometrioid, and in 86% the recurrence was confined to the vagina. At 3 years, 73% of the radiotherapy-alone group were alive and free of progression, against 62% of the chemoradiotherapy group, hazard ratio 1.25 (95% confidence interval 0.75 to 2.07)[3]. Adding chemotherapy did not improve progression-free survival, acute toxicity was higher instead, and the authors conclude that patients with low-grade vaginal recurrence should have radiotherapy alone[3]. This is a direct counter-example to the instinct that doing a bit more must be the safer bet. The guideline sits on the same line: vault recurrence is treated with pelvic external beam radiotherapy plus intracavitary image-guided brachytherapy (IV, A), and superficial lesions may have intracavitary image-guided brachytherapy alone (IV, A)[4].

Single-centre series fill in the long-term shape: in Boston, 62 patients with stage I to II disease (staged on a version predating FIGO 2023), vaginal recurrence and no previous adjuvant radiotherapy, treated with external beam radiotherapy plus image-guided brachytherapy, had 5-year vaginal control of 82% and overall survival of 61%[5]. But in the same dataset, non-endometrioid histology carried a 12.5-fold risk of recurrence (P<0.01)[5]; and in a Japanese salvage brachytherapy series of 43 patients, grade 1 to 2 against grade 3 at the primary operation gave 5-year overall survival of 96% against 40% and progression-free survival of 58% against 0%[6]. The 73% belongs to that one particular group and cannot be lent to anybody else.

Pelvis, nodes, and people already irradiated: the road narrows, it does not end

For someone who had vaginal brachytherapy only, the guideline recommends adding external beam radiotherapy with an image-guided brachytherapy boost (IV, C); for someone who had pelvic external beam radiotherapy, molecular classification should feed into the surgical or systemic decision, and radical surgery is done only when margins can be clear, the intent is curative and the morbidity is acceptable (IV, A); where surgery is not feasible, re-irradiation may be considered in experienced centres (IV, C)[4]. Every re-irradiation denominator is small: 22 patients in Pittsburgh, 3-year local control 65.8% and overall survival 68.1%, with no grade 3 or higher bowel or bladder toxicity[7]; 32 patients in Boston, vaginal control 64% at 3 years and 56% at 5, with late grade 3 toxicity of 16% gastrointestinal, 13% urinary and 16% vaginal[8]. The general principles of re-irradiation, and how to ask about it, are on the site in "Can somewhere already irradiated be treated again?".

Pelvic exenteration is the heaviest option at this point. The COREPEX registry across 20 European centres collected 862 gynaecological cancer patients (endometrial cancer among them), 676 of whom (78.4%) had tumour-free margins; split into four groups by prognostic score, 5-year disease-free survival ran from 43.7% down to 8.0%[9]. The cost goes in the same paragraph: severe early post-operative complications in 225 (26.1%), death within 30 days in 27 (3.1%), severe late complications in 87 (10.1%)[10]. A quarter of patients have a severe early complication, and what is bought lies somewhere in that 43.7% to 8.0% range; where you land is decided by whether the margins can be made clear and whether there is disease outside the pelvis.

Distant metastasis: the efficacy figures for this box are not in this article

The guideline defines oligometastatic disease as 1 to 5 metastases across at most 3 sites, for which surgery, definitive radiotherapy (including stereotactic radiotherapy) or local ablation may be considered (IV, B)[4] — how that road is assessed and how far the evidence has got is on the site in "Treat those two or three spots — and then?". First-line treatment of disseminated recurrence splits two ways: for mismatch repair deficient disease, chemotherapy with a checkpoint inhibitor followed by maintenance (I, A); for non-deficient disease that is rapidly progressive or symptomatic, carboplatin plus paclitaxel (I, A)[4]. How much each of those roads buys is written up in "Immunotherapy: who does it actually help" and "Adding chemotherapy: what it buys and what it costs"; here I only mark where they are.

One more sentence of the guideline should be put in front of patients: multiple lines of systemic therapy, particularly in patients already treated with platinum and with checkpoint inhibitors, "should be carefully evaluated for individuals, considering the low efficacy and weighed against best supportive care" (IV, B)[4]. That is not telling anyone to give up; it is the guideline itself writing down that this has to be weighed together — and it is better raised as something you bring up in clinic than something you wait to be told.

Hormonal treatment does not have a high response rate, but responders can hold for a long time

For low-grade, oestrogen receptor positive, low-volume or asymptomatic, slowly progressing recurrence, the guideline puts hormonal treatment first among systemic options, with progestins (medroxyprogesterone or megestrol) as the recommended choice (III, A) and aromatase inhibitors and tamoxifen as alternatives (IV, C)[4]. The denominators behind these figures matter: in a randomised GOG trial of 299 patients, the low-dose arm of 145 had 25 complete responses (17%) plus 11 partial responses (8%), an overall response rate of 25%, median progression-free survival of 3.2 months and median survival of 11.1 months; the high-dose arm did worse (15%)[11]. The same trial spells out that in poorly differentiated tumours, or where the progesterone receptor was below 50 fmol/mg, the response rate was only 8% to 9%[11]. Another trial, 56 evaluable patients on alternating megestrol and tamoxifen, had 15 responders (27%), 8 of whom (53%) held their response beyond 20 months; the same trial had 2 patients with grade 4 thromboembolism[12]. So the value of hormonal treatment is not in the response rate; it is that side effects are mild and the minority who do respond can hold a long time. Keep the thrombosis in mind — how to judge it is in "A swollen calf: can it wait until tomorrow?".

On prognosis, histology speaks clearly; molecular class does not yet

Histology and grade already point the same way across the series above. Molecular class is not so tidy: a pathology study of 141 metastatic or recurrent endometrioid carcinomas found disease-specific survival significantly associated with the TCGA classification, whether counted from diagnosis at high stage or from recurrence at low stage (staged on a version predating FIGO 2023)[13]; a Norwegian real-world cohort treated with platinum-based chemotherapy went the other way — molecular classification was a significant prognostic factor in advanced disease, but among 96 patients with recurrence "outcomes did not differ significantly by molecular subgroup"[14]. Both belong on the page: molecular class cannot yet be used to predict whether your recurrence can be salvaged. The names of the four groups are back in "Two kinds of endometrial cancer, now four molecular groups".

There is, though, one action you can ask about directly in clinic: the guideline recommends repeating mismatch repair testing on recurrent tissue where feasible, to guide treatment (IV, B)[4]; and in that 141-case study, the primary and the recurrent tumour were classified discordantly in 4 cases (3.8%)[13]. Not a high proportion, but retesting costs far less than going down the wrong road.

If the report really does say recurrence, settle position, timing and who has discussed it this week

First: position. Is the recurrence in the vagina, in the pelvis or distant? Has that area been irradiated before? Those two answers decide which box you are in, and therefore which of the numbers above is yours. Second: timing. A Japanese salvage radiotherapy series of 37 patients showed that those who started radiotherapy less than three months after the recurrence was diagnosed had significantly better local control and progression-free survival[15]; the numbers are small, but the direction is clear, and it is a reason to ask for the scheduling to be brought forward. Third: whether the plan has been through a multidisciplinary discussion. The guideline appendix writes for itself that multidisciplinary discussion is key to an individualised plan[16]; how to raise it is on the site in "Has my case been discussed by a team?" and "Asking for a second opinion, out loud". And one thing to state plainly: I could not find citable official or journal data on Taiwan's own salvage results after recurrence or its pelvic exenteration volumes, so every number above comes from cohorts abroad.

From the physician

After a recurrence the two things that matter most are where it is and whether that area has been irradiated before; everything else queues behind them. Take the imaging report of the recurrence site and your original radiotherapy record to clinic together, and ask one question: has my plan been through a multidisciplinary discussion? Ask about repeating mismatch repair testing on the recurrent tissue in the same visit.
