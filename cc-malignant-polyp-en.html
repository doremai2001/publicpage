AFTER THE DIAGNOSIS

The polyp is out. Why operate again?

Does a pT1 malignant polyp need a second operation? This takes apart the lines of the pathology report that decide it, and puts the benefit and the cost of completion surgery on the same table.

Colon Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 9 references
#Decisions#Surgery#Staging#Getting Ready

Roughly one in every ten people with a pT1 lesion already has cancer in a lymph node; if the other nine all go to theatre, most of their specimens will hold no residual tumour. The difficulty is that nobody can point out in advance which one of the ten it is — this article puts the weight on both sides of that onto one scale.

There is a particular silence in clinic. The patient pushes a report across the desk: the polyp was removed whole, and the line underneath says pT1 adenocarcinoma. Then I say we should book a bowel resection. He looks up: wasn't the thing already taken out?

It was. What sits between "that one is out" and "this is dealt with" is what this article is about.

pT1 means it has already crossed one particular layer

Names first. pT1 means the cancer cells have gone through the muscularis mucosae and into the submucosa; one grade lighter, pTis, is carcinoma in situ or intramucosal carcinoma, still in the lamina propria and not through that layer[1]. That layer matters because the submucosa is where the lymphatics and blood vessels run: once cancer cells reach it, they are standing somewhere they can leave the bowel wall from.

So pT1 is not "nearly cancer". It is invasive cancer that happens to be very early. How to read the rest of the letters is in "Where T3N1 actually comes from"; here I need only this one box.

What the operation clears is not the margin, it's the nodes you can't see

The second misreading is "does my doctor think it wasn't cut out cleanly?" Usually not. A completion resection is dealing with the mesenteric lymph nodes — the ones an endoscope cannot reach and a CT scan cannot show. A meta-analysis of 67 studies and 21,238 patients with T1 colorectal cancer put the overall rate of lymph node metastasis at 11.2%[2]. Roughly one in ten people with a pT1 lesion already has cancer in a node, and that node is still in the abdomen.

Read it the other way. If the other nine all go to theatre, most specimens will contain no residual tumour. Nobody misjudged anything; that is the nature of this decision — nobody can say beforehand which one of the ten it is. The rest of the discussion only works once you accept that.

Four or five lines of the report are doing the deciding

What decides it is the histological features the pathologist wrote down. In the same meta-analysis, with all the factors in one model, poor differentiation (odds ratio 2.14, 95% confidence interval 1.39–3.28), high-grade tumour budding (2.83, 2.06–3.88) and lymphovascular invasion (3.16, 1.88–5.33) each stayed an independent predictor of node metastasis[2]. An odds ratio divides one group's "happened versus didn't happen" ratio by the other group's; it is not a multiple of probability, so 3.16 does not mean the risk becomes three times bigger. A confidence interval is the range the estimate could reasonably swing across; if it crosses 1, the data are not yet enough to say the factor really has an effect.

An earlier systematic review (17 studies, 3,621 pT1 patients with nodal data) used relative risks instead: lymphatic invasion 5.2 (4.0–6.8), tumour budding 5.1 (3.6–7.3), poor differentiation 4.8 (3.3–6.9)[3]. Those authors noted themselves that the included studies were mostly Asian populations and still need validating in Western ones.

The one most easily overlooked is tumour budding: single cells or small clusters that have broken ranks ahead of the invasive front. There was no international agreement on how to count it until 2016 — an H&E slide, one hotspot at the invasive front, a field of 0.785 square millimetres, 0 to 4 is Bd1, 5 to 9 is Bd2, 10 or more is Bd3, and the report should give the absolute count[4]. Consensus arriving that late means older reports may not carry the line at all. If yours doesn't, ask whether the slides can be scored again.

"It went in deeper" is not the same as "you have to operate"

Plenty of patients, and plenty of leaflets, stop at one sentence: submucosal invasion beyond 1 millimetre means completion surgery. That sentence has not been overturned; what has been overturned is the idea that it settles the question by itself. In the same meta-analysis, modelled alongside the other risk factors, deep submucosal invasion was no longer an independent predictor (odds ratio 1.73, 95% confidence interval 0.96–3.12, a range that crosses 1)[2].

More useful still: in T1 patients with deep invasion and none of the other histological risk factors (8 studies, 1,146 people), the absolute risk of node metastasis was 2.6%[2]. 2.6% is not zero, and not so high that there is nothing left to discuss. It goes on the clinic table to be weighed with your age, your heart and lung function and your other long-term conditions — and you should not be the only person weighing it.

Half a millimetre of margin: how much does it change?

The margin answers a different question — whether anything was left behind on the spot. Data from 11 Dutch hospitals show that with no lymphovascular invasion and no poor differentiation, a margin of 0.1 to 1 millimetre left local residual cancer in 2.9% (5/171) against 0.6% (2/351) with a margin over 1 millimetre; with a margin of 0.1 to 1 millimetre and Bd1 budding, residual cancer turned up in a single case (0.8%)[5].

Which is why how the lesion was taken endoscopically shapes every judgement after it. The European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy's 2024 update recommends that when a lesion is suspected to be superficially invasive cancer and cannot be removed en bloc by conventional polypectomy or mucosal resection, a technique achieving en bloc removal should be preferred[6]. Sent to pathology in pieces, the margin and the depth of invasion cannot be read, and the only available direction from there is the conservative one.

A positive or unreadable margin does not leave completion resection as the only road. In another study from the same Dutch group, 434 patients had a margin problem but no lymphovascular invasion and no poor differentiation; 334 went on to completion resection and 100 had full-thickness resection of the scar alone. At a median follow-up of 64 months, recurrence was 2.2% against 9.0%, but metastasis-free survival (96.8% against 92.1%) and overall survival (95.6% against 94.4%) showed no statistical difference, and 5 of the 8 recurrences in the scar-resection group could still be handled with salvage surgery[7]. An absolute difference of 6.8 percentage points in recurrence, and no difference at all in survival: an awkward result like that deserves saying as it stands, rather than half of it quoted.

It was taken out first and only then called cancer. Is it already too late?

"If I'd known, I'd have gone straight to surgery" is the regret I hear most from people who learned it was cancer only after the polyp was out. Data on 6,105 patients with T1 colorectal cancer from 27 high-volume Japanese centres can answer it: matching the two groups on age, stage and risk factors by statistical means (propensity score matching) and taking 1,219 from each, endoscopic resection followed by completion surgery against primary surgery gave 5-year overall survival of 97.1% against 96.0% and recurrence of 2.6% against 2.0%, neither a statistical difference[8]. This is observational data from centres doing very high volumes, so it cannot be flipped into "it definitely won't be worse". But in this dataset, the group who had endoscopic resection first and surgery afterwards did not do worse.

Your age and your other conditions turn the whole sum around

The benefit of completion surgery takes years to show up. The cost of the operation happens on the day. A 2026 review pulled together the cohort studies in high-risk T1 colorectal cancer: 5-year cancer-specific survival differs only slightly between adding surgery and not, most deaths in the no-surgery group came from causes other than cancer, and a meta-analysis found the survival advantage only becomes visible after ten years — while perioperative complications and short-term mortality climb with age and arrive immediately[9].

That does not mean older patients don't need the operation. It means age, cardiopulmonary function and other conditions change the sum, and they belong on the table with everything else, not in the last thirty seconds of a consultation.

How much bowel to take, and laparoscopic or open, belongs to "What the node count really decides"; I am not comparing operations here. Local excision of rectal lesions follows a different logic, and this series does not cover rectal cancer.

Operate or not: the things worth saying out loud in clinic

Take the report, turn to the histology page, and ask directly:

  • On my report, what do these four say — lymphovascular invasion, differentiation, tumour budding (which Bd grade) and margin distance? Is any recorded as not assessable?

  • If I don't add surgery, what range does my combination put the chance of node metastasis in?

  • At my age and condition, how do you estimate this operation's complication risk?

  • If I don't operate, how and how often will I be followed up? Can I change my mind later?

The cost I cannot work out for you here. The length of stay, the operation and possible self-pay items (haemostatic clips, energy devices) are to confirm with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.

From the physician

Everyone's combination of features is different, and the numbers here are population averages, not your probability. Take the whole pathology report to your next appointment, turn to the histology page, and have the doctor read out each of four items: lymphovascular invasion, differentiation, tumour budding and margin distance. Those four are what decide whether that operation should happen.
