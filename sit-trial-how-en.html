WHEN TREATMENT STALLS

Clinical trials: how to find one, how to read it

The interfaces give you counts, but a registration is not a place and not a match. What to read in the criteria, and how to leave.

Situation Guides · When Treatment Stalls · 10 references
#Clinical Trials#Decisions#Getting Ready#Cost & benefits

I recommend no trial, and this is not recruitment. Two practical numbers first: 15% of people entering phase 1 trials were excluded after signing consent and before the first dose, and the largest single reason was the cancer progressing in that gap. Time itself has a cost, which is why applying for several to see is not a good strategy.

"Is there a trial I could join?" usually gets asked straight after some bad news. Put it in the right place first: a clinical trial is one line on the list of options, not something that appears once the list has been used up, and not a higher grade of treatment. The more fundamental question — that a trial is not treatment designed for you — is covered in full in "A trial is not treatment designed for you" and is not rewritten here.

Before any of the method, one sentence has to come first. Do not stop your current treatment to keep yourself eligible. Some trials do require a previous treatment to have been stopped for a period, but when that point falls is decided by the trial team together with your existing treating team. It is not yours to calculate.

Where to look: two in Taiwan, one international

The Taiwan Drug Clinical Trial Information Site has been maintained since 2006 by the Center for Drug Evaluation under commission from the Food and Drug Administration, and lists both trial phase and recruitment status[1]. The Taiwan Clinical Trials platform, also run by the Center for Drug Evaluation, has a participant recruitment section presenting recruiting trials by disease category, plus a public education section[2]. Internationally there is ClinicalTrials.gov, from the US National Library of Medicine.

For a sense of scale: I searched through the official interface on 30 August 2026, and 600,762 studies were registered site-wide; narrowing to cancer, recruiting, with a location in Taiwan, gave 551[3]. Both numbers move daily; they are counts of registrations, not of available places, and they do not mean any one of those trials fits you. The point is not the number but two things: this is searchable, and precisely because there are so many entries you need criteria to filter them — and the first criterion is your cancer, your stage and which drugs you have already had, a list that lives in your medical records, not on a web page. So my advice is always the same: take the ones that look possible into clinic and go through them with your own treating team.

Reading the eligibility criteria: screen failure is normal, and it has been counted

Across six years at a French cancer centre, of all patients entering phase 1 trials, 192 of 1,293 (15%) were excluded after signing consent and before the first dose. Matching 182 screen failures against 182 successfully enrolled patients: median distance from home to the cancer centre 69 against 55 km, more than two metastatic sites in 45% against 34%, median screening period 14 against 11 days[4].

The distribution of reasons is what should be read hardest: the cancer progressing during that period accounted for 44%, sponsor decisions unrelated to the clinical picture 25%, patient withdrawal 13.5% and relevant comorbidity 13.5%[4]. There is a stretch of time between signing consent and the first dose, and the disease can change inside it. This is why "apply for a few and see" is not a good strategy — time itself has a cost.

What is worth reading in the criteria? How finely the cancer and stage are specified; limits on the number of prior lines of treatment; whether a particular biomarker is required; the performance status threshold; the laboratory values required for organ function; and one many people miss — whether the recruitment status is still open. Not understanding those fields is fine. Printing the whole set of criteria and taking them into clinic beats judging them yourself.

Being screened out is not the same as having no road left

A separate dataset of 217 patients with solid tumours judged ineligible: the commonest reasons were rapid disease progression 16%, poor performance status 12% and abnormal liver function 12%. What happened next is the part worth knowing — 129 of the 217 (59%) went on to receive standard treatment, and in 104 (48%) the reason they had been ineligible later stabilised or improved[5].

I particularly want this paragraph read by anyone who has come away thinking "even a trial won't take me". Not meeting the criteria is a judgement against one protocol's thresholds. It is not a judgement about you.

What a phase means: read it with real response rates, not the textbook definition

Every non-paediatric phase 1 oncology trial sponsored by the US National Cancer Institute between 1991 and 2002 — 460 trials, 11,935 participants: the overall response rate was 10.6%; among the classic phase 1 design testing a single entirely new agent, which made up 20% of all the trials, the response rate was 4.4%; trials containing at least one already-approved anticancer drug made up 46.3%, with a response rate of 17.8%. Another 34.1% of participants had stable disease or a response short of partial remission; the treatment-related death rate was 0.49%, and among the 3,465 people with toxicity data, 14.3% had at least one grade 4 toxic event[6].

That set of numbers blocks two claims at once. It blocks "phase 1 offers no chance at all", and it blocks "joining a trial means getting a new drug, something better". And the era has to be labelled: this predates the spread of immunotherapy and targeted drugs, and I could not obtain a later version, so it cannot be treated as today's figure.

What a placebo actually looks like in an oncology trial

The ethical line is in paragraph 33 of the Declaration of Helsinki: the benefits, risks, burdens and effectiveness of a new intervention must be tested against those of the best proven intervention, with exceptions only in defined circumstances, such as where no proven intervention exists, and then only with strict safeguards[7]. In other words, a placebo in an oncology trial is usually not "nothing at all" but a control added on top of standard treatment; equally, it cannot be guaranteed in reverse that every trial has standard treatment underneath. That depends on the design of that particular trial, and it is exactly what the consent form is there to explain to you.

There is also an empirical observation. Across randomised trials using placebo in urological oncology from 2014 to 2024 — 60 trials, 66 placebo arms and 81 investigational drug arms — dropout rates did not differ significantly between the two, but serious adverse event rates were significantly higher in the investigational arm in 18 of 24 physiological systems, a relationship that persisted after open-label trials were excluded. The authors conclude that, however carefully designed, oncology randomised trials may struggle to be genuinely double-blind[8]. That analysis covers urological oncology only, and extending it to other cancers needs reservation.

Withdrawing is a right written into statute, not a courtesy

Article 79 of the Medical Care Act (醫療法第 79 條) requires written consent for human trials to state at least eight matters, one of which reads, word for word, "the participant's right to withdraw consent at any time"; the same article requires that disclosure and written consent "allow sufficient time for consideration, and shall not be obtained by coercion or other improper means"[9]. Among the nine matters to be disclosed under Article 14 of the Human Subjects Research Act (人體研究法第 14 條) is "the research subject's right to withdraw consent at any time, and the manner of withdrawal", and the second paragraph of the same article states that consent "shall not be obtained by coercion, inducement or other improper means"[10]. Paragraph 26 of the Declaration of Helsinki puts it as: participants must be informed of the right to refuse to take part or to withdraw consent at any time without reprisal[7].

Two statutes and one international standard, all saying the same thing: withdrawing needs no reason, and under the rules you must not be treated adversely for it. That is worth settling face to face before you sign — if I withdraw, how does my original treatment pick up?

What the consent form must contain by law is your list of questions

Turn the items in those two provisions into questions and you have a ready-made list. What is this trial's purpose and method? What are the foreseeable risks and side effects? What effect is expected? Besides joining the trial, what other treatments are possible? If I am harmed, is there a compensation or insurance mechanism? How long will my specimens, personal data and anything derived from them be kept, and how may they be reused? Who funds the research, and how are any commercial interests arising from it dealt with? If I want to withdraw, what is the procedure[9][10]? None of this is being awkward. It is what the other side is legally required to tell you.

Add three the statutes do not cover but that hurt in practice: how often you must attend, which extra tests are involved, and how travel and accommodation are counted. I could not obtain citable quantitative data on these, so I am not going to guess — but they can be asked item by item at the consent discussion, and written down. The statutes were verified on 2026-08-30, with the Laws and Regulations Database compilation cut-off at 民國 115 年 8 月 21 日.

Do not open with "I want to join a clinical trial"

Instead of "I want to join a clinical trial", say: "Given where I am now, what are my options — and is a clinical trial one of them?" Put in that order, what you get back is a set of options rather than a yes-or-no question. If having no drug available is what brought you here, the routes the system offers are in "A mutation is not a drug"; if you want a second team's view, how to ask for it usefully is in "Asking for a second opinion, out loud". Until any of this is settled, your current treatment runs as planned. And one more thing to say in advance: there is a screening period between signing consent and the first dose, and in the data above 44% of screen failures happened inside it. If you deteriorate during that period, that is not something to be solved by "waiting for the trial to start" — report it to your existing treating team immediately.

From the physician

Joining or not joining is not a last resort, and it does not mean a higher grade of treatment — the overall response rate in phase 1 trials blocks both claims at once. Withdrawing consent is written into the Medical Care Act and the Human Subjects Research Act, needs no reason, and under the rules must not be held against you. Until any of this is settled, do not stop your current treatment to keep yourself eligible.
