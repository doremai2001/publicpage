TWO OTHER DISEASES, AND TAIWAN

Anaplastic cancer is an emergency

"Emergency" is not a figure of speech here; it is written word for word into the first recommendation of a consensus, and the survival figures have changed era, so four months is already the past.

Thyroid Cancer Guide · Two other diseases, and Taiwan · 14 references
#Urgent#Tumour Board#Targeted Therapy#Molecular Testing

Many people find "median survival four months" and stop reading there. That is a historical figure: in a recent cohort at one large centre the median is 9.5 months, and one- and two-year survival have moved from 35% and 18% to 59% and 42%.

This article is not like the others here. The others are about what happens in a few months or a few years. This one is about this week. Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma grows fast enough that "let us sort it out at your next appointment" spends the time you have.

I will write the timeline, the airway, the tube of blood that has to go off at once, the treatments that genuinely exist now, and what can and cannot be looked up on the Taiwan side. I will not soften it with qualifiers and I will not say there is nothing to be done; neither of those helps anyone who is making decisions. Figures for differentiated thyroid cancer do not transfer here. Medullary carcinoma is in 〈Medullary cancer is a different disease〉.

The word "emergency" is written into a guideline

The Latin American Thyroid Society's 2026 expert panel consensus opens its first recommendation with these words: "A rapidly enlarging thyroid mass should be considered a medical emergency and managed accordingly."[1]

That consensus has one useful feature: its wording is tied to its voting, and it says so in advance. A recommendation using "should" carries unanimous agreement; "is recommended" carries at least 70% agreement; "may be considered" means agreement fell below 70%, or that it depends on what is locally available[1]. The first recommendation uses should.

The other book is the American Thyroid Association's 2021 anaplastic guideline, thirty-one recommendations plus sixteen good practice statements, which the guideline does not grade, using the American College of Physicians grading scheme[2]. Different years, different grading. I name them separately.

The numbers carry a date, and they have changed

"Median survival four months" is probably what you have already found. That is a figure from the past — the paper's own word for it is historically[3].

The study that wrote that sentence is itself data on 479 people with anaplastic carcinoma at one large medical centre between 2000 and 2019. Median overall survival across the whole cohort was 0.79 years, which is 9.5 months. What matters more is splitting it by era: of the 227 people from 2000 to 2013, one- and two-year survival were 35% and 18%; of the 152 from 2017 to 2019 they were 59% and 42%, hazard ratio between the two eras 0.50 (95% confidence interval 0.38 to 0.67)[3].

That is one hospital's number, not a world average. At another academic hospital, 97 people from 2000 to 2021 had a median overall survival of 6.5 months (confidence interval 4.3 to 10.0)[4]; a Spanish and Portuguese registry of 214 people gives 4.5 months[1]. Four numbers side by side are more accurate than any one of them pulled out alone. The differences come from the era, from whether that hospital can finish molecular testing and start treatment inside a few days, and from how far the disease had gone at diagnosis — anaplastic carcinoma is always stage IV, and what varies is the letter A, B or C.

The airway: I will put this one straight

The introduction to a 17-patient case series states that over 30% of cases "may present with" acute airway obstruction — a background generalisation, not a rate calculated from those 17 people[5]. So "should we do a tracheostomy first" gets raised in the first days after diagnosis.

The guideline first. Recommendation 23 of the Latin American consensus reads "Elective tracheostomy should not be routinely performed." — should, unanimous[1]. The American Thyroid Association's 2021 book does not carry this as a recommendation. I went through its full-text index and not one of those sentence shapes is in it. It discusses tracheostomy among the decision factors, writing that it gives immediate improvement in upper airway obstruction but requires significant education, and it does carry a recommendation that every patient have the vocal cords assessed at first presentation[2]. So I will not hang "do not do a prophylactic tracheostomy" on the American book. Those are the Latin American consensus's words.

The reasoning sits in the text: a tracheostomy markedly lowers quality of life, its effect on lengthening survival is limited, and bleeding and tumour growing out through the incision are frequent complications, which is why thinking shifted over the past decade. The same passage carries the other half as well — what might have to be done if acute airway distress happens must be discussed in advance, and an emergency tracheostomy done for symptomatic airway obstruction carries a 30-day mortality of 17.6%[1]. That figure comes from 17 consecutive patients at one hospital over two years, median overall survival 11 months, one-year survival 36%; the authors add that it should not be attempted on the ward or in the emergency department under local anaesthesia, but in an operating theatre by an experienced head and neck surgeon[5].

Put the two halves together and that is the real shape of it: not routinely done in advance, but not left until the moment of suffocation to be raised for the first time either. That conversation belongs to a time when you are awake, able to speak, and family are in the room.

How fast that tube of blood goes off

Recommendation 6 of the consensus reads "Rapid BRAF V600E testing should be performed as soon as possible after diagnosis (ideally within 24 hours) when targeted therapies are available and timely testing is feasible." — again should, unanimous[1]. It is technically possible: one group ran digital polymerase chain reaction on fine-needle aspirate material and had both patients' results inside twenty-four hours, starting drugs a few days after first contact — that was a report of two people, not routine practice[6].

Why the hurry: because the answer changes what is done that week. The pooled prevalence of BRAF V600E in anaplastic carcinoma is 33%, with individual studies running from 0% to 93%[1]; put the other way round, around 60% of these tumours do not carry the mutation[7]. The two do not conflict. One is a pooled figure, the other is the clinical reality at that centre.

The box with BRAF in it

For dabrafenib plus trametinib, the anaplastic evidence comes from one cohort of one basket trial: 36 people. Response rate 56% (95% confidence interval 38.1 to 72.1), including 3 complete responses; median progression-free survival 6.7 months, median overall survival 14.5 months, two-year survival 31.5%[8]. The earliest interim analysis had only 23 people evaluable for response, so you may come across a different denominator[9]. Thirty-six people is the cohort the indication was granted on; the evidence for the same pair of drugs later in this article is retrospective or a case series.

Recommendation 28 of the consensus lists it as first-line systemic therapy for unresectable or metastatic BRAF V600E-mutated anaplastic carcinoma, alone or combined with immunotherapy — the word is should, which under this consensus's own rules means unanimous agreement[1]. The reason for combining is written into Recommendation 34: responses to this pair are often striking but usually short-lived. One centre's retrospective comparison of 71 people gave a median overall survival of 17.0 months with immunotherapy added against 9.0 months without — retrospective, single centre, not randomised, and the authors write themselves that the conclusions are limited by the retrospective design[10].

The box without BRAF

Recommendation 33 is lenvatinib-based systemic therapy, preferably with pembrolizumab, for metastatic or unresectable anaplastic carcinoma without a BRAF mutation, and at a centre with multidisciplinary capability — the wording there is "is recommended", at least 70% agreement, not unanimous[1]. The single-centre phase 2 trial enrolled 25 people, all with distant metastases: median overall survival 13 months (95% confidence interval 7.8 to 35.6), median progression-free survival 5.4 months, best response rate in target lesions 36%[7].

Surgery has moved

Surgery used to be the first step. The order is different now. Recommendation 17 says surgery should be offered only where a complete or near-complete resection can be achieved, and should be followed by full multimodal adjuvant treatment; Recommendation 19 says debulking surgery should be strictly avoided, for lack of clinical benefit and added morbidity[1].

Recommendation 20 puts drugs ahead of surgery: for people whose disease is unresectable at the start and who carry BRAF V600E, neoadjuvant targeted therapy should be strongly considered, and Recommendation 29 has contrast-enhanced computed tomography one to three months into it, to reassess whether an operation is possible[1]. The data holding that order up are small. A consecutive series of six people all achieved complete resection, with six-month and one-year survival of 100% and 83%, locoregional control of 100%, and a line in the authors' conclusion about a decreased need for tracheostomy[11]; in that 479-person cohort, the 20 who went to surgery after BRAF-directed neoadjuvant treatment had one-year survival of 94%, hazard ratio 0.29 (95% confidence interval 0.10 to 0.78)[3]. Bear this in mind: the people who get as far as an operation are the ones whose disease was responding in the first place.

On the Taiwan side

In the 2023 cancer registry report, anaplastic carcinoma accounts for 22 men and 30 women, 52 people in the year[12]. Taiwanese survival and treatment data for anaplastic carcinoma are no entry found in the official statistics.

The National Health Insurance next-generation sequencing appendix carries a row headed 甲狀腺癌(不包含髓質癌), thyroid cancer excluding medullary thyroid carcinoma. Its second indication reads, word for word, 無分化甲狀腺癌經多專科團隊評估無法接受根除手術者: anaplastic thyroid cancer assessed by a multidisciplinary team as unable to undergo curative surgery. The column headed 應先執行之檢測項目, the tests to be performed first, reads BRAF V600E[13]. I am writing only what the text says. I am not inferring from the text how many people get it, or how fast.

As for dabrafenib and trametinib: both appear as items with payment prices in the NHI drug item listing, but the reimbursement-rules field returned by the query service is empty, and three routes failed to get me the text[14]. So this is all I can write: the reimbursement conditions for their use in anaplastic carcinoma are no entry found, and that is a question for the hospital's medical affairs office or your case manager. I am not writing that the insurance covers it and not writing that it does not; I have evidence for neither direction. How the specialties divide the work, and the rest of the NHI listings, are in 〈How this goes in Taiwan〉.

What to get asked this week

These are for moving this week along. Read them out:

  • "Has BRAF V600E been sent? On what day? Roughly when does it come back?"
  • "How is my airway assessed right now? If I get breathless in the middle of the night, where do I go, who do I speak to, who decides?"
  • "Can I be operated on now? If not, is it drugs first and look again, or a different route?"
  • "Will a multidisciplinary team discuss me? When? What should I or my family have ready?"
  • "If drugs are the plan, what has to be submitted to the insurance, and how long does that take? What happens in the meantime?"

And one worth asking while you can still speak: "At the rate this is going, what happens over the next two weeks, and how will we tell whether it is working?" Get the timeline said out loud, and every decision after it has something to stand on.

From the physician

For moving this week along, read these out: "Has BRAF been sent? On what day? When does it come back?" and "How is my airway assessed right now — if I get breathless in the middle of the night, where do I go, who do I speak to, who decides?" Whether to have a tracheostomy is a conversation to finish while you can still speak and family are in the room, not one to open at the moment of suffocation.
