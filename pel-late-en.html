DURING TREATMENT AND AFTER

Late bleeding: treatments and their evidence

Bleeding starts months later, peaks within three years and can run past ten; the options are set out one by one with their level of evidence, and of the three randomised hyperbaric oxygen trials the two in proctitis point opposite ways.

Pelvic Radiotherapy Guide · During Treatment and After · 12 references

The first question is not how to stop the bleeding but where the blood is coming from — the guideline's opening principle is not to rush the attribution, and not to biopsy. On the Taiwan box I draw no conclusion in either direction: hyperbaric oxygen has a code, and the official documents carry no indication provision for it.

You look back into the toilet and the water is red. If that happens during the course it is an acute reaction and the information sheet has covered it; this article is about the other kind — the kind that turns up for the first time months, or even years, after treatment finished. What people actually ask about it comes down to one line: does this blood need treating, and how far can treatment get?

My position first: pelvic radiotherapy is what I do every day, so every good word about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it. Implants such as the rectal spacer are not something my department provides; I write about them as an option you can go and ask about, and for cost and for who performs them, please ask that department and the medical affairs office.

Months to start, a peak inside three years, and it can run past ten

The BSG's 2025 guidance — the British Society of Gastroenterology — is candid about this: among people irradiated for a pelvic tumour, up to half will develop rectal bleeding, most of it occasional and small in volume, while severe bleeding affects about 1% of patients having radical pelvic irradiation. The time window is written out word for word too: intermittent bleeding is usually first noticed a few months after the end, peaks within three years, and sometimes persists for more than ten; the risk relates directly to the dose received by the bowel wall, and brachytherapy may move the worst-damaged site to the sigmoid[1].

On the bladder side there are only ranges, and both of them are estimates from the literature rather than measured incidences: a 2026 review writes that radiation cystitis is "estimated to affect 5% to 15%", with symptoms typically emerging after two years and risk rising beyond 60 Gy[2]; the introduction to the long-term follow-up paper of a randomised trial cites 5% to 10%[3]. The late figures for prostate cancer are in "That 13.7% is being read wrong"; for a complication that is also late but takes a different road, this topic has a separate article, "Fistula: risk, warning signs, order of care".

Work out where the blood is coming from before talking about stopping it

The BSG's first principle is not haemostasis, it is not rushing to attribute: rectal bleeding after radiotherapy must not be assumed to be radiation-induced telangiectasia, and it should not be confirmed by biopsy — that means opening a second wound in ischaemic tissue. The second principle: if the bleeding is not affecting quality of life and malignancy has been excluded, explaining the natural history is enough and no intervention is needed[1].

Before the list of procedures, one thing that is not on it and whose level of evidence is no lower than theirs: algorithmic, cause-by-cause care led by a gastroenterologist or a specialist nurse. The 218-patient randomised trial included in the Cochrane review found that, against a self-help booklet alone, six-month bowel-related quality of life improved by a mean difference of 5.47 points (1.14 to 9.81) in the doctor-led group and 4.12 points (0.04 to 8.19) in the nurse-led group — a mean difference being the average gap between the two arms on the same scale — with the quality of the evidence graded low[4]. Nothing cut, no heat applied, no known harm, and it is something you can ask for yourself.

The invasive options go side by side, each with its level attached. Sucralfate enemas are only a temporising measure in the BSG's account; in the randomised comparison included in Cochrane, using them alongside argon plasma coagulation was actually worse than placebo (quality graded low to moderate)[4]. Argon plasma coagulation is described by the BSG as "widely used", but with a severe complication rate as high as 26%, and on the anterior rectal wall after prostate brachytherapy it is an absolute contraindication — heat on ischaemic tissue can create a wound that will not heal. Formalin has single-arm data that look effective, but there is no placebo-controlled randomised trial, and its complications include enteritis, stricture and perforation[1].

On the bladder side the order is irrigation, endoscopic cautery, instillation[2]; energy coagulation, pooled across ten small studies, scores 84.7% resolution of haematuria after a single treatment, but with adverse events in 21.6% and 4.4% going on to cystectomy or diversion[5]. Another review is blunter still: there is no standard treatment for haemorrhagic cystitis[6]. Embolisation is a later line, and that review places hyperbaric oxygen ahead of diversion, embolisation and cystectomy[2].

Hyperbaric oxygen: three randomised trials, and the two in proctitis point opposite ways

The Clarke 2008 trial: chronic refractory radiation proctitis, sham-controlled, double-blind, crossover, 120 patients evaluable. Positive: SOMA-LENT improvement of 5.00 against 2.61 (p=0.0019), clinical responders 88.9% against 62.5%, number needed to treat 3 — meaning one extra responder for every three people treated[7]. That 62.5% box has to be read with the rest: the sham group improved significantly on its own[2].

HOT2 (2016): patients with chronic bowel dysfunction after pelvic radiotherapy that persisted despite optimal medical treatment, a sham-controlled, double-blind phase 3 trial, 84 patients. Neither of the two co-primary endpoints was met: change in bowel score 4 against 4 (p=0.50), rectal bleeding score 3 against 1 (p=0.092). The authors' conclusion, in their own words, is that "we found no evidence" that these patients benefit from hyperbaric oxygen[8].

Move to the bladder box and there is only one trial. RICH-ART (2019): chronic radiation cystitis, open-label, with no blinding, 87 patients. Positive: a 10.1-point difference in improvement on the EPIC urinary total score (p=0.013), number needed to treat 3; but 41% had transient visual or auditory adverse events[9]. The five-year follow-up lays out both sides: 48 of 70 responders sustained their improvement, but 22 of 70 (31.4%) never responded at any point[3].

The ceiling sentence, copied out as it stands: the 2016 Cochrane review of late radiation tissue injury goes no further than saying that hyperbaric oxygen in selected patients and tissues "may be justified"[10]. The BSG's verdict is that the randomised data are conflicting, and that clinical experience suggests at least 30 sessions are needed before any effect is seen[1]. Why the opposite directions? One explanation is patient selection and unvalidated endpoint instruments[2], another is that the trial was underpowered[1]; I am leaving both of them standing.

If you are going to do it, see the cost clearly first

The scale of the course: those trials used 30 to 40 sessions, 90 to 120 minutes each, five days a week. The side effects, item by item: myopia in 25% to 100% (mostly transient, and fully reversible after stopping, though it may take up to twelve months), middle-ear barotrauma in 2% to 3%, and oxygen toxicity that can present as a seizure at about one case per 2,000 to 3,000 treatments; the more sessions and the higher the pressure, the higher the chance[2].

Three things decide directly whether you can have it at all. Absolutely not: an untreated pneumothorax, or gas inside the eye. Needing assessment first: asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, severe claustrophobia; anyone with an implanted device has to check with the manufacturer whether it can go into a pressure chamber. And drugs: anyone who has had bleomycin needs it confirmed that there is no significant pulmonary toxicity, with an interval of three to four months; doxorubicin, an interval of three days; cisplatin is not given alongside hyperbaric oxygen — and cisplatin is exactly what concurrent chemoradiotherapy for cervical cancer uses, so this one has to be raised without waiting to be asked. On combining it with immunotherapy and targeted agents, what the review writes is that experience is lacking[2].

The Taiwan box: there is a code, and there is no indication provision

I will set out what I found and draw no conclusion in either direction. The full NHI fee schedule holds four entries under "hyperbaric oxygen": 47054C, general hyperbaric oxygen therapy, 720 points, whose notes carry no indication and no cap on the number of sessions; the other three are explicitly restricted to decompression sickness and air embolism[11]. The review notes in the Executive Yuan Gazette cover only decompression sickness, poisoning and gas gangrene, with no occurrence of "radiation" at all[12]. So I am not going to write that the NHI reimburses hyperbaric oxygen for radiation tissue injury, and I am not going to write that it does not — the documents I have will not hold up either sentence. Before going ahead, ask the medical affairs office to check the current provisions and what you would pay.

The other procedures do have codes on the table: 49023C endoscopic rectal haemostasis at 2,392 points, 49026C colonic haemostasis via colonoscopy at 8,044 points, 50011C bladder instillation at 260 points, and 33105B embolisation for gastrointestinal bleeding at 10,800 points; but "argon", "plasma" and "formalin" all return zero entries across the 6,010 items in the full table[11]. That a name is absent from the table is a fact; it does not mean the thing cannot be done, or that it has to be paid for privately.

Which bleeding is same-day, and which just gets written down

The dividing line first: occasional small amounts of blood in the stool that stop by themselves get noted down and raised at your next appointment; a large amount of fresh blood, passing clots, or bleeding together with dizziness or a cold sweat is the same-day level. Blood in the urine works the same way, and blood in the urine with being unable to pass urine or with a distended, painful lower abdomen means coming back the same day — a clot can block the urethra. As for whether it needs treating, the order is in those two BSG principles: establish where the blood is coming from, then look at whether it is affecting quality of life. If it does need dealing with, ask about the non-invasive option first, because it is the only one where you do not have to accept a harm up front. There is one action to take away — put "I have had pelvic radiotherapy" into the first sentence of every medical encounter you have from now on, so that anyone who is about to cut, biopsy or cauterise the bowel or bladder you had irradiated hears it before they start.

From the physician

Occasional small amounts of blood that stop on their own get noted down for your next appointment; a large amount of fresh blood, clots, or bleeding with dizziness or a cold sweat means being seen the same day. If it does need dealing with, ask about the non-invasive option first — gastroenterologist- or specialist-nurse-led algorithmic care is the only one where you do not have to accept a harm up front.
