Numb hands and feet: when to speak up

Oxaliplatin causes two kinds of numbness — the sort the cold sets off and the sort that piles up. This is about which stage to say something at, who decides on a dose reduction or a stop, and why shortening the course yourself loses on both sides.

Colon Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 13 references
#Side Effects#Chemotherapy#Daily Life#Late Effects

One number in the neurotoxicity data deserves to reach patients above all the others: of the people who end up with severe neuropathy, eight in ten got there through the mildest grade. So this article is not about where the limit of your endurance lies. It is about when to report, and about one line you do not get to cross on your own.

"Doctor, my fingers are a little numb, but it's manageable — I probably don't need to mention it?" You do. And you need to mention it now: by the time you can't hold a pair of chopsticks, there is much less anyone can do. What this article is really about is separating two things — speaking up early, and deciding for yourself.

The kind the cold sets off, and the kind that piles up

Oxaliplatin produces two sorts of nerve symptoms, and mixing them together leads to the wrong judgement. The first is acute: it appears within hours to two days of the infusion, and cold air, holding something cold or drinking something cold will trigger or worsen it — numbness in the hands, the feet, around the mouth or in the throat, sometimes a tight jaw, an odd-feeling tongue, speech that won't come out smoothly. Most of it fades within two weeks, and it comes back with the next dose[1]. The label estimates that about 56% of people go through this acute, reversible paraesthesia[1]. In N08CB, an adjuvant trial of 346 patients, 308 (89%) had at least one acute symptom in the first cycle alone: discomfort on touching cold objects 71%, discomfort swallowing cold things 71%, throat discomfort 63%, muscle cramps 42%, worst on day three[2].

The second is chronic. It piles up a little at a time with cumulative dose, is mainly sensory, and can take proprioception with it, so handwriting, doing up buttons and the feel of the ground under your feet all change[1]. The same N08CB study found something I bring up in clinic most weeks: the worse a patient's acute symptoms in the first cycle, the worse their later chronic neurotoxicity[2]. So what you feel during the first treatment is not something to be endured quietly. It is information.

"I'll say something when it interferes with my life" is already too late

There is one number in the label that everybody should know: of the patients who develop grade 3 persistent neuropathy (grade 3 meaning the numbness already gets in the way of everyday movements), 80% got there through an earlier grade 1 or 2[1]. Severe neuropathy mostly does not arrive one morning out of nowhere. It grows out of the stage where you think it's fine and not worth mentioning.

Does it get better? There is data pointing both ways, and I give you both. In the adjuvant setting, 60% of patients still had symptoms of some grade 28 days after the last cycle, 39% at six months and 21% at eighteen months, and at eighteen months only 1% were grade 3[1]. The MOSAIC trial randomised 2,246 patients with stage II and stage III colon cancer; in the FOLFOX4 arm grade 3 sensory neuropathy was 12.4% during treatment and 1.1% by one year of follow-up[3]. But a prospective follow-up of 91 patients shows the other side: 80% had neuropathy at the end of the course, and after a median 25 months of follow-up 84% of them had not recovered completely — only 17% did — although their median neuropathy score did fall from 9 to 4[4]. So what I say in clinic is this: most people get lighter, but "back to exactly how it was" cannot be the expectation.

Is there anything that blocks it in advance? Still nothing

I would rather you heard this from me than from somebody else's story. ASCO's 2020 update on chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy reviewed 257 new papers and 28 prevention trials and concluded that no agent is recommended for prevention, with an explicit recommendation against acetyl-l-carnitine[5]. Calcium and magnesium infusion was the one carrying the most hope: N08CB was a phase 3 randomised, placebo-controlled, double-blind trial built for exactly that question, in 353 patients on adjuvant FOLFOX, and there was no difference in the primary endpoint or in any secondary neurological endpoint; the authors concluded that its use is not supported[6]. For painful neuropathy that has already developed, duloxetine is the only agent with randomised evidence[7], and ASCO writes plainly that the size of its benefit is limited[5]. Whether these drugs are covered in Taiwan when they are used for neuropathy — I could not find a provision in the NHI's own text that I could cite; confirm that one with your case manager or the pharmacy department.

One more thing that often gets written backwards: do not suck ice when your mouth feels uncomfortable. The oxaliplatin label specifically requires that local ice be avoided, because cold makes the acute nerve symptoms worse[1].

Speaking up and deciding are not the same act

I ask patients to say how numb they are at every visit, because the ASCO guideline makes "consider delaying the dose, reducing it, substituting or stopping" the doctor's move, and that move depends on his knowing how far it has gone[5]. Cumulative dose really does buy toxicity: among the patients in SCOT with safety data, 237 (58%) of those treated for six months developed grade 2 or worse peripheral neuropathy, against 103 (25%) of those treated for three[8]; the IDEA trials set out to shorten the course in the first place precisely because oxaliplatin's neurotoxicity accumulates[9].

But here is a line I want to draw very clearly. The total length of an adjuvant course is itself part of the treatment effect: if you drop two cycles on your own, or turn your own dose down, what you throw away is the benefit and what you keep is the neurotoxicity you have already accumulated. You lose on both sides. Three months versus six is a decision with trial evidence behind it and it can be talked through; that article is "Three months or six" — take it with you and go through it with your doctor rather than settling it at home.

The rules for tablets and for infusions are not the same, and this is the point at which they most often get mixed up: Capecitabine is a tablet you take at home, and section 17 of the Xeloda label is written for the patient: at defined thresholds you hold today's tablet and phone the treatment team the same day[10]; deciding on your own to miss an infusion, to cut your own dose, or to finish the course early is not yours to decide; when you restart, and at what dose, is always the team's call.

Stopping oxaliplatin is not stopping chemotherapy

Plenty of people hear "we may take the oxaliplatin out" and assume the course has failed. These are two different things. In metastatic colorectal cancer, the OPTIMOX1 trial (620 patients) deliberately stopped oxaliplatin after six cycles and carried on with the fluoropyrimidine, reintroducing it when needed; grade 3 sensory neuropathy was 13.3%, against 17.9% in the arm that continued until progression, and the difference between the two arms did not reach statistical significance (P=.12)[11]. That is a strategy in the metastatic setting and it cannot be carried straight across to adjuvant treatment after surgery, but it does make one thing clear: the drug causing the numbness can be adjusted out while the rest goes on.

In Taiwan, section 9.10 of the NHI drug reimbursement regulations lists oxaliplatin in combination with 5-FU and folinic acid as adjuvant therapy after complete resection of a stage III colon primary[12]. How the course is then counted and claimed after a dose reduction, a delay or a stop — I could not find a formal provision I could cite. Confirm that one with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office; don't run on guesswork, and I am not going to tell you it is covered or that it isn't.

These are same-day phone calls, not next-visit questions

Two of them are tied directly to oxaliplatin. The first is cold-induced tightness in the throat. It has a very particular shape: you feel you cannot swallow and cannot get air in, but when the doctor listens with a stethoscope the lungs are clear and there is no wheeze — the label calls it pharyngolaryngeal dysaesthesia, and severe enough to interfere with daily life it runs at about 1% to 2%[1]. An episode usually follows a cold drink or walking into an air-conditioned room. It is frightening and it mostly settles by itself; the point is that the team hears about it the same day, because it changes how your next dose is arranged. The second is numbness that has started to affect movement and balance: buttons that won't do up, handwriting that goes crooked, feet that can't find the floor. In a study of 109 patients, 21 had fallen, and both the severity of loss of balance and the number of cycles completed were independently associated with falling[13]. A fall is far easier to judge than "numbness I can't stand", and it deserves to be reported sooner.

The other drugs — capecitabine, irinotecan, bevacizumab, cetuximab — each have their own same-day warning signs, and the complete list is in "The chemotherapy you take at home".

Three lines before clinic beat "it's fine, just a bit numb"

Take a sheet of paper or a note on your phone, and three lines before your appointment is enough. Line one: how far the numbness reaches — fingertips, the whole palm, or the soles of your feet — and whether today is further up or further down than before the last treatment. Line two: any movement that has become impossible this week — buttons, turning a key, carrying a bowl, going down stairs. Line three: which day the acute symptoms started and which day they faded, and whether they dragged on longer than last time. Those three lines are worth far more than "it's fine, just a bit numb", because what the doctor has to judge is a trend, not how your hand feels in the second you happen to be speaking.

If you aren't sure who to say any of this to, start with your case manager; the hospital cancer resource centre will take it too, and since the desk and its opening hours differ from hospital to hospital, the registration counter can tell you.

From the physician

Recovery runs at a different speed in everybody, and the same grade means something different to someone who works with their hands and someone at a desk. Tell your doctor your occupation and which hand you write with — it changes his judgement about when to adjust. Before your next visit, write those three lines from the article and bring them in.
