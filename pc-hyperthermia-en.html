How far the evidence for hyperthermia goes

The number of randomised trials of hyperthermia in prostate cancer is zero; here is what does exist, and what it can and cannot show.

Prostate Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 16 references
#Prostate#Hyperthermia#Clinical Trials#Decisions#NHI Coverage

This is my own subspecialty, so I have written it to a stricter standard than anything else in the series. The evidence comes first, and it does not reach as far as the internet suggests.

Every so often a man comes to clinic with printouts about hyperthermia and asks what I think. One thing first: hyperthermia is my own field, so I have set a stricter standard for this piece than for any other in the series — the level of evidence comes first, what it is and who it might suit comes after. If an article leaves you feeling you have to have this treatment, that article has been written badly.

The level of evidence comes first, and this section cannot be skipped

For radiotherapy plus hyperthermia in prostate cancer, there is to date not a single randomised trial. Not for any risk group, not for any treatment intent, not in any era. Not one. What exists is phase 1 and 2 trials, feasibility studies, single-arm series, and retrospective analyses from single hospitals.

The largest prospective study enrolled 109 men and is a single-arm phase 2 trial of salvage brachytherapy combined with interstitial hyperthermia; its primary endpoint was the rate of late grade 3 or worse bowel and urinary side effects, the efficacy endpoints secondary. What the investigators wrote in their conclusion was that, since no meaningful randomised comparative studies are available, it remains unclear whether adding interstitial hyperthermia can further improve the effectiveness of salvage brachytherapy alone[1]. That sentence is the research team's own. I cannot be more conservative than they were, and I should not be more optimistic.

There is one systematic review of the topic, and it describes hyperthermia combined with radiotherapy as a strategy worth developing and discusses future applications — the register of "promising", not "established"[2].

The "hyperthermia" your search turns up may not be the same thing at all

What this piece is about is mild heating: warming the tumour to roughly 40 to 44 °C as a way of sensitising it to radiotherapy or chemotherapy. That is the definition used by the European Society for Hyperthermic Oncology's technical committee in its quality assurance guidelines for interstitial hyperthermia[3].

A different category altogether is ablation — cryotherapy, laser, high-intensity focused ultrasound — where the aim is to destroy tissue directly. A 2026 analysis using the American SEER database compared cryoablation, laser ablation and "hyperthermia", only 107 men per group after matching, and the authors write themselves that the results must be read with extreme caution[4]. It is here only as a warning that search results mix several different things together, and this paper cannot be used to say anything about hyperthermia added to radiotherapy.

Other cancers have randomised trials. Prostate has zero

The Dutch Deep Hyperthermia Trial randomised 358 patients with pelvic tumours (bladder, cervix, rectum) to radiotherapy alone or radiotherapy plus hyperthermia: complete response 39% against 55%; in the cervical cancer group specifically, 57% against 83%, with three-year overall survival 27% against 51%. The authors' conclusion is careful: studies in larger numbers of patients are needed for the other pelvic sites before practical recommendations can be made[5]. Recurrent breast cancer has five randomised trials pooling 306 patients, local complete response 41% against 59%; not every one of the five showed an advantage on its own, and the largest effect was in recurrences in previously irradiated areas where further radiation had to be kept to low doses[6]. Soft-tissue sarcoma has a 341-patient phase 3 trial, with a hazard ratio of 0.65 for local progression-free survival and 0.73 for survival (a hazard ratio compares how fast events accrue, so 0.65 means progression arrived more slowly) — but there the hyperthermia was added to chemotherapy, not to radiotherapy[7].

Lined up: pelvic tumours 358 patients, recurrent breast 306, sarcoma 341, all randomised. The number of prostate cancer patients ever randomised is zero. That contrast is here to show the size of the gap, not to borrow strength across it. Prostate cancer does not become responsive because cervical cancer did.

Take the most-cited prostate studies one at a time and it becomes clear

  • Study 94-153 at an American cancer centre was a phase 2 single-arm trial of 37 men with no concurrent control; the two-year disease-free survival it reported was benchmarked against historical data from a different trial (RTOG 92-02)[8]. A single arm plus a historical control is the least defensible comparison in clinical research — the two populations differ in era, in stage and in what else they were given — so that figure cannot be read as "the hyperthermia is why it did better", and I am not going to quote its percentage here.

  • A 2015 retrospective study compared 82 men who had hyperthermia with 64 who did not: five-year biochemical disease-free survival 78% against 72%, not statistically significant. What was significant was a subgroup within the treated arm who received a higher thermal dose — and that subgroup was drawn after the data had been analysed[9].

  • A second retrospective study from 2022 covered 121 high-risk men (70 with hyperthermia, 51 without), and who received hyperthermia was decided by the physician; the authors' own wording is that the finding is promising and that further studies are needed to verify efficacy[10].

  • The 2021 HTProstate interim analysis (50 evaluable men) and two Polish series had acute toxicity and feasibility as their endpoints, not efficacy[11][12][13].

Another phase 2 protocol sets out the logic most plainly: if the phase 2 confirms safety and feasibility, only then is a multicentre phase 3 planned, to test whether adding regional hyperthermia improves biochemical control[14]. As of my search, that phase 3 has no results.

The genuinely interesting technical question is whether the heat actually went in

The unit of thermal dose is CEM43T90: cumulative equivalent minutes at 43 °C, taken at the level reached in 90% of the measurement points. In plain terms, it measures not how long the machine was switched on but how much the tumour was actually heated.

The numbers reported in prostate studies are small: a mean of 8.4 minutes in one[8], a median of 7.5 minutes in another, where men with a CEM43T90 above 7 minutes had better biochemical disease-free survival[10]; and in the 2015 retrospective data too, what correlated with outcome was the thermal parameters rather than whether hyperthermia had been given at all[9]. "Having had hyperthermia" and "actually having been heated to a meaningful degree" are two different things, and only the second has ever been associated with an outcome. The quality assurance guidelines make the same point a hard condition: the power output of the heating device and the temperature rise it produces determine what size and depth of tumour can be adequately heated[3].

Temperature is a safety question too. The toxicity report from that 37-man study showed that among men whose rectal wall temperature was allowed above 40 °C, 11 of 18 developed acute grade 2 proctitis, against 3 of the 19 held to 40 °C (p=0.004)[15].

When this is worth raising, and when it is not

Two settings have been studied: men with high-risk or locally advanced disease who are receiving radiotherapy with curative intent[9][10], and men whose prostate has already been irradiated, who have recurred locally, and who are being assessed for re-irradiation[1]. That is where the research has been done. It is not an indication.

The salvage setting needs one more measuring stick. A systematic review of 39 studies and 1,967 patients found five-year biochemical recurrence-free survival after re-irradiation for local recurrence to be 52.5% with low-dose-rate brachytherapy and 51% with high-dose-rate; 35 of the 39 studies were single-centre or retrospective, and there was no randomised prospective study[16]. The 58.9% at five years in that 109-man single-arm study is much the same as these results without hyperthermia[1] — which is exactly why single-arm data cannot show what the hyperthermia added.

Where it does not belong needs saying as plainly: low-risk disease suitable for active surveillance; any idea of using it instead of curative treatment (every study added it to standard treatment, none substituted it); and men whose implanted devices or anatomy make heating unsafe or simply unachievable. Not having had hyperthermia does not mean there is a hole in your treatment. Standard treatment is standard treatment.

If you do want to ask, these are the questions. What is it being added to, and how many sessions? For a situation like mine, is there prospective data, or only retrospective? How is temperature measured, and how is the rectal temperature controlled? How is the cost worked out, and which part is self-paid? The last one is the most useful of all: if I do not have it, what would be different about my standard treatment plan? If the answer is "nothing", that is the answer to this question.

From the physician

Whether this is worth raising depends on your risk group, whether the prostate has been irradiated before, and whether heating you is technically achievable and safe. Ask what it would be added to and what would change if you did not have it — and ask the case manager or billing office for the itemised cost in writing.
