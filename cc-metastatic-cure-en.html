Metastatic disease: is cure still possible?

Among people who already had liver metastases at diagnosis, five-year survival in recent years is about 20%; among the group who came through assessment and actually had liver surgery it is 42%. Most of that gap comes from who gets selected in, not from surgery making everybody better.

This article does not use the word "miracle", and it does not put anybody's story up as representative. It does one thing: it turns the line between operable and not operable into a set of conditions you can check off item by item with your notes in your hand — including the question patients in Taiwan miss most often, which is whether their imaging has ever been looked at by hepatobiliary surgery.

"It has already spread — is there nothing left to do?" and "I hear liver metastases just get cut out and then you're fine." Both of those sentences turn up in my clinic on the same afternoon, and both of them do harm. The first one makes someone who could have been cured give up on a referral. The second makes someone with widespread disease spend a whole year of effort on an operation that is never going to happen. What this article is for is drawing the line that runs between them.

The same two words, "liver metastases", sit on top of two very different numbers

Danish national registry data on 14,785 people with colorectal cancer liver metastases between 2007 and 2024 show that among those who already had liver metastases at diagnosis (synchronous disease), five-year survival rose from 13.0% (95% confidence interval 11.7%–14.3%) in 2007–2010 to 20.7% (18.1%–23.5%) in 2020–2024[1]. The figures in brackets are the 95% confidence interval: the range the true proportion can reasonably be expected to fall in, and the narrower it is the more you can lean on it. A second registry from the same country, covering 2,316 people who actually went on to have liver surgery (resection, ablation, or both), gives a median survival of 4 years and survival at one, three, five and seven years of 91%, 61%, 42% and 32%[2].

The distance between 20% and 42% is mostly not surgery making everybody better; it is who gets selected into the operating theatre. There is no answer here that applies to everyone: whether you can be cured depends on where the disease has gone, how many deposits there are, how large they are, whether enough working liver would be left behind, and whether your general condition would carry you through the operation. Where those conditions do not hold, the goal of treatment changes honestly to control and to time, not to cure.

On this subject, "cured" has a definition you can actually check

Memorial Sloan Kettering followed 612 people who had liver metastases resected between 1985 and 1994 and who had a full ten years of follow-up: 102 actually lived beyond ten years, and of those, 99 (97%) were free of disease at their last visit, with only 1 dying of this cancer after passing the ten-year mark; among the people who lived beyond five years, 34% still eventually died of cancer-related causes[3]. The authors describe it as roughly a one-in-six chance of cure from resecting liver metastases in properly selected patients[3].

Whether it can be resected is a set of conditions somebody can name one by one

A 1999 analysis of 1,001 consecutive liver resections proposed five conditions you can know before the operation: a node-positive primary tumour, less than 12 months between the primary and the metastases, more than one tumour inside the liver, a largest deposit over 5 cm, and a CEA above 200 ng/mL; the higher the score, the worse the outlook[4]. But of those 102 ten-year survivors, 50% had a node-positive primary and 39% had more than one metastasis[3] — bad prognostic factors do not automatically write a person out of long-term survival. Both of those cohorts are from the 1980s and 1990s and their absolute figures cannot be carried straight across to today; I cite them to show that "can this be resected" is something that can be laid out item by item, not somebody's instinct.

That judgement is made today by a multidisciplinary team, and what they have on the table is roughly this: how much liver you would have left after the resection (the future liver remnant, and whether it is enough to keep you alive), whether the side being kept can be grown and conditioned first, how much tumour there is altogether, whether the genetic report carries any alteration that changes the order of drugs, and whether surgery or chemotherapy comes first[5]. The Stockholm regional liver multidisciplinary team assessed 1,207 people with colorectal liver metastases between 2013 and 2021; the proportion who went on to an intervention aimed at cure was 59% of women and 62% of men, and the authors' conclusion was that a centralised, mandatory multidisciplinary referral pathway is what makes the chance of cure a fair one[6].

This assessment has to be made by a team that does liver surgery, not by whichever doctor picked you up first. There is only one question you need to be able to ask: "Has my case been looked at by hepatobiliary surgery or by a liver multidisciplinary team?"

Not operable at the start, operable later — how many people is that

One large centre analysed 1,439 consecutive patients with liver metastases between 1988 and 1999, of whom 1,104 (77%) were not resectable at diagnosis; 138 of that group (12.5%) went on to a second liver operation after an average of ten cycles of chemotherapy, with five- and ten-year survival of 33% and 23%, lower than the 48% and 30% of those resectable from the start over the same period (P=0.01), and 80% recurred in the end anyway[7]. The CELIM randomised phase 2 trial, in patients whose disease was technically unresectable or too numerous, showed that the proportion judged resectable on retrospective blinded review could be pulled from 32% before treatment to 60%[8].

Those two sets of numbers have to be read together: conversion genuinely does happen, but in about one in eight of the people who were unresectable, and it is not a road everyone gets to walk down. So "let's do chemotherapy first and see whether it becomes operable" is a question worth asking, not a result you can assume.

The local options that are not surgery differ enormously in how hard the evidence is

COLLISION is an international randomised phase 3 trial with a non-inferiority design — what it asks is not "is ablation better", but "is ablation worse than surgery by more than a margin agreed in advance". It took 300 people with resectable liver metastases, no more than 10 of them, none larger than 3 cm, with nothing outside the liver, and randomised them to thermal ablation or surgical resection. A preplanned interim analysis met the stopping rule and the trial closed early: overall survival passed the non-inferiority test (hazard ratio 1.05, 95% CI 0.69–1.58; a hazard ratio is the ratio between the rates at which deaths occur in the two groups, so 1.05 means the two are almost the same, though an interval stretching from 0.69 to 1.58 still has room in it for a difference either way), and safety was clearly better — adverse events 19% against 46% (P<0.0001), serious adverse events 7% against 20%, no treatment-related deaths in the ablation group against 3 in the surgical group[9]. That result holds only inside those very specific conditions; for deposits of 3 to 5 cm all I have to cite is retrospective registry data, and it shows better local progression-free survival per tumour with surgical resection (HR 1.86, 95% CI 1.24–2.81)[10].

Stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SABR) gets asked about often, and I have to write it down as an evidence gap: there is no randomised trial specifically in colorectal cancer liver metastases that has read out and that I can cite. The one quoted most often, SABR-COMET, was a phase 2 screening design with α widened to 0.20, and of its 99 patients only 18 had a colorectal primary[11]. What it supports is "this direction is worth running trials in", not "radiotherapy can replace surgery".

Lung and peritoneal metastases do not take the liver's logic

PulMiCC randomised 93 people with resectable lung metastases from colorectal cancer across 14 hospitals: median survival was 3.5 years (95% CI 3.1–6.6) in the metastasectomy group and 3.8 years (3.1–4.6) in the control group who did not have their lung metastases removed, with an unadjusted hazard ratio for death within five years of 0.93 (0.56–1.56)[12]. The authors' point is that the control group did far better than had been assumed — a large part of those attractive observational results comes from who gets chosen for surgery. The trial enrolled only 93 people and has been criticised for that, but it is the only randomised data there is at present.

On the peritoneal side, PRODIGE 7 randomised 265 people whose peritoneal disease was of moderate extent or less (scored on something called the peritoneal cancer index, 25 points or fewer) and in whom every tumour visible to the eye was removed during the operation, to cytoreductive surgery plus hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) or to cytoreductive surgery alone: median overall survival was 41.7 against 41.2 months (HR 1.00, 95.37% CI 0.63–1.58, P=0.99), and grade 3 or higher adverse events at 60 days were commoner in the HIPEC group (26% against 15%, P=0.035)[13]. The authors concluded that what carries this is the cytoreductive surgery itself, not the HIPEC[13]. Read the other way round, a median survival of about 41 months also leaves "peritoneal metastases mean nothing can be done" with nothing to stand on.

One more instinct that needs correcting: more drug is not better. The New EPOC randomised phase 3 trial added cetuximab to perioperative chemotherapy in people with resectable or suboptimally resectable liver metastases, and median overall survival went down rather than up, from 81.0 to 55.4 months (HR 1.45, 95% CI 1.02–2.05, P=0.036)[14]. The order of treatment is something the team designs as a whole. While you are on drug treatment, a fever above 38 °C is a same-day call to the treatment team whatever you feel like at the time; and if cetuximab is on your prescription, breathlessness, wheeze, a hoarse voice or dizziness on the day of the first infusion and in the hours after it has to be said out loud to whoever is in the room, immediately. What else each of the drugs in your hand needs watching for is written out one by one in "The chemotherapy you take at home". The treatment logic of rectal cancer is different from that of colon cancer, and this series does not deal with it.

Before your next scan, make sure two things have already been done

On the Taiwan side, the CT scans used for follow-up and assessment (33070B, 33071B, 33072B) are listed in the NHI fee schedule[15]; the conditions PET has to meet before it is covered, and why it cannot be used as routine follow-up, I have written up in "Is closer follow-up worth it?". As for liver metastasectomy, ablation, stereotactic radiotherapy, cytoreductive surgery and HIPEC, I could not find an official provision I could cite on the coverage conditions or on what you would pay yourself — that has to be confirmed with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.

  • The first thing goes in this month: find out whether your imaging has been looked at by hepatobiliary surgery or by a liver multidisciplinary team, and what the meeting recorded as its conclusion. If the answer is "not yet", that is the first sentence out of your mouth at the next appointment[6].

  • The second thing goes after every scan: ask the team to tell you, in one of three phrasings, where you are now — resectable, not resectable for the moment, or not aiming at surgery — and what would have to change for you to move from one to another. These three boxes shift as the disease responds to treatment; they are not settled once and for all[5].

  • If surgery is not the current goal, reset your calendar to the rhythm of treatment cycles and scan assessments, rather than waiting for a turn in your fortunes. Write down the size and the site of the lesions, because those are the two numbers that decide whether surgery gets reassessed[9].

From the physician

Whichever box you are in at the moment, settle one thing first: whether your imaging has been looked at by hepatobiliary surgery or by a liver multidisciplinary team. If the answer is "not yet", that is the first sentence out of your mouth at the next appointment. The coverage conditions for each of the local treatments have to be confirmed with your case manager.
