WHAT THE EVIDENCE SAYS FOR EACH TREATMENT

Radiation dose: why higher is not better

Five randomised trials pushed the dose from 50 Gy up to 60 and 66 Gy, and not one bought better control; in the end this question comes back round to "don't interrupt".

Oesophageal Cancer Guide · What the Evidence Says for Each Treatment · 17 references
#Evidence#Chemotherapy#Side Effects#Decisions

Same cell type, 10 to 16 Gy more, and no more control in return — the United States, the Netherlands, France and China each tested it once, and Japan moved its own standard back from 60 Gy to 50.4. This article lines up each trial's population, regimen and direction of result, then turns to chemotherapy combinations and interruptions.

"Can't you turn the dose up a bit and burn the tumour out more cleanly?" I get asked this almost every week in the oesophageal cancer clinic, usually by someone who already knows they have squamous cell carcinoma, locally advanced, and is about to start five to six weeks of concurrent chemoradiotherapy. The intuition is perfectly reasonable: radiation is there to kill tumour, so more of it should kill more. But oesophageal cancer is one of the few cancers where that intuition has been taken into randomised trials and tested over and over — thirty years of testing, and the answer has pointed the same way every time. Below I go through those trials in order; by the end you will see that the real point of the "dose" question is "don't interrupt".

My position first: concurrent chemoradiotherapy and proton therapy for oesophageal cancer are treatments I deliver myself, every week; the other side of every comparison in this topic is my thoracic-surgery colleagues' operation and my gastroenterology colleagues' endoscopy. That is why every comparison comes with its primary source, so you can take it to the surgical and gastroenterology clinics for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

Fifty gray with chemotherapy beat sixty-four gray of radiation alone

RTOG 85-01 is where this whole field starts. The randomised part, published in NEJM in 1992, enrolled 121 patients with thoracic oesophageal cancer (squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma both included): one arm had 5-FU plus cisplatin with concurrent radiotherapy to 50 Gy, the other radiotherapy alone to 64 Gy. Median survival 12.5 against 8.9 months, two-year survival 38% against 10%; the price was severe side effects in 44% against 25% and life-threatening side effects in 20% against 3%[1]. The long-term follow-up in JAMA in 1999: five-year survival in the randomised part was 26% for the combined arm and 0% for radiotherapy alone[2]. Look at the shape of that result: the arm with the lower radiation dose lived longer, because it had the chemotherapy. From that day on, the standard in oesophageal cancer has not been to push radiation to the maximum but to give chemotherapy with around 50 Gy of radiation. The same dataset left behind a problem that has never been solved: the commonest mode of failure in the combined arm was still "persistent" tumour, at 26%, against 37% with radiotherapy alone[2]. Local disease not being cleared is the motive behind every dose-escalation trial that followed.

Five randomised trials, and not one of them showed that more is better

The first to test dose directly was the American INT 0123 (RTOG 94-05): 218 analysable patients (squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma, cisplatin/5-FU), 50.4 Gy against 64.8 Gy. Median survival 18.1 against 13.0 months, two-year survival 40% against 31%, local failure or persistence 52% against 56% — none of it statistically distinguishable; there were 11 treatment-related deaths in the high-dose arm against 2 in the standard arm, but 7 of those occurred before the dose had exceeded 50.4 Gy, so the high dose itself cannot simply be blamed; the trial was stopped after an interim analysis[3].

Over the next twenty years three trials changed the chemotherapy, changed the radiotherapy technique, and asked again. The Dutch ARTDECO trial (260 patients, 61% squamous cell carcinoma, 39% adenocarcinoma, weekly carboplatin/paclitaxel): 50.4 Gy against a boost to the primary tumour to 61.6 Gy, three-year local progression-free survival 70% against 73%, no difference; in the squamous subgroup 75% against 79%, in the adenocarcinoma subgroup 61% against 61%; grade 4 and grade 5 (fatal) toxicity 12%/5% against 14%/10%, p=0.15, not statistically significant[4]. The French CONCORDE/PRODIGE 26 (217 patients, 88% squamous cell carcinoma, FOLFOX): 50 Gy against 66 Gy, two-year locoregional progression-free survival 42.7% against 43.8%, median overall survival 25.2 against 23.5 months (hazard ratio 1.14, p=0.44) — a hazard ratio divides the speed at which events occur in one group by the speed in the other; above 1 means deaths in the high-dose arm came slightly faster, but here it has no statistical meaning. As of 2026 this trial exists only as a 2021 ASTRO conference abstract, with no full paper, so I label it at conference-abstract level[5]. The Chinese multicentre trial (319 analysable patients, all squamous cell carcinoma, stage IIA to IVA, weekly docetaxel/cisplatin): 50 Gy against 60 Gy, three-year locoregional progression-free survival 48.4% against 49.5% (hazard ratio 1.00), three-year overall survival 52.7% against 53.1%; grade 3 or higher radiation pneumonitis was clearly more frequent in the 60 Gy arm (p=0.03)[6]. The long-term follow-up of that trial came out in 2026, at a median of 99.5 months: eight-year locoregional progression-free survival 36.3% in the 50 Gy arm and 32.7% in the 60 Gy arm (hazard ratio 1.06, p=0.70), with survival, distant metastasis and patterns of failure all indistinguishable[7].

Here I want to be very precise. What these five datasets say is that escalation is not proven to be better — not that "50 Gy has been proven equal to 60 Gy". None of the confidence intervals is narrow, and not one of the trials was designed to prove equivalence. The opposite claim, "escalation kills people", also goes too far: ARTDECO's grade 5 toxicity did not reach statistical significance[4], and the CONCORDE authors themselves wrote that 66 Gy was no more toxic than 50 Gy[5]. The only sentence that can honestly be written down is this: the extra 10 to 16 Gy has, to this day, not bought better control, and two of the trials saw more treatment-related deaths or more grade 3 or higher pneumonitis in the high-dose arm[3][6].

Japan walked its own standard back from 60 to 50.4

Japan is the country most often held up as the counter-example. Traditional Japanese definitive chemoradiotherapy was cisplatin plus 5-FU with 60 Gy, but the Japan Esophageal Society's 2022 guideline is blunt about it: INT 0123 showed no further survival extension in the 64.8 Gy arm and more toxicity; their own JCOG0909 trial changed the dose to 1.8 Gy times 28 fractions, that is 50.4 Gy, for the stated reason "to reduce the risk of adverse events and the risks associated with salvage oesophagectomy", with a complete response rate of 59%[8]. That sentence gets to the point: the radiation dose affects not just the tumour but "how safely the operation can be done if you still need one after chemoradiotherapy" — which is exactly what "After chemoradiotherapy, do you still need surgery" will draw on.

Guidelines say 50.4 and practice often goes higher; that is not a Taiwanese peculiarity. A 2026 Austrian national survey: the median prescribed dose for definitive chemoradiotherapy of squamous cell carcinoma was 57.7 Gy, range 50.4 to 66[9]. For the doses actually prescribed for definitive chemoradiotherapy of squamous cell carcinoma in Taiwan, I could not find an official statistic, so I am not writing one. Nor is the "escalation" question completely closed: a Shanghai group is testing 61.2 against 50.4 Gy stratified by PET response (ESO-Shanghai 12), and so far there is only a trial-design paper, no results[10].

Which chemotherapy goes with it changes the kind of side effects, not survival

The randomised data on chemotherapy combinations point the same way: switching drugs did not buy longer survival, it bought different side effects. The French PRODIGE5/ACCORD17 (267 patients, squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma, 50 Gy): FOLFOX against cisplatin/5-FU, median progression-free survival 9.7 against 9.4 months, no difference; the FOLFOX arm had paraesthesia in 47% against 2% and neuropathy in 18% against 1%, the cisplatin/5-FU arm had raised kidney markers in 12% against 3% and mucositis in 32% against 27%[11]. Shanghai's ESO-Shanghai 1 (436 patients, all squamous cell carcinoma, 61.2 Gy): paclitaxel/5-FU against cisplatin/5-FU, three-year overall survival 55.4% against 51.8%, no difference; the paclitaxel arm had less anaemia, thrombocytopenia and nausea and vomiting, but more leucopenia, radiation dermatitis and radiation pneumonitis[12]. The weekly carboplatin/paclitaxel you often see in clinic has never been randomised against cisplatin/5-FU in definitive chemoradiotherapy; a Dutch retrospective series of 102 patients found median survival 16.1 against 13.8 months, indistinguishable, but a completion rate of 82% for carboplatin/paclitaxel against 57% for cisplatin/5-FU, with less grade 3 or higher haematological and non-haematological toxicity[13]. So in clinic I put it this way: which combination you get depends on your kidney function, your nerves, your hearing and whether you can get through the six weeks — not on which one is more effective.

"The oesophagitis hurts so much — can I rest for a few days?" Why I won't nod

The two weeks when the oesophagitis hurts most are exactly when patients want to pause. In this section I lay out both sides of the evidence. Oesophageal cancer's own data come in two pieces that point different ways: a 1994 Kyoto series of 88 patients from the radiotherapy-alone era found that every extra day of overall treatment time cost 2.3 percentage points of local control[14]; a 2025 Xi'an retrospective of 541 squamous cell carcinoma patients on concurrent chemoradiotherapy, median overall treatment time 43 days, 4 days longer than the theoretical minimum, found no effect of prolonged treatment time on survival or local control in multivariable analysis[15]. The head-and-neck squamous cell carcinoma evidence is harder, but it is an extrapolation: the DAHANCA 6 and 7 randomised trials, 1,476 patients, same dose and same number of fractions, six fractions a week against five, overall treatment time 39 against 46 days, five-year locoregional control 70% against 60%[16]; a 2007 review estimated that in head and neck cancer each day of interruption costs 1.4% of local control[17].

So I am not going to tell you "one day of interruption costs so many percentage points" — that number has not been proven in the concurrent-chemoradiotherapy era of oesophageal cancer. What I will say is this: oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma and head-and-neck squamous cell carcinoma are the same cell type, and the mechanism by which a tumour speeds up its repopulation during a gap is the same; the oesophageal retrospective that saw no effect had a median delay of only 4 days, and reading it as evidence that "stopping for a few days is fine" is a misreading; and there is not a single dataset showing that interruption is safe. Which situations I will stop for, and which you must not stop for yourself, are written in "Getting through radiation oesophagitis".

The dose on your prescription sheet, and the reasons you are entitled to hear

On the day you sign the consent form, take out the prescription sheet and look at it. What total dose and how many fractions: if it is above 50.4 Gy, what is the reason — tumour position, or keeping the option of surgery afterwards, are both legitimate reasons, but you are entitled to hear them. Which chemotherapy goes with it, and why this one: the comparison is with your kidney function, nerves and fitness, not a ranking of effectiveness. One more question to ask: if a pause really is needed partway through, how will the sessions be made up? On the technology layer, today's basic model is photon intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), a routine technique in Taiwan; for the reimbursement details ask the medical affairs office; its comparison with protons is written in "Protons for oesophageal cancer: the case and where the trials stand". Whether to operate or not is not re-argued here — go to "After chemoradiotherapy, do you still need surgery".

From the physician

If your prescription is above 50.4 Gy there is no need to panic, but you can ask for the reason — tumour position and preserving the option of surgery are both legitimate answers. When you want to rest during the two most painful weeks of oesophagitis, ring radiation oncology first; do not simply fail to turn up. We have ways of making sessions up; nobody can make up the ones you skip.
