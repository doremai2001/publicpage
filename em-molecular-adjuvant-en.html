TREATMENT AND THE EVIDENCE

How molecular classification changes your treatment

The evidence for escalating p53-abnormal disease is a post-hoc analysis; in PORTEC-4a's molecular profile arm, 46% (168/367) were judged favourable and had no adjuvant treatment.

Endometrial Cancer Guide · Treatment and the evidence · 10 references
#Molecular Testing#Evidence#Decisions#Clinical Trials

One randomised trial now supports de-escalating by molecular profile, but what it proves is non-inferiority, and the trial's "favourable profile" is not the same thing as a POLE mutation. This article also writes the other half: for most early-stage patients, the recommendation after testing is the same one.

In clinic, molecular classification tends to get described in one of two extreme ways: "everybody gets tested now, you're behind the times if you don't", or "testing changes nothing, why spend the money". Neither is right. The accurate version is that in a few small groups it genuinely changes the direction of treatment, and in most early-stage patients it does not — and you need to know which side you are on. The names of the four groups and how they are assigned are in "Two kinds of endometrial cancer, now four molecular groups". This article is only about the next step: whether it changes the treatment you are going to have.

My position first: vaginal brachytherapy and pelvic radiotherapy for endometrial cancer are treatments I deliver myself, every week; the other side of every comparison in this topic is my gynaecological-oncology colleagues' surgery and my gynaecological-oncology and medical-oncology colleagues' drugs. That is why every comparison comes with its primary source, so you can take it back to the gynaecological oncology clinic for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

The four groups differ in prognosis so widely they hardly look like one disease

The hardest set of numbers comes from PORTEC-3, a randomised trial in a high-risk population: 411 of the 660 women (62%) had specimens that could be molecularly classified — MMRd 139, NSMP 122, p53-abnormal 99, POLE-mutated 51. At 10 years, overall survival by group was POLE-mutated 98.0% (94.3 to 100.0), NSMP 77.9% (70.7 to 85.8), MMRd 71.7% (64.2 to 80.0) and p53-abnormal 45.1% (36.2 to 56.1). In a multivariable analysis with MMRd as the reference, the adjusted hazard ratio for POLE-mutated was 0.072 (0.01 to 0.53, p=0.0090) and for p53-abnormal 2.83 (1.71 to 4.68, p less than 0.0001)[1]. The string in brackets is the 95% confidence interval: the range within which the true value plausibly sits, and the wider it is, the fewer people it rests on and the less can be said firmly.

Note that these women were all at high risk (FIGO 2009), not all endometrial cancer patients, so the percentages cannot be moved straight across to yourself. What they prove is something else: among people classed as high risk and given the same treatment, molecular group alone can put more than fifty percentage points between their 10-year survival. That is why the 2025 European three-society guideline (ESGO/ESTRO/ESP) says "molecular classification should be done for all types of endometrial carcinoma (IV, A)", adding that it is particularly relevant in high-grade carcinomas[2].

p53-abnormal: the direction is clear, but this is a post-hoc analysis

In the same 10-year data, the gap between adding chemotherapy and radiotherapy alone in p53-abnormal disease is this: 10-year overall survival 52.7% (40.8 to 68.1) against 36.6% (25.0 to 53.7), adjusted hazard ratio 0.52 (0.30 to 0.91), p=0.021. 10-year recurrence-free survival was 52.6% against 37.0%, adjusted hazard ratio 0.42 (0.24 to 0.74), p=0.0027[1]. This is the strongest escalation signal in the field.

But it has to be written with one sentence attached to it: this is a post-hoc analysis. PORTEC-3 did not randomise by molecular group in the first place; these comparisons were made after the data were in, and they carry less weight than a pre-specified primary endpoint[1]. So the correct way to put it is that the direction has support and the size is still being verified. "p53-abnormal means you must have chemotherapy" does not stand at present; whether to add anything further is exactly what the RAINBO programme is asking.

MMRd: this must not be written as "chemotherapy helps here too"

In the same 10-year data, the MMRd patients look like this: chemoradiotherapy 68.7% (58.1 to 81.2) against radiotherapy alone 74.4% (64.4 to 86.0), adjusted hazard ratio 1.34 (0.71 to 2.55), p=0.37[1]. Three statements are needed and not one of them can be dropped: the point estimate runs in the unfavourable direction, it is not statistically significant, and there are only 139 women. So this can neither be written as "MMRd benefits from chemotherapy too" nor turned round into "MMRd does not need chemotherapy". The guideline uses a double negative here: "there is currently not enough evidence for not recommending chemo-radiotherapy followed by chemotherapy in this group" — that is, there is not yet enough evidence to recommend against chemoradiotherapy[2]. That MMR line is also a hereditary clue, and that layer is in "Lynch syndrome: what the test means for you and your family".

The POLE-mutated box was 100.0% against 96.4% (log-rank p=0.40): adding chemotherapy makes no visible difference, because this group barely recurs in the first place. The authors' conclusion is carefully worded. Irrespective of stage, and on the basis of excellent recurrence-free survival, de-escalation "should be recommended" for POLE-mutated cancers, and preferably in a study setting[1].

De-escalation is no longer only an idea: PORTEC-4a has read out

PORTEC-4a was a randomised, open-label, phase 3 non-inferiority trial in 8 European countries, in women with early-stage high-intermediate-risk disease. After surgery they were assigned 2:1 to "individualised adjuvant treatment by molecular integrated risk profile" or to "standard vaginal brachytherapy"; 564 were evaluable (367/197), median age 69.0 years, median follow-up 58.1 months. The molecular profile has three boxes: favourable, observation with no adjuvant treatment; intermediate, vaginal brachytherapy 21 Gy in 3 fractions; unfavourable, pelvic radiotherapy. In the molecular profile arm, 168 women (46%) were judged favourable and had no adjuvant treatment at all[3].

The primary endpoint was the 5-year cumulative incidence of vaginal recurrence (a cumulative incidence adds up the proportion of events occurring over a period). It was 4.5% (2.23 to 6.76) in the molecular profile arm against 1.6% (0.00 to 3.32) in the standard arm, hazard ratio 2.71 (0.79 to 9.34); the one-sided upper bound on the difference, 5.3%, was below the pre-set non-inferiority margin of 7.0%, non-inferiority p=0.005[3]. Adverse events were mostly grade 1 to 2 in both arms; grade 3 or worse genitourinary toxicity was 4/367 against 4/197[3].

I want to take this result slowly, because three things in it are easy to read backwards, and the result has to be read together with its limits. First, what it proves is non-inferiority — non-inferiority proves that the difference did not exceed a line agreed in advance, which is "not much worse", not "as good as". Second, vaginal recurrence in the favourable group was 4.1% (0.81 to 7.37) against 0.9% (0.00 to 2.78), hazard ratio 3.97 (0.48 to 32.95): the point estimate is fourfold, and it fell inside the margin only because the denominator is small and the interval so wide that it carries almost no information[3]. Last, and the piece most often misread: favourable here is not the same as POLE-mutated. It is defined as "POLE-mutated or NSMP and CTNNB1 wild-type", and the whole risk profile also counts in L1CAM expression and substantial LVSI (substantial lymphovascular space invasion)[3]. There is also a gap in time to know about: the 2025 guideline was published in July 2025 and PORTEC-4a in January 2026, so the guideline has not yet taken this result in[2].

RAINBO's four colours are all still recruiting

The programme that will actually answer "what should be added and what taken away, group by group" is RAINBO, a set of four trials each with a colour. p53abn-RED gives olaparib for two years after chemoradiotherapy, against chemoradiotherapy alone, with 554 women planned. MMRd-GREEN gives radiotherapy with concurrent and adjuvant durvalumab for a year, against radiotherapy alone, 316 planned. NSMP-ORANGE is de-escalation: progestin for two years after radiotherapy, against chemoradiotherapy, 600 planned. POLEmut-BLUE is de-escalation too, with no adjuvant treatment; the protocol paper puts it at 145[4].

The protocol paper said at the time that "main results are expected from 2028", but the timelines in the registry run later. The NCT05255653 umbrella trial is still recruiting, with an estimated enrolment of 1,615; primary completion is January 2030, overall completion January 2031, and there are no results yet[5]. RAINBO-RED was registered separately as a phase 3 trial only in 2024, 554 women, primary completion June 2030[6]. The POLE-mutated and NSMP arms, separate in the protocol paper, were later registered together as one trial (RAINBO BLUE & TAPER), 393 women in total, primary completion December 2028[7]. So the answers will come, but not this year — and whoever is deciding this year is still working from those post-hoc analyses and one non-inferiority trial.

For most early-stage patients, this will not change your treatment

This sentence I have to write, because it heads off a good deal of anxiety and a good deal of unnecessary out-of-pocket testing. A prospective real-world study followed 122 women after surgery with FIGO 2023 stage I to II disease, and took molecular classification into the multidisciplinary team meeting. The recommendation changed in only 25 cases (20.5%) — 15 escalations (12.3%) and 10 de-escalations (8.2%); the changes were concentrated in the TP53-abnormal group (62.5%, against 10.2% in the remaining subgroups), and there was not a single treatment change among the NSMP or POLE-mutated cases[8]. That study is single-institution, in India, 122 women, FIGO 2023 stage I to II only, and its risk stratification still used the 2016 version: the level of evidence is not high. But the direction matches what the guideline says about itself. The 2025 version writes that access to POLE testing is not uniformly available and may cost more, so "POLE mutation analysis may be omitted in low-risk stage I EC if the POLE mutational status does not influence the adjuvant treatment decision"[2]. Put plainly: for most early-stage patients the recommendation after testing is the same one — four in five in that study, though it is 122 women at a single institution, so the number itself is not worth memorising and the direction is.

Three lines to find when you read the report

First line: the immunohistochemistry result for the MMR proteins (is any of them lost). Second line: the p53 staining pattern (normal type or abnormal type). Third line: whether POLE sequencing was done, and what it showed. Those three lines, together with your stage and the degree of LVSI, are what decide which risk group you go into. Check which version of staging the report uses, for the reason given in "The staging changed; your treatment does not change with it"; the risk groups themselves are listed in "After the operation, do you still need radiotherapy". Once you have found the three lines, ask two questions: "will the molecular result in my box change the recommendation you would otherwise make?" And if it will: "is what changes the direction or the intensity, and is the evidence a randomised trial or a post-hoc analysis?"

At the Taiwan end I will say plainly what I found. The NHI has three next-generation sequencing items, 30301B to 30303B, but a full-text search of the appendix listing the reimbursed cancers returns zero hits for 子宮 (uterus)[9]. Nor could I find any item specific to MMR, microsatellites or POLE anywhere in the fee schedule, only the general 25012B, "immunohistochemical staining (per antibody)", 1,354 points. The note on that item sets rules about the number of antibodies, quoted word for word in "Lynch syndrome: what the test means for you and your family"[10]. Not finding a listed item named after endometrial cancer does not mean it cannot be claimed, and it does not mean you have to pay for it yourself — how it is actually claimed, and whether anything is self-paid, is a question for the medical affairs office or your case manager.

From the physician

Do not pay out of pocket for a test that will not change your treatment because "everyone else has had it". When the report comes, find three lines: MMR, p53, POLE. Then ask one question: will those three lines change the recommendation you would otherwise make? And if they will, is what changes the direction or the intensity?
