CHOOSING THE TECHNIQUE

Heart dose: the core question on the left side

The line that says every extra 1 Gy raises the risk by 7.4% is real; so is the fact that techniques you do not have to pay extra for bring the mean heart dose for the left breast to somewhere around 2 Gy.

Breast Radiotherapy Guide · Choosing the Technique · 10 references
#Heart Dose#Breath-Hold#Evidence#Costs & Self-Pay

The skeleton of this article is a chain of evidence: the relationship between dose and events comes from two cohort studies, and how far the dose can be brought down comes from a dosimetric meta-analysis — the two pieces sit at different levels of evidence, and I label each one. Not everyone can manage a breath-hold, and the direct evidence at the level of cardiac events is still empty; both of those are written as they are.

"The tumour is on the left — will the heart be in the beam?" Patients with left-sided breast cancer who walk into a radiation oncology clinic usually get that question out within their first three sentences. It is a good question, and the answer has two layers. The relationship between heart dose and cardiac events rests on cohort evidence at the scale of thousands of women, and it is real; at the same time, how far current technique pushes the dose down, how much more a breath-hold takes off, which of those figures are measured doses and which have no event-level evidence behind them yet — all of that has numbers too. This article lays the whole chain out and labels it box by box.

My position first: every technique compared in this topic — ordinary intensity-modulated radiotherapy, TOMO, deep inspiration breath-hold — has a self-paid component among the more expensive options, and all of them are delivered in my own department; protons mean a referral elsewhere. So every "better" here comes with its primary source and the level of evidence behind it, and on cost I cite the official schedule where one exists and tell you to ask the medical affairs office where it does not. Concluding that the basic option is enough for you is a completely reasonable conclusion.

Every extra Gy multiplies the risk a little — this is how that line was drawn

The anchor for the cardiac question is the Darby study published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2013: a population-based case-control study of 2,168 women who had radiotherapy for breast cancer in Sweden and Denmark between 1958 and 2001 — 963 who had a major coronary event (myocardial infarction, coronary revascularisation, death from ischaemic heart disease) and 1,205 controls, with the heart dose reconstructed woman by woman from the radiotherapy records of the day. Mean whole-heart dose in that cohort averaged 4.9 Gy; the rate of major coronary events rose linearly with mean heart dose, a relative increase of 7.4% for every additional 1 Gy (95% confidence interval 2.9 to 14.5 — a confidence interval is the range within which the estimate can reasonably swing); the study saw no obvious safe lower threshold, and the risk began to rise within five years of radiotherapy and carried on into the third decade[1]. Two things need labelling. First, that is a proportional figure, not "7.4 more people in trouble per 1 Gy" — how high the absolute risk is to start with depends on your own cardiovascular condition, and the same study states that women who already had cardiovascular risk factors had the larger absolute increase[1]. Second, 4.9 Gy is the average produced by the technology of 1958 to 2001, not today's; today's numbers are in the next two sections.

Redraw it with modern doses and the slope is still there

"Technique has moved on — isn't that line out of date?" Someone has actually redrawn it. In 2017 van den Bogaard's group in the Netherlands followed 910 women treated with radiotherapy after breast-conserving surgery, all with complete 3D dose data, for a median of 7.6 years: the median mean heart dose in this cohort was already only 2.37 Gy, the cumulative incidence of acute coronary events still rose with dose, a relative increase of 16.5% for every additional 1 Gy (95% confidence interval 0.6 to 35.0, p=.042), and the authors state plainly that the relative increase per Gy calculated on mean heart dose is similar to Darby's[2]. This cohort had only 30 events and the interval is wide, so do not commit 16.5% to memory too firmly; its meaning is in the direction — within the modern dose range, the relationship between dose and events has not gone away. So "lower dose is better" is not a marketing line, it is what two cohort studies stacked on each other come to; and equally it cannot be read as "you cannot protect the heart without paying", because what the basic option's doses actually look like is the next set of numbers.

The NHI basic option already builds the heart into the plan

Lay the publication years out and the mean heart dose for the left breast has been coming down all along: across 149 studies published between 2003 and 2013, the left-sided average was 5.4 Gy[3]; across 99 studies published between 2014 and 2017, the left-sided average was down to 3.6 Gy, and among the studies published in 2017 the average was only 2.6 Gy; in that same review, 1.7 Gy with respiratory control and 4.5 Gy without[4]. Measured data point the same way: one single institution laid out the doses of 4,687 patients treated between 2011 and 2018, with a median mean heart dose of 2.16 Gy for left-sided conventional fractionation (50 Gy in 25 fractions) and 1.47 Gy for hypofractionation (42.6 Gy in 16 fractions)[5]; the Dutch cohort in the previous section, mostly 3D technique and with no particular use of breath-hold, had a median of 2.37 Gy[2]. These results come from 3D conformal and ordinary intensity-modulated radiotherapy — that is, the class of technique Taiwan's National Health Insurance (NHI) pays for, not the machines you have to pay extra for. Choosing the beam angles, setting the dose constraints, contouring the heart at planning as an organ to be protected: all of that is already being done without a self-paid item, and the heart-sparing of ordinary intensity-modulated radiotherapy does real work. Translated into absolute risk: the EBCTCG group used modern doses (a mean whole-heart dose of 4.4 Gy from the 2010 to 2015 literature) to estimate the absolute risk of cardiac death caused by radiotherapy at about 0.3% in a non-smoker and about 1% in a long-term smoker — that is a modelled estimate, not a measurement, but it puts "7.4% per Gy" back on a scale; the same paper concludes that stopping smoking can change the net benefit of radiotherapy[6].

Why the left side — and why the right side is not exempt

Mean heart dose is intrinsically higher on the left: in the same 2003 to 2013 systematic review, 5.4 Gy on the left against 3.3 Gy on the right[3]. A bigger variable than side is the internal mammary nodes: left-sided treatment that includes the internal mammary chain averages about 8 Gy, against 4.2 Gy without — the review's own concluding sentence is that including the internal mammary nodes doubles the typical heart dose[3]. So the right side is not a side where the heart can be left alone: when the internal mammary nodes are being treated, the right-sided heart dose has to be laid out and looked at as well. The clinical-event figures by side come from the Danish DBCG IMN2 prospective cohort (4,541 node-positive patients, median follow-up 13.7 years): it allocated internal mammary irradiation by side — treated on the right, not treated on the left, the design reason being precisely to avoid the left-sided heart dose — and the fifteen-year cumulative incidence of death from ischaemic or valvular heart disease was 0.2% on the right and 0.7% on the left[7]. That is a prospective cohort, not a randomised allocation; the case for and against treating the internal mammary nodes at all I have written in the breast cancer topic, in "Should the axilla and supraclavicular area be treated too?", and I am not repeating it here.

Deep inspiration breath-hold: what comes down is the dose, and that word has to be labelled

Deep inspiration breath-hold (DIBH) is a posture, not a machine: take a full breath in, the diaphragm drops, the chest expands, and the heart is pulled away from the chest wall and out of the field. It can be layered on top of any photon technique, and how the techniques stack together is in "The machine, and the breathing". The reduction is measurable: a review estimates that the mean dose to the whole heart and to the left anterior descending artery (LAD) falls by about 20 to 70%, and it varies a lot between people[8]; a meta-analysis of 12 observational studies and 1,019 left-sided patients found heart and LAD doses significantly lower in the breath-hold group, with no difference in target coverage[9]. Everything to this point is a dosimetric endpoint. Here is the sentence that has to be written honestly: direct evidence that DIBH reduces cardiac events — a randomised trial that has read out, or a large cohort comparison — I could find none. The right way to write this chain is in two pieces: the dose reduction is measurable (dosimetric meta-analysis); the relationship between dose and events is cohort-level evidence (Darby, van den Bogaard). Both pieces are solid, but they are two pieces, and they cannot be sewn into "breath-hold has been shown to reduce heart attacks". Applicability has to be said straight as well: not everyone can do a breath-hold. In one study, of 72 patients who had already been pre-selected and trained, 29% still could not complete the breath-hold planning scan; in another, 20 of 112 could not tolerate an active breathing control system; the review estimates that about 75% of patients who do a breath-hold gain a dosimetric benefit[8]. Not managing it is nobody's failure — planning goes back to heart-sparing under free breathing; how the breath-hold is practised, and what actually happens on planning day, is in "From planning scan to first treatment".

Taiwan: what the NHI pays for, and who to ask about the cost of a breath-hold

The basic option is covered. Breast radiotherapy is billed as linear accelerator field items plus computerised treatment planning; the provision for "computerised treatment planning — complex" (36015B) explicitly covers the design of techniques including intensity-modulated radiotherapy[10]. Whole-breast hypofractionation after breast-conserving surgery has bundled items of its own, 36022B and 36023B, and the detail of their indications and fractionation is in "Why am I not on the shorter course?". As for the breath-hold: searching the full NHI fee schedule table, I found no item at all for respiratory gating or breath-hold[10]. So do individual hospitals set up self-paid items of their own, under what name and at what amount? I could find no official announcement from any hospital — so the only honest way to write this box is one sentence: ask the medical affairs office at your treating hospital to confirm the item name and the amount. Not finding an item in the full table cannot be turned round into "so it must be self-paid", and it cannot be turned round into "so it costs nothing" either; all it means is that I am not inventing a number.

At your next appointment, take the phrase "mean heart dose" in with you

The use of this article is very concrete: print it, circle Darby's 7.4% per Gy and the Dutch cohort's 2.37 Gy, and take it to your next appointment. The phrase I most want you to remember is "mean heart dose" — it is a number that exists the moment your plan is finished, you can ask for it, and you can hold it against any number in this article. While you are there, ask the team to assess whether you are suitable for a deep inspiration breath-hold, and what the plan would be adjusted to if you cannot manage it; and if a self-paid item comes up, ask the medical affairs office to list the name and the amount for you in writing. The cardiac question on the left side is not a scare story and it is not an upsell page — it is an engineering problem that can be quantified, planned for and asked about out loud, and you are entitled to see your own numbers.

From the physician

Being on the left is not a verdict; what matters is the numbers in your own plan. At your next appointment ask straight out, "what is my mean heart dose in Gy?", and ask the team to assess whether you are suitable for a deep inspiration breath-hold. If a self-paid item comes up, ask the medical affairs office to list the name and the amount in writing.
