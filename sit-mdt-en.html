THE FIRST MONTH

Has my case been discussed by a team?

The NHI writes team discussion into some reimbursement rules. Across 64 observed meetings the least-discussed item was the patient's own view: 1.12.

Situation Guides · The First Month · 7 references
#The system#Getting Ready#How to ask#Decisions

In that room the same images and the same pathology go up on one screen, a group of people look at them, and an order comes out. This is about what the room does, what traces the Taiwanese system leaves, how far the evidence goes, and what nobody will raise for you unless you raise it in clinic.

By the time the treatment plan reaches you it is usually already a conclusion: surgery first or radiotherapy first, chemotherapy added or not, starting when. Some people want to know how that conclusion was reached — whether one person decided it, or a group worked out an order after looking at the same images and the same pathology. It is a good question, and it can be answered.

What the law requires is a committee, not a case-by-case discussion of you

Article 15 of the Cancer Control Act (癌症防治法) reads: "A cancer control medical institution shall establish internally a cancer care quality group (癌症醫療品質小組), so as to ensure the quality of its cancer screening and of its diagnosis and treatment."[1] Note that this is a standing committee, not a meeting held case by case — and the two collapse into one impression just as easily in English, because "quality group" and "team meeting" both sound like a room with people in it. Article 29 of the Cancer Care Quality Assurance Measures Regulations (癌症診療品質保證措施準則), made under the same Act, requires institutions to join the quality assurance programmes the central competent authority runs for five subjects: mammography, cervical cytology, colposcopy, pathology and hospice care[2]. Also institutional-level quality work, not something aimed at your case.

For some items, the NHI writes "discussed by a multidisciplinary team" into the reimbursement conditions

This is the most concrete set of traces in the Taiwanese system. The NHI fee schedule (支付標準) is a single-payer rulebook, so a payment rule doubles as a statement of what the system requires. In the full table published by the National Health Insurance Administration (健保署), next-generation sequencing in solid tumours and haematological malignancies (30301B to 30305B, effective 1 December 2024) has an institutional eligibility column requiring "a regional-level or higher hospital, or one publicly announced by the competent authority as having passed cancer care quality certification", and "an in-house or cross-hospital molecular tumour board (分子腫瘤委員會)" — this is the NHI's administrative condition on which institutions may claim for this test, not a ranking of hospitals and not a suggestion that you change hospital; where such a test really is needed, whether and how to refer is a sentence you can put directly to the clinic. For EGFR and ALK testing in lung cancer, the indication at stage ⅢB and stage ⅢC reads "discussed by a lung cancer multidisciplinary team, not completely resectable by surgery and not suitable for chemoradiotherapy"; and sequencing for one class of T-cell or NK-cell lymphoma goes as far as requiring the minutes of the multidisciplinary discussion to be attached to the claim[3].

Two things go alongside that. First, these requirements appear only on specific tests and a small number of items; they are not a condition of reimbursement for cancer treatment generally. Second, they mean a team discussion leaves a record inside the system. So "has my case been discussed" is a question with an answer, not an abstract hope.

The certification standard itself is the thing I could not retrieve

Let me put this first: a meeting having been held does not mean your case was discussed in detail, and does not mean the conclusion is right. This piece is not telling you that places with team meetings are better. It is telling you what this arrangement looks like, how far the evidence goes, and how you can ask.

The competent authority does publish a description of cancer care quality certification (癌症診療品質認證): run since 2008 (97 年) for hospitals with 500 or more newly diagnosed cancer cases, with conditions for passing that include "a hospital-wide cancer committee made up of medical and administrative units across departments with expertise related to cancer care", and "a cancer multidisciplinary care team, bringing together medical and surgical oncology, radiation oncology, pathology, diagnostic imaging and oncology case managers, taking part jointly in the treatment plan"[4].

But the official full text of the certification standards and the scoring notes I did not obtain this time, by any of three routes (the Health Promotion Administration website unreachable, the National Health Research Institutes' accreditation site disallowing automated retrieval across the whole site, the Ministry of Health and Welfare law retrieval system returning an error). So there is one sentence I will not write: whether the certification requires that "every patient's case must go to a team meeting". I will not say that it requires it, and I will not say that it does not. For what your own hospital does in practice, ask your case manager (個管師). The institutional material above was verified on 30 August 2026; the NHI fee schedule cited is the 114.03.13 edition of the full table, payment rules go through new editions, and the actual conditions are whatever the NHIA has most recently announced.

Does it help? Every study points the same way, and the sizes will not go together

A systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2025 searched to 7 June 2024 with no language or date limit, identified 179 comparative studies out of 2,832 titles, and of those 37 (56,187 patients in total) reported a mortality hazard ratio from a multivariable analysis. The result has two halves: all 37 pointed towards lower mortality among patients whose cases had been through a team meeting, but the estimates scattered to an I² of 87% — the variation between studies is too large for them simply to be added together, and the range runs from almost no difference all the way down to a hazard ratio of only one-tenth. Only two subgroups came down to low-to-moderate heterogeneity: breast cancer at a hazard ratio of 0.86, and hepatocellular carcinoma at 0.82 (a hazard ratio compares the rate at which events happen in two groups, and 1 means the same rate)[5].

All 37 are observational comparisons. Cases taken to a discussion differ to begin with from cases that are not — age, stage, performance status, whether the case is complicated. So the correct statement is "there is an association", not "the meeting saved you".

What gets covered fully at the meeting is the imaging and the pathology, not you

This is the part I think patients most need to know. Three Australian academic-affiliated institutions ran a prospective structured observation across 14 kinds of multidisciplinary meeting, scored by two trained observers on a validated instrument (1 means not discussed at all, 5 means discussed fully), covering 64 meetings and 498 patients, 99% of whom had a management decision made on the spot. Mean scores: history 4.60, imaging 4.10, pathology 3.73 — and comorbidity 1.69, psychosocial circumstances 1.27, the patient's own views 1.12[6].

That does not mean nobody attends to your state of mind or your wishes. It means those things happen in clinic, not in the meeting. How well your diabetes is controlled, whether anyone at home can drive you in, whether you could manage five days a week — if you do not say it in clinic, it is unlikely anyone will say it for you in the meeting.

A meeting was held is not the same as the meeting was followed

A Japanese university hospital matched its meeting minutes from 2012 to 2017 against the electronic records: 1,813 cases discussed, 66% recommended radiotherapy by the meeting, and 7% of the recommendations in the end not carried out. The reasons, in order: the clinician's opinion 30%, the patient's wishes 27%, disease progression 20%, a change of stage 12%[7]. This is single-institution data on radiotherapy only, but it makes one thing plain: the meeting's conclusion is a recommendation, and between it and you sit changes in your disease and choices of your own.

This one sentence, they can answer

"Has my case been to a team meeting? If it has, what was the conclusion? If it has not yet, which investigation is it waiting on?" That sentence carries no confrontation, and it can be answered.

One thing to say alongside it: the team meeting is an internal process on the medical side, not a gate you have to reach first. Investigations and treatment already scheduled go ahead as planned; do not postpone or cancel anything on your own to wait for a meeting's conclusion. If you have doubts about what the meeting recommended, say so and discuss it together — do not unilaterally stop treatment that has already started.

Two things to hold together. First, not every case needs to go to a meeting — in many situations the treatment path is entirely clear and discussing it case by case would change nothing, so not having been to a meeting does not mean being taken lightly. Second, a cross-specialty consultation and a team meeting are not the same thing: the first brings one department in to see you, the second is a group of people in one time slot looking at the same material. Getting your other illnesses, your household situation and your own worries into that process mostly depends on your saying them out loud in clinic, and on your case manager passing them on. The order of the whole first month is in "The first month: the boxes that actually have to be ticked"; wanting another opinion, getting records out or having the pathology re-reviewed is in "Asking for a second opinion, out loud"; why an older patient needs a separate set of functional assessments is in "Eighty years old: treat, or not?".

From the physician

What this gives you is one sentence: has my case been to a team meeting, what was the conclusion, and if not yet, which investigation is it waiting on? Not having been to a meeting does not mean being taken lightly — many paths are clear already. Comorbidity, help at home and your own worries mostly depend on your saying them out loud in clinic.
