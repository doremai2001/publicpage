SPECIAL SITUATIONS

Weight, diabetes and this cancer

Relative risk about 1.6 per 5 kg/m² of BMI, and a population attributable fraction of 34.0% in UK women; weight loss lowering incidence has observational evidence, weight loss lowering recurrence has no randomised trial.

Endometrial Cancer Guide · Special situations · 20 references
#Nutrition#Exercise#Prevention#Evidence

One box in this article is blank — whether losing weight lowers recurrence in someone already diagnosed has never been answered by a randomised trial. I have written that box as blank, and marked what strength of evidence each of the other boxes stands on, and what the Taiwanese clauses actually say.

A risk factor is a statistic epidemiology works out across a very large number of people. It is not a personal fault. Talking about weight in clinic slides easily into a post-mortem, and a post-mortem does nothing for treatment. The link between weight and whether this disease occurs is strong; the link between losing weight and incidence is moderate; and the box for losing weight and whether someone already diagnosed relapses is empty. Three different strengths — they cannot be said in the same tone of voice.

That BMI line is not a straight one

Two independent large meta-analyses landed on the same order of magnitude. The one covering 141 prospective studies and 282,137 incident cancers: in women, each 5 kg/m² of BMI came with a relative risk for endometrial cancer of 1.59 (p less than 0.0001)[1]. The one done specifically for endometrial cancer (24 prospective studies, 17,710 cases) gives 1.60 (95% confidence interval 1.52 to 1.68)[2]. A relative risk is one group's incidence divided by another's — not "your chance of getting the disease".

Drawn as a curve, the shape carries more information than the average. Against a normal BMI, BMI 27 is 1.22 times (1.19 to 1.24), 32 is 2.09, 37 is 4.36, and 42 is 9.11 (7.26 to 11.51). The curve is also modified by hormone replacement therapy — 1.90 (1.57 to 2.31) in women who never used it, 1.18 (1.06 to 1.31) in those who had, interaction p=0.003. The authors read this as supporting an excess-oestrogen mechanism, while the residual risk in users points to other systems being involved as well[2].

In the UK attributable analysis for 2015, overweight and obesity accounted for 6.3% of all cancers, and cancer of the uterine corpus had the highest attributable proportion of any cancer in UK women, 34.0%[3]. A population attributable fraction is a population-level estimate — how much a population could in theory be spared if the factor were absent — not a verdict on any one person.

Diabetes is a separate line, and it does not only run through the cancer

A meta-analysis of 22 cohort and case-control studies put diabetes and endometrial cancer at 1.72 (1.48 to 2.01); across the 13 studies that adjusted for BMI as a confounder, the pooled risk was still 1.62 (1.34 to 1.97)[4]. A difference shows after diagnosis too: in a meta-analysis of 31 studies and 55,475 women with endometrial cancer, those with pre-existing diabetes had worse cancer-specific survival (hazard ratio 1.15, 1.00 to 1.32) and worse overall survival, 1.42 (1.31 to 1.54)[5] — the second being larger than the first means some of the extra deaths are not from this cancer.

The Australian ANECS cohort (1,359 women, median follow-up 7.1 years) points the same way: cancer-specific mortality in women with diabetes had a hazard ratio of 2.09 (1.31 to 3.35), and it was higher, 4.13 (2.20 to 7.76), in those with diabetes but without obesity[6]. The 2026 clinical practice statement of the Society of Gynecologic Oncology and the Society of American Gastrointestinal and Endoscopic Surgeons puts it this way: obesity is associated with worse oncologic and overall outcomes, with cardiovascular disease representing the leading cause of mortality "in this population"[7]. "In this population" means women with obesity and endometrial cancer; the qualifier cannot be dropped. Causes of death across all patients with this cancer are a different set of numbers — see "Life after treatment".

The polycystic ovary syndrome figure has to be read with the authors' warning attached. Across 11 studies, 919 women with polycystic ovary syndrome against 72,054 controls, the pooled odds ratio for endometrial cancer was 2.79 (1.31 to 5.95); excluding studies that included women over 54 raised it to 4.05. An odds ratio is not a multiple of a probability; and the authors themselves write that this evidence is "far from robust", that obesity and other associated factors together with selection bias "may have resulted in an exaggeration of the increased risk", and that any increase in risk must be judged in the context of this cancer's "relatively low incidence in the general population"[8].

Weight loss and incidence: how far this layer goes

22,198 people who had bariatric surgery against 66,427 who did not, matched on sex, age, site, BMI and comorbidity index, mean follow-up 3.5 years: hazard ratio 0.67 for any cancer and 0.50 (0.37 to 0.67) for endometrial cancer[9]. The Swedish SOS prospective matched study, median follow-up 18.1 years: endometrial cancer 0.56 (0.35 to 0.89), with the relative benefit of surgery larger the higher the baseline insulin (interaction p=0.022)[10]. Two meta-analyses give pooled odds ratios of 0.41[11] and 0.46, the authors of the more recent one writing that "study heterogeneity and biases highlight the need for more rigorous research"[12].

All of this is observational data; not one of these studies randomised anyone to lose weight, and people willing and suitable for surgery differ from those who do not have it to begin with. One finding inside the same meta-analysis points the other way: self-reported intentional weight loss gave relative risks from 0.61 to 0.96, with definitions inconsistent enough that only a qualitative synthesis was possible, while self-reported weight cycling — losing weight and putting it back on — was associated with higher risk, odds ratios of 1.07 to 2.33[11].

"Lose weight and it won't come back" — I could not find evidence for that

I have looked: there is no randomised weight-loss trial in endometrial cancer survivors with recurrence or survival as its primary endpoint. What the searches return are pilot trials of behavioural interventions, with weight, behaviour and quality of life as endpoints. "Losing weight lowers recurrence" has no randomised evidence behind it today — that is not the same as saying it does not work; it is saying nobody has shown it.

Observational data can go this far and no further. A cohort of 1,400 showed that, against a BMI of 18.5 to 25, a pre-diagnosis BMI of 25 to 30 carried a 5-year all-cause mortality hazard ratio of 1.74, 30 to 35 gave 1.84, and 35 or above gave 2.35 (1.48 to 3.73), trend p less than 0.001; in the same study, women active for more than 7 hours a week had a hazard ratio of 0.57, but after adjusting for BMI this became 0.64 with a confidence interval crossing 1, and it was unrelated to disease-specific mortality[13]. Another cohort of 745 survivors gives 0.61 (0.41 to 0.92), the authors concluding that physical activity remains "still important for other non-mortality outcomes" for endometrial cancer survivors[14]. For the exercise question see "Move, or rest, during treatment", and for managing weight after treatment ends, "Life after treatment".

GLP-1 drugs and bariatric surgery: the clauses side by side, no inferences

GLP-1 receptor agonists have only observational data so far, and neither dataset was done on the treatment of endometrial cancer. An electronic health record cohort of women with endometrial hyperplasia or benign uterine conditions who were taking progestins compared 18,414 on a GLP-1 agonist plus progestin with 426,406 on progestin alone: hazard ratio for endometrial cancer 0.34 (0.27 to 0.44), with the qualifier "further investigation is warranted"[15]. The other took women aged 45 or under having fertility-sparing treatment for endometrial intraepithelial neoplasia or endometrial cancer, 432 in each arm after 1:1 matching, and found a hysterectomy rate at 18 months of 10.2% against 23.4%[16] — the endpoint is hysterectomy, not tumour response or survival, and the design is retrospective matching.

Taiwan. The Taiwan FDA has approved a weight-management indication; one semaglutide licence reads "as an adjunct to a reduced-calorie diet and increased physical activity, for adults with an initial body mass index (BMI) of ≥30 kg/m² (obesity), or ≥27 kg/m² to <30 kg/m² (overweight) with at least one weight-related comorbidity"[17]; clause 5.1.3.2 of the NHI drug reimbursement regulations reads "restricted to patients with type 2 diabetes who have received the maximum tolerated dose of metformin and/or a sulfonylurea and, after six continuous months of concurrent use of one of the following drugs, still have an HbA1c above 8.5%", and a search for tirzepatide in the reimbursement regulations returns 0 hits[18]. Those two passages sit side by side; how it is paid for is a question for the medical affairs office or your case manager.

Bariatric surgery does have an explicit clause. The indications for items 72051B, laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy, and 72052B, laparoscopic gastric bypass, in the NHI fee schedule read: "1. Indications (all of the following must be met): (1) body mass index BMI ≧37.5 kg/m²; or BMI ≧32.5 kg/m² with a high-risk complication, such as type 2 diabetes with HbA1c still ≧7.5% after medical treatment, hypertension, sleep apnoea syndrome, and so forth. (2) at least six months of weight-management clinic attendance (or six months of equivalent clinic documentation) and more than six months of exercise and dietary control. (3) age 20 years or above and under 66. … 2. Restricted to specialists in gastroenterological surgery"[19]. The high-risk complications the clause lists end with "and so forth", and endometrial cancer and endometrial hyperplasia are not named among them; how "and so forth" is interpreted is not something I can infer, and this one needs gastroenterological surgery and gynaecological oncology to assess together.

Weight changes what has to be prepared for surgery, not whether you are eligible

A cohort of 109 women with early endometrial cancer having robotic hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and sentinel lymph node mapping was split three ways (BMI 40 or above, 26 women; 30 to 39, 45; under 30, 38). Intra-operative complications, post-operative complications, conversion to open surgery, blood loss, intensive care days and length of stay: no difference between the groups. Two things did differ: operating time was significantly longer in the BMI 40 or above group (p=0.003), and bilateral sentinel node mapping succeeded in 73.1% of them, against 95.6% in the 30 to 39 group and 81.6% in the under-30 group (p=0.026). The authors conclude that robotic surgery is "safe and feasible" in this group, but that the longer operating times and lower mapping rate call for tailored strategies[20]. For surgery and the nodes, see "What the operation removes, and what happens to the lymph nodes".

That is what "preparation" means: the anaesthetic assessment, positioning, allowing more theatre time, a fallback for when mapping fails — homework for the team, not a threshold that decides whether you can be operated on. On the radiotherapy side, I could not find citable primary research on how obesity affects set-up or dose distribution in pelvic radiotherapy, so I am not writing it; for the general principles of set-up reproducibility see "What happens on the day of the planning scan" and "Daily imaging: the underrated half".

Three numbers worth digging out, more than the one on the scales

The first is HbA1c and fasting glucose: the diabetes line is independent, it acts on overall survival, and it is worth managing in its own right. The second is blood pressure and lipids — in this group of women, cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death, and in an oncology clinic that tends to end up last on the list. The third is your own history of weight change, including the times it came off and went back on; what the weight-cycling data means is that rushing it off and bouncing back is not necessarily better than holding steady. Three questions: who takes over managing my metabolic comorbidities? If metabolic surgery is to be considered, which specialty assesses it first? And for weight-loss drugs, which box does my situation fall into, under the licensed indications and the reimbursement clauses?

From the physician

Weight and diabetes are risk factors, not anyone's fault, and that is a sentence I say twice in clinic. What you can do today is dig out three numbers — HbA1c, blood pressure and lipids — and take them in with the question "who takes over managing my metabolic comorbidities?" In women with obesity and endometrial cancer, cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death, and it deserves a place in the diary.
