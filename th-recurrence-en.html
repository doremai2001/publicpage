FOLLOW-UP AND BEYOND

The number rises and nothing shows

One cohort saw a third of these patients walk back to no evidence of disease with no extra treatment at all; another saw a third of them grow something.

Thyroid Cancer Guide · Follow-up and beyond · 8 references
#Recurrence#Biomarkers#Imaging#Decisions

A number going up while imaging stays clean is a named state, not a doctor fobbing you off. Where it started and how it has been sloping separate those two groups better than any single reading — which is why one blood test never changes what I do.

The sentence I hear most often at follow-up is "doctor, my Tg has gone up". Trailing behind it, usually unspoken, is the real question — has it come back.

This article is about exactly that situation: the blood number is moving and imaging finds nothing. How follow-up is scheduled and how that number is read is in 〈Follow-up is an ultrasound and one number〉; if a lesion really is found and radioiodine no longer works on it, that is 〈After radioiodine stops working〉.

Three words that need separating first

Thyroglobulin (Tg) is a protein only thyroid cells make, so after the gland comes out it ought to be very low. The 2025 American Thyroid Association guideline sorts response to therapy into four categories, and two of them get confused with each other constantly[1]. A biochemical incomplete response is an abnormal Tg, or rising anti-thyroglobulin antibodies, with no localisable disease on imaging. A structural incomplete response is metastatic disease you can actually see, locoregional or distant. These are not the same event, and they are not handled the same way.

One more thing to settle before the numbers. People who had a lobectomy have no biochemical incomplete response box at all — the guideline's table simply prints N/A in that column[1]; a separate systematic review puts the reason more bluntly: with half a thyroid still in place, Tg measurement is not accurate[2]. What those patients should be followed with instead is in 〈Follow-up is an ultrasound and one number〉. Every figure below comes from people who had a total thyroidectomy.

Three independent datasets on how often this number comes down by itself

The first: 506 patients with differentiated thyroid cancer, all with total thyroidectomy and remnant ablation, median follow-up 10 years. Among those in the biochemical incomplete group, 34% were eventually reclassified as having no evidence of disease without receiving any additional treatment whatsoever; by contrast, of those with a structural incomplete response, even after further treatment only 9% ended up reclassified that way[3]. One cohort, one follow-up period, a clear denominator — 34% against 9% is the spine of this article.

The second: 299 patients with differentiated thyroid cancer, all with total thyroidectomy and remnant ablation, and none of whom received any other treatment afterwards, measured on nonstimulated Tg drawn while on the tablet. Among those sitting between 1 and 5 ng/mL at six months, 54% eventually fell below 1 with no additional treatment; and a quarter of the whole group took more than eighteen months to reach their nadir[4].

The third is Korean: 186 patients with differentiated thyroid cancer, total thyroidectomy plus remnant ablation, measured on Tg after thyroid hormone withdrawal — not the tube drawn at your ordinary clinic visit — all of whom were above 2 ng/mL a year after ablation with no structural disease on imaging. Split by that one-year value into three bands: those at 2 to 4.9 had spontaneous biochemical remission 41% of the time (median 69 months to get there), those at 5 to 19.9 17%, those at 20 and above 1%[5]. Where you start decides how reasonable waiting is.

The figures pointing the other way belong here too

I am not going to print only that half. Within those same 186 people, structural recurrence appeared in 24% of the 2 to 4.9 band, 30% of the 5 to 19.9 band and 50% of those at 20 and above, 32% overall; median time to recurrence in the highest band was 84 months[5]. The ranges the guideline collects point the same way: in adults with differentiated thyroid cancer after total thyroidectomy and radioiodine, with structural recurrence as the endpoint, the biochemical incomplete box runs 20% to 53%; the column for total thyroidectomy without radioiodine runs 0% to 31.6%[1].

Both directions are true, then. What separates the two groups is not any single reading but where it started and which way it has been sloping since: among those 186, patients whose stimulated value fell by more than half reached biochemical remission at a median of 63 months, while those falling by less than half, or rising, took a median of roughly 117 months — and their structural recurrence rates differed significantly too[5]. Which is why one blood test never changes what I do, and three consecutive ones pointing the same way can.

When nothing shows, how hard do you go looking

The guideline's order of operations is clear. When Tg or antibodies rise after a total thyroidectomy and the neck ultrasound shows no structural disease or only a minimal burden, cross-sectional imaging of the common metastatic sites, lungs and bone, should be added — which the guideline does not grade, listing it as good practice[1]. In oncocytic (Hürthle cell) carcinoma and poorly differentiated carcinoma in the same position, PET/CT may be considered (Conditional recommendation, low certainty evidence). Those who never had radioiodine should not have surveillance whole-body iodine scans at all (again, good practice, ungraded); intermediate-high and high-risk patients with a clinical suspicion of recurrence are the ones who may be scanned (Conditional recommendation, low certainty evidence).

PET is not a solution to everything. The guideline places it in patients at high risk with clearly raised Tg, and the situation the text describes is generally above 10 ng/mL; the same passage records that false positive lesions run from 0% to 39% between series, that it is insensitive for brain metastases, and that standard imaging stops at mid-thigh[1]. Its measured performance comes from 17 studies and 1,195 patients with differentiated thyroid cancer who had a rising Tg and a negative iodine scan: per-patient sensitivity 0.86 (95% confidence interval 0.79 to 0.91), specificity 0.84 (0.72 to 0.91). Heterogeneity between studies was high and there were statistical signs of publication bias. Whether TSH stimulation was used first made no difference to performance[6].

What another round of radioiodine actually buys

This is the request I field most often: can we not just do the iodine again. The guideline answers in two layers. With no demonstrable structural disease and a stimulated Tg below 10 ng/mL after thyroid hormone withdrawal, or below 5 with recombinant human TSH, you can be followed on thyroid hormone alone, holding further treatment back for a value that keeps climbing or for structural progression (Conditional recommendation, low certainty evidence)[1]. Where the value is much higher or rising fast and imaging has failed to reveal anything treatable, empiric radioiodine may be considered (Conditional recommendation, low certainty evidence again) — but the same passage says plainly that more than half of these patients see their Tg fall afterwards, and that improved survival has not been shown with this approach.

Here is what the numbers look like. 164 patients with differentiated thyroid cancer, total thyroidectomy, at least two courses of radioiodine, distant metastases excluded, followed on stimulated Tg: in the 61 with biochemical persistence only, the value fell from 41.9 to 24.6 ng/mL, which was statistically significant; among the 41 with structural disease and enough data to assess, 10 progressed, 5 shrank or disappeared, 26 were stable. Out of all 164, only 56 (34.1%) ended up disease-free[7]. So doing it again is not nothing. It is something that lands in the box marked "the number came down a bit", not the box marked "this is dealt with".

A rising number is not a reason to start a systemic drug

I will quote the guideline directly here, because it says it better than I would: in the absence of structural disease progression, increasing Tg alone should not be considered a criterion warranting the initiation of systemic therapy; rising or accelerating levels should instead lead to more frequent and more comprehensive imaging, to find the structure that corresponds to them[1].

The reason is not hard. The randomised trials of those targeted drugs enrolled people with lesions visible on imaging that were actively growing. Somebody whose only moving part is a number, with clean imaging, was not in those trial populations. When those drugs do belong is in 〈After radioiodine stops working〉.

Your doctor is reading the trend, not the reading

The guideline mentions Tg doubling time — the time the value takes to double — noting that the shorter it is, the likelier a positive PET; and in the same breath it says the cutoffs used vary from report to report[1]. So I am not going to hand you a doubling time to measure yourself against. What I do instead is three things: test serially on the same assay, read the antibodies alongside, and let enough time pass to see a direction. Change the assay, the hospital or the method and the number itself will jump. That is not the disease moving.

And one more. The box you were sorted into at the start gets rewritten by what follow-up shows. The three reclassified recurrence rates from that 588-patient cohort[8] I have put in 〈Follow-up is an ultrasound and one number〉. A number going down changes more than a laboratory value.

The number went up: three sentences to ask first

On the day you get the result, ask these three:

  • "Compared with the last two, is this one up, flat, or down?"
  • "Were these done at the same laboratory on the same assay? Where are my anti-thyroglobulin antibodies now?"
  • "How do you plan to look for it? What gets added to the ultrasound, and roughly when?"

If it has gone up three times, add one more: "At which result would you change the plan? I would like to know where that line is before we get there."

From the physician

Read these out at your next appointment: "Compared with the last two, is this up, flat or down?" and "Same laboratory, same assay?" and "If we keep finding nothing, what is the plan and how often do I come back?" Then copy your last three values and their dates onto one sheet of paper and bring it. What your doctor needs to see is the line.
