Why immunotherapy works for some people and not others

Checkpoint inhibitors release a brake, they do not press an accelerator. MSI-H, TMB and PD-L1 predict different things, and side effects have a timetable.

Situation Guides · Beyond Standard Treatment · 8 references
#Immunotherapy#Reading evidence#Decisions#Regulation

This comes to clinic in two forms. Whether "PD-L1 positive" is good news is the easier one: it means nothing until you know which drug, which antibody clone, which scoring method, which cancer. Why it works for someone else and not for you is harder — markers narrow the field, none guarantees your result.

"Same immunotherapy — why does it work for him and not for me?" I have heard that in clinic many times, and the answer sits inside how this class of drug works. It does something unlike chemotherapy or a targeted drug, so what decides whether it works is unlike them too.

It releases a brake; it does not press an accelerator

T cells carry brakes. CTLA-4 presses early, in the lymph node, when a T cell has just been activated; PD-1 presses late, out in the tissue — the cancer cell displays PD-L1 and holds down a T cell that has already found it. Checkpoint inhibitors are antibodies, and what they do is let the brake off. They neither attack the cancer cell nor supply T cells[1].

Two things follow straight from that mechanism, and they are the two patients find hardest. First, there has to be an immune response there to release before there is anything to release — cancers with few mutations and few T cells inside the tumour respond poorly, as you would expect. Second, letting the brake off lets it off everywhere, which is why the side effects look like autoimmune disease rather than like chemotherapy[1].

The infusion is the dull part; the work is between infusions

The process has no drama in it: an intravenous infusion every 2 to 6 weeks depending on drug and schedule, 30 to 60 minutes, as an outpatient, until the disease progresses, until side effects are unacceptable, or until the ceiling set by the trial and the label. The effort does not go into that hour; it goes into watching for immune side effects between infusions. In most settings it is added to, or follows, other treatment; a few indications are single-agent — the NHI provision itself is split into "used alone" and "combined with other drugs"[2]. Which applies to you is decided by the team from the cancer, the stage and the markers; it is not something a patient can swap in for the plan they already have.

Three biomarkers, three different things predicted

MSI-H/dMMR predicts most consistently of the three, and it does it across cancers. KEYNOTE-158 enrolled 373 patients with non-colorectal MSI-H/dMMR tumours, followed 4.5 years: overall response rate 33.8% (about one in three), median duration of response 63.2 months, median overall survival 19.8 months, grade 3 or worse treatment-related adverse events 13%[3]. Those two numbers have to be read together: the people who respond often respond for a very long time, and two-thirds do not respond at all. This is the steadiest predictor of the three, and it still leaves most people without a benefit. A newer drug, or a better-looking marker report, is a separate question from whether it works in you — "New does not mean better".

TMB (tumour mutational burden) is real but weak, and the cut-off is a human decision. The prospective biomarker analysis from the same KEYNOTE-158: of 1,050 enrolled, 790 (75%) were evaluable for TMB, and 102 (13%) counted as "high" (10 or more mutations per megabase); objective response 29% in the high group, 6% in the rest[4]. The gap is genuine; but even in the high group, seven in ten did not respond, and "high" is a chosen cut-off on a particular platform.

PD-L1 is the most widely used and the messiest. Its weakness is structural: which antibody clone, TPS or CPS scoring, where the cut-off sits, which cancer, how long the specimen sat — each changes the conclusion. Taiwan's NHI provision states this more clearly than any patient leaflet: reimbursement requires the class III in vitro diagnostic device (class III IVD) matched to that particular drug, the table names the antibody clone drug by drug, and the thresholds differ by indication (written below with the same symbols as the text: second-line gastric adenocarcinoma CPS ≥1; first-line gastric cancer with chemotherapy CPS ≥5; durvalumab as consolidation in non-small-cell lung cancer PD-L1 ≥1%), while several indications including melanoma and renal cell carcinoma need no PD-L1 report at all[2] (115.08.21 version, verified 2026-08-30). "PD-L1 positive" means nothing until you know which drug, which clone, which scoring method and which cancer. The colorectal dMMR/MSI-H version is in the colon cancer series' "Immunotherapy is not an option for everyone"; this is the general one, and the two point at each other.

Whether the tests themselves are covered or paid for out of pocket varies by cancer, and I have not verified them item by item — ask your case manager (個管師). How to get a written itemisation is in "How to ask about a self-pay item".

"It got bigger after the infusion" has two meanings, handled in opposite ways

Pseudoprogression is a lesion looking larger on imaging because immune cells have moved into it; it shrinks afterwards. That is why response assessment for immunotherapy differs from the usual criteria, and why a growing scan does not always mean an immediate change of drug. It is not common.

Hyperprogression is the opposite: in a small proportion the tumour grows faster than before. It is real, but definitions differ. In a meta-analysis of 24 studies and 3,109 patients the incidence ranged from 5.9% to 43.1%; by tumour growth rate ratio 9.4%, by growth kinetics ratio 15.8%, by early increase in tumour burden 20.6%[5]. There is currently no way to predict who.

One safety line belongs in this section: do not use "it might be pseudoprogression" as a reason to keep waiting. Report a growing scan or worsening symptoms; leave the judgement to the team rather than interpreting it yourself.

Which organs, and when

A pooled analysis of 23 trials and 8,436 patients puts the median time to onset of immune-related adverse events of any grade at 2.2 to 14.8 weeks, renal events latest; and the severe ones (grade 3 or worse) appear distinctly later — 27.5 weeks against 8.4 weeks with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. Resolution took 0.1 to 54.3 weeks, quickest for infusion reactions, slowest for endocrine events[6]. So "three months in and nothing has happened" does not mean nothing will.

Fatal side effects are rare, but the organ pattern is clear. An analysis of 613 fatal events in the World Health Organization pharmacovigilance database plus seven academic centres and 112 trials (19,217 patients): deaths with anti-CTLA-4 were mostly colitis (135 of 193, 70%); with anti-PD-1/PD-L1, pneumonitis (35%), hepatitis (22%) and neurotoxicity (15%); with combinations, colitis (37%) and myocarditis (25%). Myocarditis has the highest reported fatality: 52 of 131 cases (39.7%). Overall treatment-related fatality was 0.36% with anti-PD-1, 0.38% with anti-PD-L1, 1.08% with anti-CTLA-4 and 1.23% with the two combined; median time from starting treatment to fatal toxicity was 14.5 days for combinations and about 40 days for single agents, and the median from symptom onset to death was 32 days[7]. A network meta-analysis of 36 head-to-head trials and 15,370 patients puts adverse events in skin, endocrine organs, liver and lung, with one drug generally producing milder side effects than two, or than one plus conventional treatment[8]. That analysis compares toxicity, not efficacy — one drug or two is a decision weighing effect against toxicity together, and this is only half of it.

Contact the treating team the same day for: new or worsening breathlessness or dry cough (pneumonitis); diarrhoea 3 to 4 times a day more than your normal, blood or mucus in the stool, or abdominal pain (colitis); yellowing of the skin or the whites of the eyes, dark urine, right upper abdominal pain (hepatitis); chest pain, palpitations, unexplained tiredness or breathlessness (myocarditis, the one with the highest fatality); severe headache, confusion, weakness, double vision (neurological); sudden thirst and passing a lot of urine (immune-related diabetes); extreme fatigue, dizziness, low blood pressure (adrenal insufficiency). Do not manage these yourself and do not stop the drug yourself. At the dentist, at another specialty, at the emergency department, say without being asked that you are on immunotherapy — and keep saying it for months after it stops.

The situations that need a longer conversation

Taiwan's NHI provision writes several in as exclusions or restrictions: previous organ transplant, current immunosuppressant use, active or past autoimmune and inflammatory disease (depending on indication); plus organ-function conditions across classes — cardiac function NYHA class I to II, GOT/GPT under 60 U/L with total bilirubin under 1.5 mg/dL (advanced liver cancer and biliary tract cancer have their own rules), and renal thresholds varying by indication[2]. None of that means "you cannot have it". It means "discuss it specifically with your doctor, and count the risk in". Reimbursement conditions are written more narrowly than the label, and the two are not the same thing — "Approved, covered, effective: three different things".

Get "what if it does not work" asked before you need it

  1. Which markers has my tumour been tested for? Which assay, and what cut-off? The NHI provision itself requires the companion test matched to the drug, so this question has a definite answer.

  2. For my cancer and my markers, roughly what is the response rate? If there is no response, what is next? Mapping the "no response" route now means not starting to think about it on the day of the bad news.

  3. Which symptoms mean phoning the same day, and phoning whom? Put the number on the fridge. The serious immune side effects arrive later on average, and from symptom to real trouble is about a month.

From the physician

The sense of timing is what to take away: severe immune side effects arrive later on average than mild ones, and they still count months after the drug stops, so say you are on immunotherapy at the dentist, at another specialty, at the emergency department. And one safety line — do not explain a growing scan to yourself as pseudoprogression. Report it and leave the judgement to the team.
