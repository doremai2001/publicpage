Protons for the breast: where the evidence stands

A heart dose at the 0.5 Gy level is measurable dosimetry; RADCOMP is estimated to read out only in 2036 — not read out is not about to read out — and for adult breast cancer in Taiwan the whole cost is self-paid.

Breast Radiotherapy Guide · Choosing the Technique · 18 references
#Proton Therapy#Heart Dose#Clinical Trials#Costs & Self-Pay

The physics and the regulations were taken apart in the next-generation therapy topic; here I look only at the breast: the dosimetric numbers, the prospective trial in 70 patients, the randomised trial that has not read out, the costs in ribs and skin, the NHI provision restricted to patients under nineteen, and the one announced price I could find — each put into its own box.

Protons are the one technique in this topic that I cannot deliver in my own department and have to refer out for, and the one where the gap between what circulates publicly and what is documented is widest. The physics is real: a charged particle stops at the end of its range and leaves almost no dose beyond it — that physics, and its regulatory status in Taiwan, I have taken apart in "Protons: precise. Then what?" and "Is proton therapy actually better?", and I am not rewriting it here. This article does one thing only: it lays out the evidence for protons in the breast specifically, box by box — dosimetry, clinical outcomes, the costs that come with them, and the shape of the money.

My position first: every technique compared in this topic — ordinary intensity-modulated radiotherapy, TOMO, deep inspiration breath-hold — has a self-paid component among the more expensive options, and all of them are delivered in my own department; protons mean a referral elsewhere. So every "better" here comes with its primary source and the level of evidence behind it, and on cost I cite the official schedule where one exists and tell you to ask the medical affairs office where it does not. Concluding that the basic option is enough for you is a completely reasonable conclusion.

The dosimetric box: the numbers really are handsome

Start with the numbers that can be cited. In the systematic review covering 2003 to 2013, the proton mean heart dose for breast irradiation without the internal mammary nodes is at the 0.5 Gy level (0.1 to 0.8); with the internal mammary nodes it is 2.6 Gy, the lowest of any technique that includes them — in the same review, left-sided photon treatment including the internal mammary chain averages about 8 Gy[1]. Prospective measurements are lower still: a prospective series of 70 patients (91% left-sided, regional nodal irradiation included) had a mean whole-heart dose of 0.44 Gy and a mean left ventricular dose of 0.12 Gy, with no change in echocardiographic strain or in cardiac biomarkers between the start and the end of the course[2]. Breath-hold plays a slightly counter-intuitive part in this box: a systematic review of 9 comparative studies found that the proton mean heart dose under free breathing is already only 0.48 Gy, falling to 0.31 Gy with breath-hold added — and concluded that for most breast cancer patients having protons, breath-hold brings no clear extra dosimetric benefit[3]. The proton heart dose is already close to zero, so the marginal gain from a breath-hold is small. All of the above is dosimetric; clinical outcomes are in the next two sections.

The prospective box: seventy patients, five years

The most-cited prospective data for breast protons is the single-arm phase II trial from Massachusetts General Hospital: 70 patients who needed regional nodal irradiation and were considered unsuitable for conventional radiotherapy (91% left-sided, 94% stage II to III, 72% immediate reconstruction). Five-year locoregional failure 1.5%, overall survival 91%; one case of grade 2 radiation pneumonitis, no grade 3 pneumonitis, no grade 4 toxicity[4]. The number that has to be read alongside those: unplanned reoperation related to the reconstruction within five years, 33%[4] — that is a composite of the reconstruction itself plus radiotherapy, not something protons caused on their own, but for a patient with an immediate reconstruction who is putting protons into the discussion, that number belongs on the table. Single-arm, 70 patients, a selected population: it can say "this is deliverable and the early results are steady"; it cannot say "better than photons".

The randomised box: not read out, and not about to read out

The trial that could answer "have protons made cardiac events fewer" does exist; it is called RADCOMP, a pragmatic randomised trial of protons against photons whose primary endpoint is precisely major cardiovascular events[5]. On 31 August 2026 I looked at the trial registry myself: accrual complete (1,238 patients), status no longer recruiting, no results of any kind posted on the registry, and the estimated primary completion date given as November 2036[6]. A review article from 2026 relays that a 2025 quality-of-life analysis found the two arms equally good — that is at the level of a relayed account; the formal primary results paper I could not find[7]. So "not read out" is not "about to read out": this box will stay empty for many years. Until that result reads out, "protons are safer for breast cancer" has no evidence behind it that I can cite, so I do not say it. The same discipline applies in the other direction — "not read out" does not mean "no benefit", and the dosimetric gap is measurable; what RADCOMP has to answer is whether that gap buys a difference in event rates.

The cost box: protons have a side-effect shape of their own

Rib fracture is a signal peculiar to protons: one prospective trial cohort had a rib fracture rate of 7%, higher than the photon literature would lead you to expect, and the mechanistic analysis points to a rise in relative biological effectiveness (RBE) at the end of the proton range[8]; the figures from a larger series of 225 patients are gentler — a 3-year cumulative incidence of in-field rib fracture of 3.7%, symptomatic in 0.4%, most of them noticed incidentally on imaging[9]. The skin story has two sides. The interim analysis of a randomised phase II trial (26 Gy in 5 fractions, 72 evaluable patients, median follow-up 9 months) showed more acute radiation dermatitis in the proton arm (97.2% against 75%, mostly mild, grade 2 at most)[10]; in another comparison of 176 patients after mastectomy, once a skin dose constraint was written into the proton plans, grade 2 or worse dermatitis was the same as with photons (47% against 48%)[11]. Put together, the correct reading is this: the entrance side of a proton beam has none of the skin-sparing effect photons have, so skin dose depends on the plan — the early experience runs high, and dose constraints can bring it back down. The thyroid is not automatically spared either: a single-institution substudy within the RADCOMP trial (71 patients) found clinical hypothyroidism at 3 years after regional nodal irradiation in 13%, 16% with protons against 9% with photons, with no statistical difference (p=.14 — a p value measures how easily a difference of that size could have arisen by chance alone)[12].

Who has grounds to put protons into the discussion

The consensus statement of the Particle Therapy Cooperative Group (PTCOG) breast cancer subcommittee puts the position very precisely: that protons reduce non-target dose is consensus, but studies have not yet demonstrated that protons improve on the treatment outcomes photon radiotherapy already achieves; the roles the consensus suggests cluster in regional nodal irradiation (especially where the internal mammary nodes are included), high cardiac risk, difficult anatomy and re-irradiation[13]. Translated into clinic language, it is a stacking of conditions: young (decades of late risk still to carry), left-sided, internal mammary nodes to be treated (the box where the photon heart dose doubles), and a cardiac history on top of that — the more of those stack up, the more weight the proton dosimetric gap carries. The other way round, the photon heart dose for whole-breast irradiation alone is low to begin with; and what that dose gap does to cardiac event rates is exactly the question RADCOMP has not yet answered.

I am putting the numbers for the basic box here as well, because without a starting point you cannot see how large the gap is. Modern mean heart dose for the left breast: an average of 3.6 Gy across the 99 studies published between 2014 and 2017, and only 2.6 Gy among the studies published in 2017[14]; in one single institution's measured data on 4,687 patients, the median for left-sided hypofractionation is 1.47 Gy[15]; tangential fields plus respiratory control averaged 1.3 Gy across the 2003 to 2013 literature[1]. Those are all dosimetric numbers, and all of them were produced by techniques that need no extra payment — the heart-sparing of ordinary intensity-modulated radiotherapy does real work. That protons push the 1 Gy level down again to the 0.5 Gy level is true, but the starting point is not zero protection; the full string of numbers and the argument behind them are in "Heart dose: the core question on the left side".

The shape of the money: I cite the official documents as they are written

On the NHI side, from 1 January 2026 there are three proton therapy reimbursement items (36025B to 36027B), but the scope of all three is restricted to patients "under nineteen years of age", and they require prior review[16]; the full fee schedule table has no reimbursement item for adult protons, and the proton-related registered entries are marked "HTA item" with 0 points[17]. So for adult breast cancer, the shape the official documents give proton therapy is entirely self-paid. There is exactly one official announcement of an amount that I could find: the fee page of the Kaohsiung Chang Gung Memorial Hospital proton therapy centre — intensity-modulated proton therapy at NT$21,750 to NT$34,500 per session, respiratory gating within a proton course at an additional NT$5,000 or NT$9,600 per session, plus items for consultation, simulation and planning[18]. That respiratory gating is an item at a proton centre; it is not the same thing as photon deep inspiration breath-hold, and it cannot be treated as a reference price for photon breath-hold — the charging status of photon breath-hold I have written up in "Heart dose: the core question on the left side", where I could find no official announcement. This also has to be labelled clearly: that is that hospital's own announcement, not a breast-cancer-specific quotation, and the total for a course depends on the number of fractions and on whether respiratory gating is used. I cite this one only because it is the only official announcement I could find, and only to show the order of magnitude of the self-paid amounts; it is not a recommendation of that hospital, and it is not a statement that other hospitals are dearer or cheaper — for other hospitals I could find no official announcement, so ask each hospital's medical affairs office. Photon radiotherapy is covered by the NHI, and adult protons are entirely self-paid — those two sentences can sit side by side; the clinical outcome figure you would need in order to appraise them has not read out.

Before the referral consultation, get your own photon numbers first

If your conditions stack up far enough — young, left-sided, internal mammary nodes to be treated, a cardiac history — print this out and ask two things at your own hospital first: "For my field, what mean heart dose in Gy does a photon plan with breath-hold come out at?" and "Set against the 0.5 Gy level for protons, how do you read that number, given my cardiac condition?" Only after that, at the referral consultation, ask the proton side: "How many fractions is my course, does it include respiratory gating, what is the total, and please give it to me in writing." The order is the point: get your own photon number first, then go and listen to the proton quotation — without that first number there is nothing to compare against.

From the physician

Young, left-sided, internal mammary nodes to be treated, a cardiac history — the more of those stack up, the more reason there is to put protons into the discussion. Before the referral consultation, get the mean heart dose of your own photon plan from your own hospital, and only then compare the proton side's written quotation and number of fractions.
