WHEN TREATMENT STALLS

A rising marker is not a growing tumour

The same number means two things in two people: CEA rises with age and smoking, and CA19-9 baselines can differ by more than double.

I have deliberately printed no reference value here. Ranges differ between hospitals and between assays, and the one printed on your own report is the one that applies to you. What can actually be read is not the box but its direction against your last few results — and those points are on the clinic's chart, not in this article.

The abbreviations on the blood report — CEA, CA19-9, CA125, AFP — are the numbers that keep people awake. In a single afternoon I will often meet both of the opposite misreadings: one person whose marker has gone up two points, asking for a change of drug; another with a new pain who has decided it can wait until next time because the markers are all normal. This article is built to block both, and to say what these numbers can actually be read for.

"Treat earlier the moment it rises" has been tested head-on by a randomised trial

Ovarian cancer has one very cleanly designed trial. Women in complete remission after first-line platinum chemotherapy were registered for follow-up with CA125 measured every three months, with both patients and investigators blinded to the values, monitored by a coordinating centre; once a value exceeded twice the upper limit of normal, they were randomised to immediate treatment or to waiting until clinical or symptomatic relapse[1].

1,442 women were registered, 529 randomised, median follow-up 56.9 months: no difference in overall survival, hazard ratio 0.98 (95% confidence interval 0.80 to 1.20), p=0.85[1]. A hazard ratio compares the relative rate at which events occur in two groups over the same period, not a multiple of probability; 0.98 in plain words means "in this dataset deaths arrived at almost the same speed in both groups", not "a 2% lower chance of dying". The authors concluded there is no evidence that treating relapse early on the basis of a rising CA125 alone brings a survival benefit.

This is not an argument against measuring — guidelines still recommend regular measurement in particular settings, which is below. What it says is this: whether something predicts an outcome and whether treatment can be changed on the strength of it are two different questions. Exactly the same line runs through blood testing, and that is covered in "One tube of blood, two opposite answers". One caveat: this trial was published in 2010, and the treatment options since then have changed.

A rise as chemotherapy starts may be the drug working

Colorectal cancer has a phenomenon called the CEA surge. Among 89 evaluable patients with metastatic disease, 10 showed it; in all 10 the rise lasted less than four months, all 10 were associated with clinical benefit, and not one met the definition of progression; the authors' conclusion is that a CEA rise after starting chemotherapy cannot be used as an indicator of progressive disease unless it persists beyond four months[2]. A 99-patient study using different drugs saw the same thing: 11 patients (11.1%) showed the surge and none had progressive disease (4 partial responses, 7 stable disease), with the authors putting it more directly still — an early rise of this kind should not lead to a change of chemotherapy[3].

This section is the one most easily flipped into "a rise is good news", so let me nail the conditions down. It is one possibility a doctor holds in mind on seeing the combination of "treatment has just started" and "the number went up", and confirming it needs the next value and the one after that. You do not have those points yet. The imaging version of the same logic is in "The scan looks worse. The disease may not be.".

The same number means different things in two different people

All of these markers move for reasons that have nothing to do with a tumour. A reference-interval study in 498 healthy people showed CEA concentrations rising with age and with smoking, AFP rising with age, and CA125 unrelated to age[4]. CA19-9 is stranger still: its expression is governed by Lewis blood group and secretor genotype, and there is a group of people in whom it barely registers at all; a second 500-person study calculated between-individual biological variation of 102.2% — in plain terms, one person's natural baseline can be more than double another's, so comparing your number with someone else's means nothing, and only your own trend does[5]. Laboratory medicine also has a concept of a change being large enough to count, but that threshold is a statistic derived from a particular study and a particular assay, not a rule you can apply to yourself.

False positives after surgery have been measured. Among 168 patients followed after curative gastrectomy for gastric cancer, mean follow-up 23.1 months, the false-positive rate was 14.3% (24 patients), mostly in early-stage disease and in people with chronic benign conditions (bronchitis, abnormal liver function, diabetes, renal impairment), and most of those false positives in that study fell back on their own one to two months after the first high value[6] — a population figure the investigators calculated looking back over whole strings of values, not a rule for you to decide to "give it a month or two". Whether to repeat the test, when, and whether to arrange imaging alongside it is still settled at that clinic appointment. The kidney point needs stating precisely: in 131 patients with chronic kidney disease not on dialysis, the reference limits for CEA, CA125 and CA19-9 were much the same as in healthy people, and the two markers strongly correlated with glomerular filtration rate were different ones[7]. So "poor kidney function pushes markers up" does not hold for these three.

I am deliberately not printing a single reference value in this article. Ranges differ between hospitals and between assays, and the range printed by the laboratory on your own report is the one that applies to you.

What the guidelines themselves say: a rise has to be confirmed by something else

The 2006 American Society of Clinical Oncology gastrointestinal guidelines say that serially rising CA19-9 in pancreatic cancer "suggest progressive disease but confirmation with other studies should be sought"[8]. The same document recommends CEA every three months for at least three years after surgery in stage II and III colorectal cancer, on the premise that the person would be a candidate for resection of metastases or for chemotherapy. The European Group on Tumour Markers is more conservative: existing serum markers lack the specificity and sensitivity for early detection of colorectal cancer[9]; the National Academy of Clinical Biochemistry's laboratory medicine guidelines list CEA as recommended, with caveats, for prognosis, postoperative surveillance and monitoring of advanced disease, and CA125 for detecting recurrence and monitoring therapy in ovarian cancer[10].

Three documents, three decades, one sentence: these numbers are for a doctor to read alongside other information, not something that reaches a conclusion on its own.

A normal marker does not downgrade a symptom

The misreading in the other direction is the more dangerous one. Serial CEA measurement for detecting colorectal cancer recurrence has a sensitivity of about 80% and a specificity of about 70%, leading imaging by roughly five months[11]. Eighty per cent means that one in every five genuine recurrences will not show up. The same review says outright that there is little evidence this kind of surveillance in all colorectal cancer patients improves outcomes or quality of life.

So symptoms are not governed by markers. A change that is new, persistent and unlike anything before — pain, weight loss, breathlessness, blood in the stool, jaundice — does not become less important because those numbers are normal. Report it; do not wait for the next blood draw. PSA is another marker read this way, and its bounce and kinetics are in "A PSA bounce is not a recurrence".

The markers themselves are existing NHI items

Carcinoembryonic antigen, CA-125, CA-199 and alpha-fetoprotein all have laboratory codes in the National Health Insurance (健保) fee schedule (支付標準)[12], so a doctor booking them at intervals is not adding tests. On how many times a year they can be claimed and whether there are indication limits, I checked the full table line by line and found no matching provision (CA-199 carries only a note that in primary-care institutions it must be ordered by a full-time physician); for the actual position, ask your case manager (個管師) or the hospital billing office (醫務課). The full table is the 114.03.13 version, verified 2026-08-30; these four codes do not appear in the amendment schedule effective 115 年 9 月 1 日, but check the current version before printing anything out. I was unable to obtain any official Taiwanese guidance on how often these markers should be measured or how they should be read, so this article contains no sentence beginning "the recommendation in Taiwan is".

On the next report, read the trend, not the box

First: what is the trend in this number compared with my last few? (A single point means nothing; a trend does.) Second: given that trend, will you repeat the test, arrange imaging, or leave it — and why? Third: before the next blood test, what change should bring me back early? Put the answer in your phone, next to your case manager's (個管師) number. As for whether to change drug because of this number — that question goes to your medical team. Do not move a single tablet on your own.

From the physician

A marker two points higher is not a reason to adjust your own drugs; markers all normal does not make a new, persistent, unfamiliar symptom less important. The first goes to the clinic visit that decides whether to repeat the test or arrange imaging; the second should be reported without waiting for the next blood draw. Read the run of past values in order before you go in.
