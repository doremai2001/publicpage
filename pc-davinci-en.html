HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

Is the robot worth what it costs you

The randomised evidence says the robot buys fewer transfusions and a shorter stay — not better continence or better erections.

Prostate Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 15 references
#Prostate#Surgery#Decisions#NHI Coverage#Quality of Life

Patients ask me this every month, and I am not the one operating. The honest version has three separate accounts in it, and only one of them is still in your hands: who holds the instruments.

"Is the extra money for the da Vinci worth it?" I get asked this most months in clinic. I am not the one holding the instruments, so I have no stake in the answer — which is why I have to write it from the data, without talking it up or down. The shape of the answer first: what the machine buys is real, it is just not the item most men think they are paying for.

One trial randomised the two operations, and function came out level

The phase 3 randomised trial at the Royal Brisbane and Women's Hospital in Australia enrolled 326 men aged 35 to 70 with newly diagnosed localised prostate cancer who had already chosen surgery, randomising them to open retropubic or robot-assisted laparoscopic prostatectomy; 151 and 157 operations were completed. Every operation was performed by two surgeons — one experienced in open surgery and one in robotic surgery[1].

At 12 weeks the EPIC urinary score was 83.80 for open and 82.50 for robotic (p=0.48), sexual function 35.00 against 38.90 (p=0.18); positive margins 10% against 15%, with equivalence not established and the superiority test not significant either (p=0.21)[1]. At 24 months: urinary 90.86 against 91.33, sexual function 46.90 against 45.70, IIEF erectile function 33.89 against 33.95 — not one functional endpoint differed significantly at 6, 12 or 24 months. Biochemical recurrence was 9% against 3%, but the authors warn in the paper against reading that as an oncological advantage, because postoperative management was not standardised between the groups[2].

The conclusion these investigators drew is worth copying out whole: the robot's benefits should be understood as coming mainly from its minimally invasive nature; and at the end of the 12-week paper they wrote that patients should be encouraged to choose an experienced surgeon they trust and can talk to, rather than a surgical approach[1][2].

The machine does buy two things: less transfusion, earlier discharge

The Cochrane review gathered 2 randomised trials and 446 men: hospital stay was on average 1.72 days shorter (−2.19 to −1.25, moderate-quality evidence), and the relative risk of transfusion was 0.24 (0.12–0.46), which against a baseline risk of 8.9% is 68 fewer transfusions per 1,000 men (low-quality evidence). Urinary and sexual quality of life came out at −1.30 and 3.90, written up as "probably little or no difference"; overall complications gave 0.41 (0.16–1.04), a confidence interval crossing 1 — a confidence interval is the range the true value plausibly sits in, and once it contains 1, whether there is any difference at all is unsettled. No study reported survival or biochemical recurrence-free survival, and the review states outright that it could not take surgeon volume and experience into account — which happens to be the variable patients care about most[3].

American SEER-Medicare data from 2003 to 2007 (1,938 minimally invasive against 6,899 open) agrees exactly here: median stay 2 days against 3, transfusion 2.7% against 20.8%, anastomotic stricture 5.8% against 14.0%[4]. A transfusion rate of 20.8% is not a small number, so calling the robot "just marketing" is not honest either.

On incontinence the data even run the other way

The other half of that same SEER-Medicare analysis reads like this: genitourinary complications 4.7% against 2.1% (p=0.001), coded incontinence diagnoses 15.9 against 12.2 per 100 person-years (p=0.02) and coded erectile dysfunction 26.8 against 19.2 (p=0.009), all higher after minimally invasive surgery[4]. Reading it takes two caveats: these are billed diagnosis codes, not patient-completed questionnaires; and 2003 to 2007 is exactly when minimally invasive prostatectomy went from 9.2% to 43.2% of American practice. What it records is the cost of a technique spreading faster than the experience to use it, not today's scorecard.

Sweden's LAPPRO study was prospective but not randomised, across 14 hospitals, with validated questionnaires before surgery and again at 12 months: self-reported leakage at 12 months was 21.3% (366/1,717) after robotic surgery and 20.2% (144/713) after open surgery, unadjusted odds ratio 1.08 (0.87–1.34) — no difference. At the same 12-month point, on the same basis of self-reported erectile dysfunction, robotic surgery gave 70.4% and open surgery 74.7%, odds ratio 0.81 (0.66–0.98), which the authors describe as modestly in favour. An odds ratio is not a multiple of a probability: 0.81 does not mean a fifth fewer men[5].

"The da Vinci means less incontinence" is a sentence I can find no data to support.

Margins and cancer control: the two approaches draw

A systematic review and cumulative meta-analysis of 79 papers put the mean positive margin rate after robot-assisted prostatectomy at 15%, and 9% for pathologically organ-confined disease; against open surgery the odds ratio was 1.21 (p=0.19), against laparoscopic 1.12 (p=0.47), with no difference in biochemical recurrence-free survival either. The authors read this as meaning margins are driven more by surgeon-related characteristics and technical detail[6].

What is still movable here is the person, not the machine

An analysis of 72 surgeons and 7,765 patients at four American academic centres found the learning curve measured by biochemical recurrence to be steep: improvement continued until roughly 250 cases. Predicted five-year recurrence was 17.9% (12.1–25.6) at 10 cases and 10.7% (7.1–15.9) at 250, an absolute difference of 7.2% (4.6–10.1), P<0.001[7].

The same question asked of robotic surgery (1,827 cases): more experience meant fewer positive margins (p=0.035), 16.7% at 10 cases and 9.6% at 250, a risk difference of 7.1% (1.7–12.2); but experience had no significant relationship with biochemical recurrence (p=0.8), which the authors note is a different shape from the open literature and calls for larger multicentre studies[8].

The most concrete account is one high-volume surgeon's 1,552 consecutive cases (866 robotic, 686 open, with some 3,000 open operations behind him already): his robotic sexual function scores did not overtake his own open results until case 99 and plateaued at 600 to 700; early continence scores did not overtake until case 182, plateauing at 700 to 800; pT2 positive margins started higher than his open figures and only fell below them after case 108. Their conclusion is plain: for this high-volume surgeon the learning was worth it, but that curve is unlikely to pay off for a surgeon near retirement or with a low caseload[9].

I am not going to tell you to hold a surgeon to "250 cases". A systematic review of 97 studies shows that for operative time alone, reported learning curves for robot-assisted prostatectomy span 10 to 250 cases, with inconsistent definitions of what counts as having learned it and poorly reported confounders[10]. The range is the honest part; the threshold figure is not.

The direction of the volume effect is at least stable: a systematic review of 49 publications found higher volume associated with lower mortality, complications, length of stay, readmission and cost, and that relationship holds in robotic surgery too; though in the 11 studies analysing hospital and surgeon volume together, neither came out an independent predictor[11]. So "how many of these do you do a year" is a fair question — just do not expect a clean pass mark to exist.

Nerve-sparing is not a free extra

A Taiwanese single-surgeon dataset, 419 robot-assisted operations and 838 prostate sides: posterolateral positive margins were 10.9% with full nerve-sparing, 22.4% with partial and 18.9% with none (p=0.001), the odds ratio for partial against full sparing 2.187 (1.19–4.03)[12]. These are one surgeon's numbers, not the result you will be handed; their use is to show that the extent of nerve-sparing is a trade-off between margin risk and sexual function, not an on-off switch. That question is covered fully in "Does rehabilitation work, and what happens to ejaculation".

What you pay in Taiwan: half of this one is answerable

The National Health Insurance Administration (健保署) states it clearly: 「達文西根治性前列腺切除術」 (robot-assisted radical prostatectomy) may, since January 2017 (106 年 1 月), be billed for its surgical fee under "79410B radical prostatectomy with bilateral pelvic lymph node dissection + 28014C laparoscopy", with the patient charged the difference above what National Health Insurance (健保) pays[13]. In plain terms: partly covered — neither entirely self-paid nor paid in full. The same announcement carries another sentence worth keeping on your phone: da Vinci operations in other specialties are outside the benefit, so that operation and its related special materials are self-paid in full, but consultation, ward, examination, drug and anaesthesia fees during the admission are National Health Insurance benefits and may not be charged to the patient[13]. A further announcement at the end of 2022 (111 年底) brought the surgical fees of 17 da Vinci procedures into coverage, describing it as a continuation of the approach taken for prostate and partial nephrectomy surgery[14].

How large is the difference? There is no national price, and the system is not built to produce one. Self-pay charges follow the Ministry of Health and Welfare's reference principles for approving medical fee schedules, set by each municipal and county authority after review by the local medical review committee[15]. So the price can only be asked of the hospital that will operate on you: ask the case manager (個管師) or the hospital billing office (醫務課) for an itemised written quotation. The NHI Administration also states that self-paid items require a signed consent form after full discussion between doctor and patient[13].

Whether it is worth it is three separate accounts

The first: the machine buys fewer transfusions and a shorter stay. That is real, and it has been shown more than once. The second: it does not buy better continence or sexual function — the randomised trial and the large Swedish prospective study both come out level. The third is still in your hands: who operates, how many he has done, and what his own results look like.

So these are worth asking at the next visit. Who will be operating, and how many of these does he do a year? Given my imaging and where my biopsy was positive, how many sides do you plan to spare, and why? What does the self-paid difference cover, and can I have it itemised in writing? And if I chose open surgery instead, what would be different here? That last one is the most useful — it forces a real answer rather than a brochure.

From the physician

Volume and technique vary between surgeons far more than between machines, so the answer that matters is your surgeon's, not the literature's. Ask for the self-paid difference in writing from the case manager or the billing office, and ask what would change if you had open surgery instead.
