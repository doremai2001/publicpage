HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

Why you cannot skip a day of radiotherapy

Accelerated repopulation, what one missed day is worth, the six weeks from surgery to radiotherapy and the hundred-day mark, and why smoking during treatment blunts radiation.

Head & Neck Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 9 references
#Treatment Pathway#Decisions#Quitting#Daily Life

The length of a course is part of the prescription, not an administrative convenience. This page also covers the direction people forget: the situations in which stopping is the right call, and who gets to make that decision.

"Doctor, my daughter is getting married next week — can I stop radiotherapy for five days?" I get this almost every month, and the people who ask are almost always polite and slightly embarrassed. I advise against it every time, and the reason is not a rule. It is that halfway through a course the tumour does something: it changes gear. This article is about that mechanism, and about an equally important flip side that gets forgotten — when treatment should stop, and who decides.

The tumour changes gear mid-course

A classic analysis reconstructing dose–response data from nearly 500 patients with oropharyngeal cancer produced an observation still in use today: in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, tumour cell repopulation only accelerates after a lag of about 4 ± 1 weeks from the start of radiotherapy, and from then on each extra day requires roughly an additional 0.6 Gy just to hold the same effect. That rate corresponds to tumour cells doubling every four days, against a median doubling time of about 60 days in an untreated tumour[1]. The authors themselves stress that these are population averages still needing prospective verification, and that there is as yet no way to predict when any individual patient enters the accelerated phase.

The direction, though, is clear: the early part of the course is a race against something growing slowly, the later part a race against something that has started running. Wanting time off in weeks four and five lands on exactly the worst spot to take it.

What one extra day, or one missed day, is roughly worth

Two studies can be used to estimate this. A review across tumour sites puts it bluntly: head and neck is the site with the strongest evidence, and even a one-day interruption decreased the local control rate by 1.4%[2]. A model built from larynx cancer data at four centres — Edinburgh, Glasgow, Manchester and Toronto — comes out lower: against a baseline local control of 80%, extending treatment by one day, or a one-day gap, reduced local control by about 0.68% (95% confidence interval 0.28–1.08%); a five-day gap took control from 80% to 77%. The authors' central point was that any gap — and most of them are public holidays — does the same damage as lengthening the prescribed course in the first place[3].

So the honest formulation is "roughly 0.7% to 1.4% of local control lost per extra day", and the confidence interval around that 0.68% is wide. This is a population-level estimate. It explains why a radiation oncology department fights over scheduling with you, with the machine and with the long weekends. It is not a number for passing sentence on any one person.

The other direction: pushing through when you shouldn't is also wrong

This article has to protect two directions at once, so let me be blunt about both.

Direction one: do not skip, postpone or stop any fraction on your own. Not because your mouth hurts, not because something has come up at home, not because you feel too tired today. If it is genuinely bad, say so — pain control, mouth care, fluids, nutrition support, even admission can all be added, and none of it requires sacrificing the course.

Direction two matters just as much: do not push through a genuine emergency in order to make a fraction. Fever with a low white cell count, severe dehydration, a whole day unable to swallow liquids, choking that suggests aspiration pneumonia, pain that cannot be controlled — these need the medical team immediately. A break decided by a radiation oncologist on medical grounds and a patient deciding not to turn up are two completely different things. A planned interruption is something the team has methods to compensate for, discussed explicitly in the review above[2]. The days a patient disappears for, we usually find out about afterwards.

One group I particularly want to reassure: people who have already lost days to an infection, a typhoon, or a family member being admitted to hospital. Do not conclude that your treatment is now pointless, and do not take the percentages above and multiply them by your own number of days. What you should do is tell your doctor how many days you missed, so the rest of the schedule can be adjusted.

After surgery, the clock starts running

If you need postoperative radiotherapy, there are two more deadlines to watch. The first is starting radiotherapy within six weeks (42 days) of surgery — the first and so far only quality metric approved by the American College of Surgeons Commission on Cancer (CoC) for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and the definition NCCN uses. That meta-analysis found 48.6% of patients started postoperative radiotherapy more than six weeks after surgery, with a median start time of 45.8 days[4]. About half of patients internationally miss the standard, which makes "when does my radiotherapy start?" worth asking out loud at the post-operative visit.

The second is the whole treatment package. Ang's randomised trial (213 patients treated) found that in the seven-week arm, a longer interval between surgery and postoperative radiotherapy was associated with worse locoregional control (p=0.03) and worse survival (p=0.01), and that the cumulative duration of the combined treatment also significantly affected locoregional control (p=0.005) and survival (p=0.03)[5]. Rosenthal analysed 208 patients treated with surgery plus postoperative radiotherapy, dichotomising the time from surgery to the end of radiotherapy at 100 days, and the conclusion is unambiguous: a package time under 100 days is associated with better tumour control and survival, and every effort should be made to keep it under 100 days[6]. A Portuguese series of 79 high-risk oral cavity cancer patients points the same way: an optimal cut-off of 104 days, with 5-year survival of 77.4% versus 46.7%[7].

Putting those together, the practical target is: start radiotherapy within six weeks of surgery and finish the whole package in about 12 to 13 weeks; the threshold the literature has actually tested is 100 days. To be clear about the evidence: the interval and total-time findings inside the Ang trial are secondary, and Rosenthal and the Portuguese series are retrospective. These are consistent associations. They cannot be stated as cause.

Still smoking during treatment is another kind of interruption

A prospective study published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 1993 followed 115 head and neck cancer patients through radiotherapy, recording smoking status every week: among the 53 who kept smoking during treatment, the complete response rate was 45% and 2-year survival 39%; among the 62 who did not smoke or had quit before treatment, the figures were 74% and 66%. After adjustment for other factors, smoking remained an independent prognostic factor, with a relative risk of 2.5[8].

The most important sentence in that paper: the result could not be explained by the type of chemotherapy received, by coexisting illnesses, by differences in radiation side effects, or by the number of treatment interruptions[8]. In other words, smoking is not just costing you extra days off. It appears to make the radiation itself less effective, and the most plausible explanation is tumour hypoxia.

The good news is in the same dataset: quitting late still helps. People who quit less than 12 weeks before diagnosis had a 40% risk reduction; those who quit more than a year before, 70%[8]. Worth noting that this is a 1993 study, predating IMRT and routine concurrent chemotherapy, so the numbers cannot be transplanted directly onto today. The direction has never been overturned.

Betel quid, tobacco, alcohol — and one thing you can do today

The figures the Ministry of Health and Welfare (衛生福利部) quoted when releasing its analysis of the 2022 national cancer registry carry weight: chewing betel quid raises the chance of oral cavity cancer to 28 times that of non-chewers, and smoking to 18 times that of non-smokers; for people who use betel quid, tobacco and alcohol together, the risk of upper aerodigestive tract cancer reaches 10.5 times[9]. The Health Promotion Administration's (國民健康署) advice is equally simple: give up betel quid, tobacco and alcohol, and have regular oral mucosal screening[9].

I am not going to lecture here. I want one thing to be clear: the six or seven weeks you are in now are the worst weeks of your life to be smoking through. Most hospitals in Taiwan run a smoking cessation clinic or counselling service; for what is offered and how it is subsidised, check with your hospital or your case manager (個管師), and you can ask your treatment team to arrange it alongside radiotherapy.

Three things worth asking at your next visit: how many fractions is my course, and what day does it finish? If a long weekend or machine maintenance falls in the middle, how will it be made up? And if I get a fever or cannot swallow, which number do I ring, and who decides whether to pause? The people who get that third answer straight are usually the ones who run into the least trouble.

From the physician

If you have already missed days, please do not take the per-day percentage in this article and multiply it by your own number of missed days; it is a population estimate, not a verdict on your case. Tell your radiation oncologist how many days were lost, so the remaining schedule has a chance to compensate.
