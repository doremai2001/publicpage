Eating after an oesophagectomy

Once the stomach has been pulled into the chest, how weight, dumping, reflux and gastric emptying each change; every number comes with its source, so you can tell what is expected from what needs a visit.

Oesophageal Cancer Guide · After Treatment · 21 references
#Oesophagectomy#Nutrition#Swallowing#Survivorship

Many people assume discharge is where recovery begins; in fact the first six months after surgery are when weight falls hardest. This article does not tell you "it stabilises after so many months" — I could not find citable data for that — only what will happen, how to tell the events apart when they do, and when to come back.

Almost every patient whose oesophagus has been removed and whose stomach has been pulled up to replace it asks me the same thing before discharge: "Will I be able to eat normally again?" My usual answer: you will eat, but not the way you used to; and you will lose weight over the first few months, which I want said now rather than after the panic sets in. Below, in order — weight, dumping, reflux, gastric emptying — each with numbers, so you can tell which things are expected and which ones mean you should come back.

Once the stomach sits in the chest, food travels a different road

After most of the oesophagus is removed, my surgical colleagues fashion the stomach into a tube and bring it up into the chest or the neck to join what is left of the oesophagus (the operation itself is in "What surgery removes, and where the stomach goes"). The new road differs from the old one in three places: the lower oesophageal sphincter is gone, so acid and bile are no longer held back; the stomach is now a tube with only part of its former capacity, so it cannot hold what it used to in one sitting; and the vagus nerve is divided during surgery, so the opening and closing of the pylorus is no longer under nervous control. The international consensus on dumping syndrome describes the same three mechanisms: food rushing into the small bowel too fast, a stomach that cannot hold it, and gut hormones all called out at once[1]. Nearly every symptom below traces back to these three changes.

How much weight, and for how long

Numbers first, because this is the thing patients and families most often underestimate. In a Swedish nationwide cohort of 226 patients operated on for oesophageal cancer, at six months 63.7% had lost more than 10% of their weight (measured as BMI) and 20.4% had lost more than 20%; the factors linked to weight loss were poor appetite, being unable to eat, and painful swallowing — difficulty swallowing and reflux themselves were, surprisingly, not associated[2]. The same cohort followed to three years (203 survivors): weight can keep falling for up to three years; patients who were overweight before surgery (BMI 25 or above) had odds ratios for malnutrition of 4.9 at six months and 4.6 at three years — an odds ratio divides one group's odds of the event (occurrences against non-occurrences) by the other's, and cannot be read as "five times the probability", but the direction is clear: the people who started with more weight were the ones more likely to end up malnourished[3]. A systematic review of 18 studies reached a similar conclusion: 5 to 12% lost at six months, more than half of patients down by more than 10% at 12 months, 27 to 95% never back to baseline weight on long-term follow-up, and inadequate energy and protein intake persisting for up to three years[4]. I could not find any citable data saying "it stabilises after so many months", so I am not going to give you a number of months; the honest version is: it will fall, it may fall for a long time, and many people never get back to their original weight. The goal is to hold the size of the loss, not to wait for it to come back by itself.

The jejunostomy tube you go home with: keep feeding through it or not

Practice differs between hospitals here; the evidence looks like this. The Dutch NUTRIENT II randomised trial (132 patients after minimally invasive Ivor Lewis oesophagectomy) compared starting oral intake straight away against five days of fasting with tube feeding: functional recovery 7 days against 8, anastomotic leak 18.5% against 16.4%, pneumonia 24.6% against 34.3% — no difference could be seen in any of them[5]. So "start eating early" is safe in this group — minimally invasive surgery with an intrathoracic anastomosis — but the result cannot simply be carried over to open surgery or a neck anastomosis. What about after you get home? A secondary analysis of a small British randomised trial of 41 patients (32 oesophagectomies, 9 total gastrectomies) found that at discharge, what patients took in by mouth covered only 9% of their energy needs and 6% of their protein, and only reached 61% and 55% at three months; among those who kept feeding through the jejunostomy for six weeks at home, 28% lost more than 5% of their weight, against 82% of those who did not — and tube feeding did not make them eat any less[6]. The numbers are very small, but the direction is clear: what goes in by mouth at discharge is a long way from enough. The European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) surgical guideline's principle is that patients after upper gastrointestinal surgery particularly need early enteral nutrition, an early return to oral intake, and nutritional therapy started at once wherever there is nutritional risk[7]. Choosing between the three routes, and when to take them out, is in "Can't swallow, weight falling: a feeding tube first?"; how the NHI handles the consumables and formula for home tube feeding — I could not find the clause, so ask your case manager, dietitian or the medical affairs office.

Palpitations, sweating, needing to lie down after a meal: this is dumping syndrome

A racing heart, sweating, dizziness, cramping and an urge to open your bowels within half an hour of eating; or shaking hands, cold sweat and ravenous hunger one to three hours after a meal — this is dumping syndrome, the first pattern early dumping, the second late dumping (low blood sugar). How common it is depends on how you ask. A meta-analysis of 16 studies pooled a prevalence of 27%, but the three studies that used a dedicated questionnaire pooled to 67%[8]; a Danish single-centre adenocarcinoma cohort followed for two years with structured interviews found 78% had experienced it at least once[9]; the Swedish nationwide cohort (384 patients, self-reported at one year after surgery) put "significant dumping symptoms" at 11%[10]. The three numbers do not contradict each other: when asked by symptom questionnaire it is very common; what actually disrupts life and needs treatment is about 10%. The international consensus puts dietary adjustment first in the order of management — small frequent meals, solids and liquids taken separately, less simple sugar; the low blood sugar of late dumping can be treated with acarbose; only when diet plus acarbose both fail is a somatostatin analogue considered[1]. One warning: in late dumping the blood sugar can fall below 50 mg/dL, and people faint. If you get these symptoms, come back and see us; do not solve it on your own by simply not eating.

Reflux and waking up coughing at night: why the bed head goes up

With the lower sphincter gone and the stomach now in the chest, when you lie flat the stomach contents run back up by gravity. In the Swedish nationwide cohort, 27% reported severe reflux at one year after surgery; the odds ratio for poor sleep quality among those with reflux was 4.9, and people with both reflux and disturbed sleep scored worse on global quality of life, role, emotional and social function[11]; followed to three years, patients with malnutrition plus severe reflux scored 10 to 16 points worse than those with malnutrition but no reflux on nausea and vomiting, pain, body image and eating restrictions[12]. Reflux damages more than sleep: in a German series of 50 Ivor Lewis patients endoscoped every year for five years, despite routine PPI, 43% had visible damage to the remnant oesophageal mucosa at one year and 55% at five, adenocarcinoma patients more than squamous (68% against 35%), and 20% developed new Barrett's oesophagus[13]. Raising the head of the bed, not eating in the hours before sleep, not lying flat after meals — these are clinic routine, and the reason is the two anatomical changes above; there is no controlled study specific to post-oesophagectomy patients, and I am not going to invent a number of centimetres. Night-time reflux together with cough and fever should make you think of aspiration pneumonia — see "When to come back the same day".

Should the pylorus be dealt with in advance? Practice varies, and the evidence has not taken a side

At the far end of the gastric conduit is the pylorus, which after vagal division may not open well, so food fails to pass — delayed gastric emptying. A meta-analysis of 125 studies put the pooled incidence at 15.9% early and 9.4% late[14]. To prevent it, some surgeons perform a pyloroplasty or pyloromyotomy during the operation, inject botulinum toxin, or dilate; others do nothing. Where does the evidence stand? A meta-analysis that included observational studies found pyloric drainage lowered the odds ratio for delayed emptying to 0.54, but pulmonary complications, bile reflux, leak and mortality did not differ[15]; a meta-analysis restricted to randomised trials (9 trials, 529 patients) concluded that pyloric drainage is "not routinely necessary"[16]; in the 125-study meta-analysis, prophylactic pyloric drainage had no statistically significant effect on either early or late delayed emptying[14]; and intra-operative botulinum toxin (9 studies, 1,070 patients) showed no detectable difference against no injection[17]. So whether your surgeon did it or not, both are within what the evidence allows. Asking "was my pylorus dealt with at the time?" is still worthwhile, because if emptying is poor later on, endoscopic dilatation of the pylorus is one road — and that belongs to the gastroenterologists.

Three years on, ten years on: what life looks like

Two ways of comparing give two answers, and I will give you both. In a British prospective cohort of 47 patients surviving three years, most quality-of-life domains had returned to pre-operative levels, but physical function, breathlessness, diarrhoea and reflux remained worse than before surgery; emotional function was actually better[18]. The Swedish nationwide cohort compared against age- and sex-matched members of the general population: at six months after surgery most domains were clearly worse, and there was no improvement by three years[19]; followed to ten years (92 respondents), there was no improvement between five and ten years, 23 of 25 domains declined, and the four heaviest problems were reflux, eating difficulties, diarrhoea and loss of appetite[20]. So both sentences are true: compared with yourself before the operation, you slowly close the gap; compared with healthy people your age, the gap stays. Who is at higher risk? The same dataset shows age, sex and BMI were unrelated; people with comorbidity, stage III or IV disease, or a tumour in the middle or upper oesophagus did worse, and squamous cell carcinoma patients did worse than adenocarcinoma patients[21]. That is a predictor, not a recommendation; if you are a squamous cell carcinoma patient reading this, read it as "I should see the dietitian sooner".

The first month home: three things to do first

Buy a set of scales when you get home, weigh yourself every morning before eating, and record it on your phone; if you lose more than 10%, come back — do not wait for the next appointment. If you feel unwell after a meal, note the timing — within half an hour, or one to three hours later — that single piece of information lets us separate early from late dumping. If swallowing gets worse again within three months, or weight falls faster than expected, anastomotic stricture and recurrence have to be excluded first — that is the business of "Swallowing worse again: stricture or recurrence" — do not put it down to "eating too little". Bring three things to the next clinic visit: your weight record, the timing of your symptoms, and one question: "How long do I need my jejunostomy, and who decides when it comes out?"

From the physician

In the first three months after surgery, what I care about most is your weight, not how many mouthfuls you managed. Weigh yourself at home every morning before eating; if you lose more than 10%, or get palpitations and sweating within half an hour of a meal, or cold sweats and shaking hands one to three hours after, come back and see us. Bring your weight record and how the jejunostomy is being used to the next visit.
