WHY, AND HOW IT IS DECIDED

The operation is done — why radiotherapy as well?

After breast-conserving surgery, whole-breast irradiation is part of the package; after a mastectomy it turns on the node count — 0 nodes no, 4 or more recommended, 1 to 3 a decision to sit down and discuss.

Breast Radiotherapy Guide · Why, and How It Is Decided · 6 references
#Decisions#Staging#Surgery#NHI Coverage

One box on this map is particularly hard: 1 to 3 positive nodes after mastectomy, where the old meta-analysis says radiotherapy lowers breast cancer mortality and the new trial says ten-year overall survival is no different. The two pieces of evidence still do not meet, and I lay them out as they are without declaring a winner. At the end it also picks up how long the catastrophic illness certificate lasts — stage I breast cancer is not the same as the other stages.

"The tumour has been taken out cleanly, hasn't it — so why does anything still need irradiating?" That is how patients ask it after breast-conserving surgery. After a mastectomy the question comes in a different shape: "The whole breast is gone. What is there left to treat?" The two questions have different answers, and each has its own evidence behind it. This article draws "who needs radiotherapy after surgery" as a map you can use in clinic: the operation you had (conserving or mastectomy), whether the axillary nodes were involved, and how many were involved decide which box you land in — three of those boxes have a clear answer, and one of them is something to sit down and talk through.

My position first: every technique compared in this topic — ordinary intensity-modulated radiotherapy, TOMO, deep inspiration breath-hold — has a self-paid component among the more expensive options, and all of them are delivered in my own department; protons mean a referral elsewhere. So every "better" here comes with its primary source and the level of evidence behind it, and on cost I cite the official schedule where one exists and tell you to ask the medical affairs office where it does not. Concluding that the basic option is enough for you is a completely reasonable conclusion.

Breast conservation was designed as two halves, not an operation plus an extra

Breast-conserving surgery was built from the start as a two-stage design: the operation takes out what can be seen, the radiotherapy deals with what is left microscopically. The numbers hold that account up. The EBCTCG pooled 17 randomised trials and 10,801 women treated with breast-conserving surgery (most of them enrolled between the 1960s and the 1980s): without radiotherapy, any first recurrence within 10 years was 35.0%; with it, 19.3% — an absolute difference of 15.7 percentage points; breast cancer death at 15 years was 25.2% against 21.4%[1]. The meta-analysis puts it in its own words: radiotherapy halves the rate of recurrence and lowers breast cancer mortality by about one sixth; converted into people, for every 4 recurrences avoided within 10 years there is 1 fewer breast cancer death by 15 years[1]. The landmark NSABP B-06 followed its patients for 20 years (1,851 randomised): with lumpectomy alone, cumulative recurrence in the same breast was 39.2%; with radiotherapy added, 14.3%[2]. How breast conservation plus radiotherapy compares with mastectomy is the breast cancer topic's question, and I will say only one thing about it here: the three arms of B-06 did not differ in overall survival.

The same set of numbers has to be read honestly in the other direction as well: 65% of the women who had no radiotherapy were free of recurrence at 10 years, and 19.3% of the women who did have it recurred anyway[1]. Radiotherapy is not an insurance policy, and going without it is not a sentence; what it changes is a probability, and the size of that change is large enough that "breast conservation plus whole-breast irradiation" is discussed as one complete package.

The proportional benefit is much the same for everyone; the absolute benefit is yours alone

Take the same meta-analysis apart. Node-negative after breast-conserving surgery (pN0, 7,287 women): 10-year recurrence fell from 31.0% to 15.6%, and 15-year breast cancer death from 20.5% to 17.2%. Node-positive (pN+, only 1,050 women, so the confidence intervals are wider — a confidence interval is the range within which the true value plausibly sits, and a narrow one means the estimate is firm): recurrence fell from 63.7% to 42.5%, breast cancer death from 51.3% to 42.8%[1]. Proportionally the size of the cut is much the same across the groups, but the higher your own starting risk, the bigger the absolute number radiotherapy takes off it. These trials belong to an older era, and today's patients generally have absolute figures below the ones in the table — which is why I always carry the label "old-era meta-analysis, the proportional effect is the more trustworthy part" when I quote them. In the other direction, for a group of older women with small hormone-receptor-positive tumours there is omission to be discussed; the conditions and the price of it are in "Omitting radiotherapy: every condition has to hold", and I am not repeating them here.

After a mastectomy, the map reads the node count

Whether the chest wall and the nodal areas are treated after a mastectomy (including a modified radical mastectomy) turns on one variable: the axillary nodes. A second EBCTCG meta-analysis (22 trials, 8,135 women randomised between 1964 and 1986, chest wall plus regional nodes against none) gives three boxes[3]. 0 positive nodes after axillary dissection (700 women): radiotherapy lowered neither recurrence nor breast cancer death (rate ratios 1.06 and 1.18, neither significant — a rate ratio divides the speed at which events occur in one group by the speed in the other, so a ratio of one means no difference) — node-negative after mastectomy is not a routine indication. 4 or more positive nodes (1,772 women): overall recurrence rate ratio 0.79, breast cancer mortality rate ratio 0.87 — this is the box where the word I write in clinic is "recommended", and no randomised trial redone with modern drugs has challenged it. 1 to 3 (1,314 women) gets its own section below. One thing about the time scale while we are here: the breast cancer mortality benefit in this meta-analysis only showed itself in full once follow-up reached 20 years[3] — the accounts in radiotherapy have always been settled in decades.

1 to 3 positive nodes: not "recommended", but a decision to discuss

The old meta-analysis puts this box like this: with 1 to 3 positive nodes after axillary dissection, irradiating the chest wall plus the nodal areas pushed the breast cancer mortality rate ratio down to 0.80 (95% confidence interval 0.67 to 0.95); 1,133 of those women had systemic therapy in both arms, and the conclusion did not change[3]. But SUPREMO, which read out in 2025, redid the same question with modern systemic therapy: 1,607 "intermediate-risk" patients (pT1N1, pT2N1, pT3N0, or pT2N0 with grade 3 disease or lymphovascular invasion) were randomised after mastectomy, axillary surgery and systemic therapy to chest wall irradiation or none, with a median follow-up of 9.6 years — 10-year overall survival was 81.4% against 81.9% (hazard ratio 1.04, p=0.80, no difference; a hazard ratio compares the rate at which events happen in the two groups, and a ratio of one means the same rate in both), while chest wall recurrence was 1.1% against 2.5% (hazard ratio 0.45, a real difference)[4]. The two pieces of evidence do not meet, and the interventions were not the same either: the old trials treated the chest wall plus the regional nodes, SUPREMO treated the chest wall only; and SUPREMO's population is a mixed intermediate-risk group, which is not the same thing as "everyone with 1 to 3 positive nodes". So in my clinic this box is neither an automatic yes nor an automatic no. It is a decision made by putting the node count, the tumour size, the grade and the lymphovascular invasion out on the table and talking about them. How the two pieces of evidence are read side by side, in full, is in "Should the axilla and supraclavicular area be treated too?", and I am not repeating it here.

In Taiwan: how long the catastrophic illness certificate lasts depends on the stage

The administrative business is on this map too. After a breast cancer diagnosis you can apply for a catastrophic illness certificate: under the appendix to the current regulations (the version applying from 1 January 2025), the certificate for a stage I malignant tumour of the breast is valid for 3 years; every other stage falls under "other malignant tumours", where the certificate is valid for 5 years[5]. The diagnostic certificate used for the application is valid for 30 days from the date it is issued, and the insurer decides within 14 days of receiving it (public holidays not counted); while the certificate is valid, the regulations exempt from copayment the treatment that your treating doctor judges to be related to that illness[6] — post-operative radiotherapy belongs in that category, but that sentence is not the same as "radiotherapy is all free". The fractionation and the individual techniques each have their own provisions: fractionation is in "Why am I not on the shorter course?", and the techniques are in the group B articles of this topic.

Next time you are in clinic, work out which box you are in first

Take three pieces of information in with you: the type of operation, the number of positive axillary nodes (the pN line on the pathology report), and the grade together with the lymphovascular invasion. Then put the question like this: "With my operation and my node status, am I in the box where radiotherapy is recommended, the box where it is not, or the box that has to be discussed?" If you had a mastectomy with 1 to 3 positive nodes, add one more line: "Which features are you weighing up?" Ask those two questions out loud and this map has done its work.

From the physician

The pN line on your pathology report decides which box of this map you are in. Take the type of operation, the number of positive nodes and the grade with you to your next appointment and ask straight out: "Which box am I in? Which features are you weighing up?" That question can always be asked out loud, and it should get a specific answer.
