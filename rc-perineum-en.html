The “don’t wash it” rule has the evidence backwards

Why perineal skin breaks down during pelvic radiotherapy, what MASCC could and could not agree on, and the washing advice that two randomised trials contradict.

Rectal Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 8 references
#Skin Care#Side Effects#Daily Life

Two randomised trials tested whether patients should keep water off irradiated skin. Both found the people allowed to wash did better — and the perineum is the one place where not washing costs the most.

The perineum and the skin around the anus is what patients in a rectal cancer course put up with longest before mentioning it. Awkward place, hard to raise, and most people assume soreness means they have not washed properly. It is the other way round — this skin breaks down because of what it is, not because of anything you did.

The patient-education section of this site already covers general skin care during radiotherapy. This piece is only about what is particular to the perineum.

Why this patch of skin, of all places

Stack the conditions up and it becomes obvious: two folds of skin lying against each other, permanently damp, rubbed by walking and by sitting, and in contact with stool every day — loose stool, during the course. The nursing literature defines perineal-rectal care as the skin between the vulva or scrotum and the anus, notes that care of this area "is complicated by the anatomical location", and points out that cancer patients are at particular risk because of immunosuppression, the effects of radiotherapy and chemotherapy, and compromised nutritional status[1].

One line from that same paper is worth knowing before anything else: no standardised perineal-rectal care approach exists[1]. That is not a Taiwanese gap, it is an international one. So for everything below, I will tell you what evidence it stands on.

How badly does it break down, and for how many people

The skin data for preoperative rectal chemoradiation, usually around 50.4 Gy, is patchy. The best available upper bound comes from chemoradiation for anal cancer, where the perineum and the natal cleft sit inside the high-dose volume. In 155 patients treated with IMRT and image guidance, 36 Gy to the elective volume with a boost to 59.4 Gy, mostly with mitomycin C and 5-FU: grade 3 or worse acute toxicity was 22%, and it was "mainly as moist desquamation" (25 patients), against 10 patients with diarrhoea[2].

Read that as "what happens at a higher dose", not as "what will happen to you". The same study also shows the technique matters: grade 3 or worse acute toxicity was 38.5% with fixed-gantry sliding-window IMRT and 15.3% with volumetric modulated arc therapy or tomotherapy (p=0.049)[2].

"Keep water off the treated skin" — the evidence points the other way

This is the sentence I most want to take apart. It has been circulating for over thirty years, and there were randomised trials testing it thirty years ago.

Campbell and Illingworth's 1992 randomised controlled trial put 99 patients having breast or chest wall radiotherapy into three arms — no washing, water only, soap and water. Both subjective and objective measures of acute skin reaction were less in the two groups randomised to wash, and the authors concluded that washing should be encouraged[3].

Roy and colleagues repeated it in 2001 with 99 patients, 49 not washing and 50 washing with water and soap: moist desquamation was 33% in the non-washing group and 14% in the washing group; maximum grade 2 toxicity was 57% against 34%; pain, itching and burning scores were all higher in the non-washing group. The authors wrote it plainly — washing irradiated skin is not associated with increased skin toxicity and should not be discouraged[4].

The 2023 MASCC guideline on acute radiation dermatitis — 42 experts, four Delphi rounds, a 75% consensus threshold — could not make a recommendation on washing with water and soap, because the panel was split; it did reach consensus against recommending antiperspirants and deodorants as a preventive measure. But the same document closes the door on the misreading: antiperspirant, deodorant and washing are not methods of preventing acute radiation dermatitis, and their use should not be contraindicated during radiotherapy[5].

The honest version: there is no evidence that washing makes the skin worse, and none that not washing is safer. And in a place that meets stool every day, the cost of not washing is far higher than it is on a breast. The method is unremarkable — warm water, a mild unscented cleanser, no scrubbing, then pat dry with a towel rather than rubbing back and forth. One qualification: both trials were done in breast and chest wall radiotherapy, where the skin dose is lower than in the perineum. Carrying the conclusion across is inference, not evidence.

The short list of things the guideline actually agrees on

Here are the MASCC 2023 consensus results in the two categories the document uses — and remember first that this is a generic guideline: the panel states that it "does not provide site-specific or symptom-specific guidance", and lists developing site-specific recommendations as future work[5]. Nothing in it was written for the perineum.

  • Prevention: Hydrofilm polyurethane film (31/33, 94%), the topical steroids mometasone and betamethasone, and olive oil reached consensus; photobiomodulation and Mepitel film reached consensus in breast only.

  • Managing dermatitis once it has appeared: only Mepilex Lite foam dressings reached consensus (28/33, 85%). Nothing else did — including topical corticosteroids, where the conclusion in the management column is "no recommendation".

  • Consensus against: StrataXRT for prevention (30/33 against), Cavilon No Sting barrier film (30/33 against), a combination cream with fusidic acid and betamethasone (28/33 against), calcium alginate, hydrocolloid dressings, oral sucralfate, antihistamines. No consensus reached — neither recommended nor rejected: silver leaf nylon dressings (24/33, 73%, just short of the threshold), sulfadiazine silver, StrataXRT for management, and Mepitel film in head and neck.

The systematic review underneath the guideline covered 235 studies including 149 randomised trials, and its authors are equally reserved: because high-quality evidence is limited, recommendations could not be made on the published evidence alone[6]. So the items above are expert consensus, not trial conclusions.

Two things go before all of that. First, topical steroids are a prescription, not a bathroom-cupboard item. The consensus named mometasone and betamethasone; hydrocortisone and beclomethasone reached none. Steroid on macerated, fissured, possibly infected perineal skin is a wholly different proposition from steroid on intact breast skin, and it has to be a doctor's decision. Second, do not buy dressings yourself. Adhesion, shear and faecal soiling in the natal cleft turn dressing choice into a clinical judgement.

Moist desquamation has happened — getting through those days

Two sentences from the BSG 2025 guidance on gastrointestinal symptoms after cancer treatment are directly usable: skin irritation from incontinence may be helped by barrier agents; and for radiation dermatitis, topical agents such as hydroactive colloid gels are used for mild symptoms, and wound-healing agents or dressings for moderate to severe ones[7]. That passage is the closest fit for pelvic patients, because with loose stool and urgency, many people break down from faecal irritation first.

A few things I say in clinic, each with its evidence label attached. A warm sitz bath after opening the bowels is standard in Taiwanese medical centres — Taichung Veterans General Hospital's colorectal radiotherapy handout tells patients to care for the skin around the anus and to take a warm sitz bath after defaecating[8] — but I found no trial evidence that sitz baths improve radiation perineal dermatitis, so it is a comfort measure and local practice, not a treatment. Loose, breathable cotton with no seam in the natal cleft is the same: sense and experience, no trial. Dry with cool air or by patting, never a hairdryer on hot.

One thing I would ask you not to do: do not get so worried that you stop moving, stop washing and stop changing. Skin left sitting in damp and stool matters more than any choice of dressing.

When it stops being sore skin and becomes an infection

You are on 5-FU or capecitabine at the same time and your white count will fall; broken perineal skin plus faecal contamination is a very real route in. The MASCC panel notes that sulfadiazine silver might help prevent secondary infection once moist desquamation has appeared, but that the evidence for primary prevention is insufficient[5]. Infection is a separate problem, to be treated as one — not something a change of dressing sorts out.

Contact the team the same day, or go to the emergency department, for either of these:

  • A fever of 38 °C or above. Fever during chemoradiation is treated as possible neutropenic sepsis.

  • Redness spreading further each day, pain bad enough that you cannot sit, or a wound with pus or an odour.

The rest of the chemotherapy emergency list — severe diarrhoea with dehydration, mouth ulcers that stop you swallowing, worsening numbness in the hands and feet, hand-foot syndrome, cold-induced neuropathy — is the same list as in "Ten trips to the toilet and nothing feels finished", where it is written out in full.

Ask before the skin breaks, not after

The earliest question is about the field: does it cover the perineum and the natal cleft, and roughly what dose does the skin there get? The answer tells you whether to prepare early rather than hunt for supplies once it hurts. Then ask which dressing or ointment your hospital would use if the skin does break down, and whether it is covered by National Health Insurance (健保) or paid out of pocket — I could not verify the reimbursement status of dressings and barrier products for radiation dermatitis from any official NHI Administration document, so ask your case manager (個管師) rather than trusting what you read online.

And ask one question before anything happens: if I am too sore to sit, or the broken area spreads, might the radiotherapy have to be paused? Knowing the threshold in advance beats asking on the day. The length of the course is itself part of the treatment, and that trade-off belongs to the two of you, not to you alone at home.

From the physician

None of this is a perineum-specific guideline, because none exists; every recommendation above was written for other body sites and carried across. Ask which dressing or ointment your hospital would use, and whether you pay for it, before the skin breaks rather than after. And settle in advance what would make your team pause the course.
