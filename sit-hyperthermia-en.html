BEYOND STANDARD TREATMENT

Heating a tumour to forty degrees: what is that doing?

Ablation is a different subject. This is 40 to 44 °C, used to make radiotherapy or chemotherapy work better; the evidence is filed cancer by cancer.

Situation Guides · Beyond Standard Treatment · 20 references
#Newer therapies#Reading evidence#Radiotherapy#Decisions

This is my own field, so two results that do not flatter it go first: in the bladder trial the authors recommend against it, and in the pancreatic trial the primary endpoint was not met. Then the sentence I most want kept — the machine being switched on and the heat reaching the tumour are not the same thing.

Hyperthermia is what I do. I said so at the top of the prostate article, and I say it again because this piece is about the method itself. Most people who ask have not yet worked out what it is, so I describe it in full. The evidence section is written as it stands, and it does not get warmed up because the subject happens to be mine.

The word you searched for is covering at least three different things

In oncology the definition is narrow: a target temperature of roughly 40 to 44 °C, used to make radiotherapy or chemotherapy work better. That is how the European Society for Hyperthermic Oncology's technical committee framed it — an addition to another treatment, never given on its own[1].

Taiwanese regulation draws the line even more plainly. Appendix 2, item 10 of the Regulations Governing Specific Medical Techniques (特管辦法) defines a "deep diathermy system" as a radiofrequency or microwave generator able to heat the patient to 40 to 45 °C, which may be combined with radiotherapy or chemotherapy, and expressly excludes invasive radiofrequency or microwave ablation devices and focused ultrasound, which produce temperatures above 60 °C[2] (current version effective 1 January 2026; verified 2026-08-30). Radiofrequency ablation, HIFU and cryoablation are a different subject. Those are meant to destroy tissue.

What forty-odd degrees actually does inside the body

The mechanism textbooks lead with is inhibition of DNA repair: above 43 °C, biochemical and cytogenetic data both point that way. Work in repair-deficient cell lines suggests the step that matters is base excision repair rather than non-homologous end joining — a hypothesis reached by exclusion, not a settled finding[3].

In the clinic the temperature usually does not get that far. Most clinical work has only reached 40 to 42 °C, and in that range neither direct cell killing nor vascular damage is substantial; what mild heating does there is produce a sustained rise in tumour blood flow and oxygenation, seen in animals and in humans[4].

"Did you have it" is the wrong question; "did the heat get in" is the right one

Thermal dose is measured in CEM43 — the time-temperature curve converted into cumulative equivalent minutes at 43 °C. That reference point was chosen arbitrarily by the authors of the 1984 paper, and the formula does not assume different tissues are equally heat-sensitive[5]. What gets reported in practice is usually CEM43T90, the level reached at 90% of the measurement points.

A systematic review in recurrent breast cancer covering 22 papers and 2,330 patients reports that patients with a higher thermal dose had complete response about 34% higher (range 27 to 53%) — and that the higher the maximum temperature, the more heat-related toxicity there was[6]. Read that according to its design: one cohort who all had hyperthermia, sorted afterwards by the thermal dose actually achieved, not randomised into two arms. Lesions that can be heated to a high dose sit in different places, in different bodies. It supports "whether the heat really went in is related to the outcome"; it does not support "heat it more and you buy a better result".

How much power a machine puts out, and how far it pushes the temperature, decides the size and depth of tumour it can heat at all[1]. A chest-wall recurrence is superficial; cervix, thigh sarcoma and pelvic recurrence are deep, and the equipment differs. The 1995 randomised melanoma trial left one sentence behind: because of equipment difficulties, only 14% of treatments reached the target set in the protocol[7].

What a course of it looks like from the chair

A 2024 survey of 12 European sarcoma centres found that 9 set an intratumoural target of 40 to 43 °C, and all 12 measured temperature with skin sensors or intraluminal probes[8].

In the trials below it is given once or twice a week, slotted into a radiotherapy course or onto a chemotherapy day, holding the target temperature about 60 minutes plus warm-up. It is an outpatient appointment. It is added to the treatment you were already having: it does not mean fewer radiotherapy fractions and it does not mean a lower chemotherapy dose.

Heating first or irradiating first seems not to matter; on the gap between them, two differently designed datasets disagree. A retrospective study of 400 women with cervical cancer found gaps of 30 to 230 minutes had no effect on any clinical outcome[9]. A preclinical experiment plus a retrospective series of 58 women found the opposite: a shorter gap produced 2 to 8 times more DNA double-strand breaks, with five-year survival 22% (80 minutes or more) against 54% (under 80 minutes)[10]. There is no settled answer here and I am not going to pretend there is.

The evidence is filed by cancer type, and it cannot be borrowed across

"Hyperthermia improves the effect of radiotherapy" holds only in the cancers where a randomised trial tested it; elsewhere it is either untested or has been tested and come out negative.

  • Cervix and pelvis — this is where the randomised data sit. The Dutch pelvic trial randomised 358 patients; complete response across the whole group was 39% against 55%, and in the cervical group three-year overall survival was 27% against 51%, complete response 57% against 83%. The authors kept it tight: other pelvic sites need larger studies before anything is recommended[11]. A meta-analysis of six randomised trials (427 patients) gives a risk difference of +22% for complete response and +23% for long-term local control (a risk difference is one group's percentage minus the other's — 22 percentage points), but the 8.4% survival difference was not statistically significant[12]. Added to modern concurrent chemoradiotherapy it is a separate question: a randomised trial in five Japanese hospitals, 101 patients, complete response 88% against 77.6%, while five-year overall survival 77.8% against 64.8% was not statistically significant[13].

  • Recurrent breast cancer on the chest wall. Five randomised trials pooling 306 patients, local complete response 41% against 59%. Not every trial showed an advantage alone, and the advantage was concentrated in recurrences in previously irradiated areas, where only a low further dose was possible[14].

  • Soft-tissue sarcoma. A phase 3 trial, 341 randomised, 11.3 years of follow-up: hazard ratio 0.65 for local progression-free survival (a hazard ratio compares how fast events accrue in two arms, not a multiple of probability; 0.65 means progression arrived more slowly in the heated arm, not "the risk is only 65% of what it was") and 0.73 for overall survival[15]. But there it was added to chemotherapy, not to radiotherapy.

  • Head and neck. A meta-analysis of six two-arm studies, 451 patients (five randomised): complete response 39.6% with radiotherapy alone against 62.5% with radiotherapy plus hyperthermia[16]. What has to be read carefully: the control arm was radiotherapy alone, and most of those trials predate the era of chemoradiotherapy.

  • Bladder cancer is the counter-example. HIVEC-II, randomised phase 2, 259 patients: 24-month disease-free survival 61% against 60%; on intention-to-treat analysis, progression-free survival was worse in the hyperthermia arm; and only 59% of that arm completed treatment against 89% of the control. The authors recommend against it[17].

  • Pancreatic cancer is a clear failure. The HEAT randomised trial, 117 patients after resection: the primary endpoint of disease-free survival was 12.7 against 11.2 months, p=0.394, not met[18]. A result like that goes in as well.

  • In prostate cancer, not one patient has ever been randomised. That question is dealt with in "How far the evidence for hyperthermia goes".

Whole-body hyperthermia is a different thing and does not belong in the same breath as regional deep hyperthermia. The latest human data are a phase 1 trial in 12 patients, 41.5 °C held for 2 to 6 hours, with safety and tolerability as the only primary endpoints; the authors say it supports designing a randomised trial to assess efficacy[19]. A phase 1 trial can tell you what safety looked like at that dose. It cannot tell you the treatment works.

There is a code for it in Taiwan, which is not the same as your course being covered

Under 特管辦法 the institution must be a hospital; the doctor a specialist with at least two years of clinical cancer practice who has completed clinical training or a hyperthermia course of at least twenty hours; the technician a radiographer or nurse with at least ten hours of training, who works alongside the operating doctor[2] (verified 2026-08-30). Article 6 requires a medical device licence to be filed before registration — which makes "may I see the licence for this machine" a reasonable question. Which devices hold one I could not verify, so I am not writing it either way.

There is one NHI code: 37001B, "hyperthermia treatment, per session", 2,158 points, effective 2004-07-01. I searched the item list and the NHI fee schedule (支付標準): this item carries no indication list and no payment note[20] (verified 2026-08-30). There is no national price list for the self-pay version and there cannot be one — charges are approved hospital by hospital by each municipal or county authority. Getting a written itemisation is in "How to ask about a self-pay item".

Three sentences I can answer if you say them to me

  1. For my cancer, in my situation, is there a randomised trial, or only single-arm and retrospective data? Single-arm data says what happened to that group of people. It cannot say "better than standard treatment", because there was no other group to compare with.

  2. How is temperature measured, what is the target, what happens if we miss it, how many sessions? Four in ten patients in HIVEC-II did not finish. Asking this is not being difficult.

  3. If I do not have it, what part of my treatment plan goes missing? Not having had hyperthermia does not mean there is a hole in your treatment.

New and better are two different things — "New does not mean better". Approved, covered and effective are three different things — "Approved, covered, effective: three different things". Those two are the rulers I read this whole section of the site with.

From the physician

Written deliberately conservatively. It is added on: no fewer radiotherapy fractions, no reduced chemotherapy dose. Each cancer's evidence counts only for that cancer and cannot be borrowed across. NHI item 37001B carries no indication list and no payment note — having a code is not the same as your course being covered.
