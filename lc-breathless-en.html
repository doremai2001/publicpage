Breathlessness, blood, fluid: which one is today

Same-day or ambulance, for breathlessness, coughing blood, pleural fluid and superior vena cava syndrome — plus one Taiwan gap worth asking about.

Lung Cancer Guide · Side Effects, Emergencies and Follow-up · 14 references
#Lung#Urgent#Side Effects#Quality of Life#NHI Coverage

Three problems, three different clocks. What follows is written to be usable when you are tired and frightened and it is the middle of the night: observable behaviour, a phone number, a decision. Read the section you need now and settle the rest in daylight.

Breathlessness, coughing blood and fluid on the lung come up about as often as each other in clinic, and are nothing like each other in urgency. This article does one job: it draws the line between "clinic today", "call an ambulance now" and "raise it next time" clearly enough to follow at three in the morning. Every threshold below is a behaviour you can observe, not "see a doctor if you feel unwell".

First, separate tiredness from breathlessness

Tiredness is not recovering after you have done something, and "The tiredness rest doesn't fix" has finished that subject. Breathlessness is one more stop on the same walk. They often arrive together; only the second is what this article measures.

So the threshold is this: the same walk, the same stairs, and now you need one more stop; or lying flat makes you breathless and you need an extra pillow to sleep — clinic, the same day. Markedly worse within hours, or unable to finish a sentence — emergency department. You do not have to work out the cause first.

You can buy a pulse oximeter and use it, but it only ever adds. The British Thoracic Society emergency oxygen guideline calls oxygen saturation the fifth vital sign, with a target of 94–98% for most acutely ill patients and 88–92% or a patient-specific target range for those at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure, such as people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)[1]. A new drop below 94% at rest, or below the range your team set for you, is same-day. One sentence from the same guideline goes with that number: oxygen is a treatment for hypoxaemia, not breathlessness; in patients who are not hypoxaemic, oxygen has not been proven to have any consistent effect on the sensation of breathlessness[1]. A normal reading is not a reason to stay at home.

One pointer about direction. Breathlessness from fluid usually builds over days to weeks, so becoming suddenly breathless within hours usually points elsewhere — pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, a large bleed. Pulmonary embolism is in "A swollen calf: can it wait until tomorrow?", at the same threshold. A new cough with breathlessness after treatment should make you think of the three pneumonitides: "Breathless after treatment — which pneumonitis is it".

Blood streaks and a whole mouthful are two different events

About one in five people with lung cancer coughs blood[2]. A 2025 narrative review sets out the grading in common use: under 100 mL in 24 hours is mild; 100 to 500 mL is moderate, when you may cough up a whole mouthful of blood; over 500 mL, or a single episode of more than 100 mL, is severe[2]. The same review says plainly there is no consensus on this definition — published cut-offs run from "50 mL in one episode" to "1,000 mL over 48 hours" — and volume is not the only criterion; breathing and consciousness count too[2]. Do not measure blood in a jug. Hold two anchors.

  • Streaks of blood in the sputum, new or increasing: clinic, the same day. Mild haemoptysis is more than 90% of cases and does well, but that review states that even mild haemoptysis should not be ignored[2].

  • A whole mouthful of blood, or a single episode of more than 100 mL: call 119, do not drive yourself.

The next sentence decides what you do while the ambulance comes. Death in major haemoptysis is from asphyxiation, not blood loss — the review defines it as any haemoptysis that is life-threatening or capable of obstructing the airway and causing asphyxiation[2]. So: do not lie on your back, and do not "lie down and rest". If you know which side is bleeding, lie on that side; cough the blood out rather than hold it in; call 119 at the same time. Lying on your back lets blood run into the other lung.

Two riders. If you take an anticoagulant or antiplatelet drug, those drugs can themselves cause or worsen bleeding[2] and your threshold is lower — bleeding means phone, it does not mean stop your own medication, which is the same sentence as in "A swollen calf: can it wait until tomorrow?". And coughing blood with a fever is not a coincidence: in a Paris cohort of 130 patients admitted to intensive care with severe haemoptysis, 39% had a microbiologically confirmed lower respiratory tract infection[3]. The fever threshold is the one from "The fever that cannot wait until morning": one ear or axillary reading of 38.0°C or above counts. That cohort is the most severe end; do not read it as the risk of ordinary haemoptysis.

Fluid that fills again is not a wasted drain

Malignant pleural effusion affects about 15% of people with cancer[4]. The 2018 ATS/STS/STR official clinical practice guideline makes seven recommendations, and all seven are conditional. Three matter most here: an asymptomatic malignant effusion should not be drained or treated; with symptoms and a lung thought to be expandable, either an indwelling pleural catheter or chemical pleurodesis is reasonable; and a large-volume thoracentesis comes first, to assess symptom response and lung expansion[5]. That last one is where people misunderstand: the first large drain is an assessment step — does breathlessness improve once it is out, does the lung re-expand — and the answer decides the next move. Fluid coming back does not mean the drain was pointless.

The randomised evidence on the two routes looks like this. TIME2 (106 patients at 7 UK hospitals) compared an indwelling pleural catheter with a chest tube plus talc pleurodesis: daily self-rated breathlessness scores over 42 days were near identical (a difference of 0.16 mm, 95% confidence interval −6.82 to 7.15). The differences lay elsewhere: median initial hospital stay 0 days against 4, further pleural procedures 6% against 22%, but more adverse events with the catheter (21 of 52 against 7 of 54)[6]. OPTIMUM (142 patients) tested the outpatient route head-on: at day 30 overall health status had improved in both arms with no difference between them, and the authors concluded that "the outpatient pathway using an IPC was not superior to inpatient treatment with a chest drain"[7]. In a meta-analysis of 4,983 patients the catheter complication rate was 20.3%, infection 5.7%[8]. So this is not "which is better"; the costs are simply different.

One more thing worth asking: the fluid drawn off can be sent for driver gene testing at the same time, covered in "Those two weeks of waiting are not wasted".

The indwelling catheter route: I could not find it in the NHI listings

This is where a Western article will mislead you most. Repeated drainage, a chest tube, and instilling an agent for pleurodesis are all National Health Insurance (健保) items in Taiwan: thoracentesis 29012B 1,000 points, chest tube insertion 56010B 2,400 points, intrapleural chemical instillation 37033B 1,339 points, pleurodesis 67034B 9,199 points, pleural fluid analysis 16003C 170 points[9].

But the tunnelled indwelling catheter from those overseas trials, the kind you take home and drain yourself, I searched for line by line through the NHI special-materials listing (特材收載品項表, 9,839 rows) and could not find a matching item[10]; nor could I find a corresponding placement procedure code in the full payment schedule (支付標準)[9]. The portable chest drainage device the NHI does list has conditions (A211-2) written for pneumothorax: "a persistent air leak beyond 7 days despite initial treatment, unsuitable for surgery, and assessed by the attending physician as able to be cared for at home"[11].

Read this literally: absent from a listing is not the same as the NHI stating in writing that it is not covered. What I can say is "I could not find a listed item", not "the NHI does not pay for it". So in Taiwan those two routes are not equivalent options. If you want the indwelling catheter route, settle how the cost is calculated first — please confirm with your case manager (個管師) or the hospital billing office (醫務課). The codes and clauses above were verified 2026-08-30, and the full payment schedule is the 114.03.13 version. How to ask about a self-pay item and get an answer — which fields, when, and who may answer — is in "How to ask about a self-pay item".

Superior vena cava syndrome: which is same-day, which is an ambulance

It is less common than textbooks suggest: in a systematic review, superior vena cava obstruction was present at diagnosis in 10.0% of small cell and 1.7% of non-small cell lung cancer[12]. Its thresholds need no measurement. A 2026 vascular surgery review describes patients whose disease has progressed as unable to lie flat, needing several pillows to sleep, and uncomfortable when they bend forward or tie their shoes[13]. So:

  • Clinic, same day: waking with a swollen face or eyelids; veins standing out on the neck or upper chest that were not there before; a necklace or collar becoming tight; needing extra pillows to sleep; a full head or breathlessness when you bend to tie a shoe[13].

  • Call an ambulance now: too breathless to speak, a high-pitched noise on breathing in (stridor), a changed voice, altered consciousness or drowsiness[13]. Laryngeal oedema and cerebral oedema are the emergencies the literature recognises[14].

Two things to be clear about. First, apart from laryngeal and cerebral oedema, the literature's position is to get a pathological diagnosis before starting treatment[14] — so on "should we start radiotherapy immediately", the answer is usually that getting the diagnosis right matters more than speed. Second, on steroids, that review says: no study has described the efficacy of steroids in superior vena cava obstruction, and, like the timing of stenting, it remains uncertain[12]. That means "there is still no randomised evidence on this", not "steroids do not work", and it is not a reason to refuse them. While symptoms are mild, sleeping propped up, avoiding tight collars and avoiding bending forward are the conservative measures the literature lists.

There is another gap in Taiwan worth asking about. The procedure of stenting the superior vena cava is an NHI item: peripheral vascular stent placement 33133B 12,948 points, complex 33147B 24,278 points (effective 2024-09-01), with a note that lists the superior vena cava among the venous sites and requires the insurer's prior approval[9]. But the rule for the stent itself (A220-15) is written for "patients with central venous (including superior vena cava) stenosis in whom the stenosis recurs within 3 months of effective treatment", and requires prior review[11]. Whether a first stent placed for tumour compression meets that clause, I cannot tell from the text — please confirm with your case manager or the hospital billing office whether it is self-paid, verified 2026-08-30.

Four sentences to settle today, for use in the middle of the night

One: "how breathless do I have to be before I come in the same day, and which emergency department at night?" Two: "if I cough up a whole mouthful of blood, do I call 119 or call you first?" Settle it now and you will not hesitate at night. Three: "my effusion needs treating — which options can be done here, and how many days in hospital each?" Four: "if I need an indwelling catheter or a superior vena cava stent, will the NHI cover it, and how is any self-paid portion calculated? Could you ask the billing office for me?" Write those four and your case manager's number on one sheet and stick it by the door.

From the physician

Thresholds set at clinic beat thresholds read online, because yours depend on your lung function and on what has already been treated. The indwelling catheter paragraph is the one to take with you: what I could not find in a listing is not a statement that the NHI declines to pay, and only the billing office can tell you what it would cost.
