SIDE EFFECTS AND LIVING WITH THEM

Does rehabilitation work, and what happens to ejaculation

The drugs work while you take them. Three randomised trials with drug-free washouts say they do not restore unassisted function.

Prostate Cancer Guide · Side Effects and Living With Them · 18 references
#Prostate#Sexual Function#Side Effects#Quality of Life#Rehabilitation

Two things almost nobody is told before treatment: what happens to ejaculate volume, and that leaking at orgasm is common. Both have names, and both can be raised.

In my clinic this question usually comes out after the family has left the room. "Sex during treatment: who raises it?" already covers talking to a partner and raising it with the team, so this piece is only the prostate-specific part: nerve-sparing, the drugs, rehabilitation timing, ejaculation.

Definitions first, or three numbers will contradict each other

Almost every trial defines "still has sexual function" differently. Three are common: an IIEF-EF domain score of 22 or above, an IIEF-EF above 21, and the man's own answer to "erections firm enough for intercourse". The three do not convert into each other. A systematic review of 15 robot-assisted prostatectomy studies shows the cost: "potent" at 12 months ranged 54% to 90%, at 24 months 63% to 94%[1]. The spread is the point: it reflects definitions, populations and surgeons, not technique.

Surgery is a cliff then a climb; radiotherapy is a slope

ProtecT used one definition across all three groups — erections firm enough for intercourse. At year 7: 18% after surgery, 30% on active monitoring, 27% after radiotherapy; by year 12 all three had converged at a low level[2]. The six-year report gives the shape: radiotherapy's effect was largest at six months, then partly recovered and stayed level; surgery recovered partly but stayed worse than the other two throughout; monitoring declined gradually[3]. That last matters: active monitoring is not "nothing happens". Age alone takes points off.

The American CEASAR cohort used the EPIC-26 sexual domain score (0 to 100, higher better; 10 to 12 points the smallest clinically visible difference). In the low-risk group, nerve-sparing prostatectomy was 15.2 points worse than active surveillance at year 3 (95% confidence interval −18.8 to −11.5); in the high-risk group, external beam radiotherapy with hormone therapy was 12.5 points better than surgery at year 5 (6.2 to 18.7)[4].

The robot again: the Brisbane trial in Australia, 326 men, found no significant difference between groups in the EPIC sexual domain or the IIEF at 6, 12 or 24 months (EPIC 45.70 against 46.90 at 24)[5]. What decides whether nerves can be spared is where the tumour sits and how far it reaches, not the machine.

Radiotherapy without hormones: where the ceiling actually is

Plenty of men assume the loss of sexual function after radiotherapy is all hormone therapy. PACE-B did not permit it, so its numbers are the reference for radiotherapy alone. Defined as erections firm enough for intercourse, the stereotactic group went from 35% at baseline (133/374) to 17% at five years (43/250), the conventional and moderately hypofractionated group from 40% (157/391) to 20% (47/240); over the same period, moderate or big problems with sexual function rose from 24% to 31% and 22% to 32%[6].

Baseline was only 35% to 40% to begin with. A substantial share of men this age already have erectile difficulty before treatment, so establishing where you are beforehand beats attributing it afterwards. Hormone therapy is different — it takes out desire and erection at once — and that is in "What hormone therapy costs you".

Penile rehabilitation: widely done, and the randomised trials are negative

This runs against what circulates in clinic, so it gets room. Three randomised trials built in a drug-free washout, all with one endpoint: unassisted erectile function after the drug stops.

  • REACTT enrolled 423 men at 50 centres in nine countries, all with normal erectile function before surgery and bilateral nerve-sparing, randomised to daily tadalafil, on-demand tadalafil or placebo: 9 months of treatment, then 6 weeks of washout. On the primary endpoint (IIEF-EF of 22 or above after washout) the groups were 20.9%, 16.9% and 19.1%, odds ratios 1.1 (0.6 to 2.1) and 0.9 (0.5 to 1.7) — an odds ratio divides one group's "with against without" odds by the other's, not a multiple of probability, so 1.1 means no difference was seen. The authors concluded unassisted erectile function had not improved after withdrawal. Scores during treatment were better, and the daily group had significantly less penile shortening (by 4.1 millimetres)[7].

  • The multicentre vardenafil trial enrolled 628 men at 87 centres, 9 months double-blind plus 2 months of washout: afterwards no statistical difference between the groups, while during treatment on-demand dosing beat placebo significantly[8].

  • A 100-man sildenafil trial compared nightly with on-demand dosing, 12 months then a one-month washout: no difference at any time point on any endpoint (scores P=0.765, proportion returning to an IIEF-EF above 21 P=0.830)[9].

A systematic review of 2,317 men with trial sequential analysis is plainer still: the available trials do not support PDE5 inhibitors restoring drug-free erectile function, although long-term low-dose tadalafil may help preserve erectile tissue integrity; it also found starting more than six months after surgery did not give worse drug-assisted recovery[10]. So "miss the golden three months and it is too late" is a sentence the evidence will not hold up.

The evidence pointing the other way goes in too. In a network meta-analysis of 22 randomised trials and 2,711 men, only two of 16 strategies beat placebo: pelvic floor muscle training (odds ratio 5.21, 95% confidence interval 1.24 to 29.8, so wide it carries almost no information, certainty low) and regular high-dose sildenafil (4.00, 1.40 to 13.4, moderate certainty), while on-demand dosing was not shown to beat placebo[11] — the opposite direction to the previous review, on another endpoint. For vacuum devices, a meta-analysis of 6 randomised trials and 273 men found early use improved IIEF-5 and penile shortening, with study quality rated limited[12]; in a four-arm randomised trial of 100 men the combination arm scored better at 6 and 12 months, but at 12 months the proportion returning to target erectile function did not differ significantly across the four groups[13].

So here is how I put it in clinic. The drugs work while you take them — that much is clear. They are not a course of treatment that grows the nerve back. The reasons to do rehabilitation are keeping tissue, keeping confidence, and having a sex life during recovery — not better odds of an erection without a tablet later. Not the nicer version, but you will not feel misled a year from now.

The changes to ejaculation and orgasm that almost nobody mentions beforehand

This is the section I most want read: men find out afterwards, and mostly cannot bring themselves to ask.

  • After radical prostatectomy the orgasm is always dry: the prostate and seminal vesicles are gone, so there is no semen to emit. An anatomical consequence, not a complication, and not a sign anything went wrong.

  • Leaking urine at orgasm (climacturia) is commoner than most men assume: a systematic review of 13 studies, 5,208 men, 1,417 cases put prevalence at 27.2%[14]. In a prospective study of 749 men, 221 (29.5%) reported it, with no difference between robot-assisted and open surgery; painful orgasm was 11.6% after open and 7.1% after robot-assisted (P=0.04). It gets better, on a scale of years: counting men no longer having climacturia, 48% of the robot-assisted group were free of it at 84 months against 15% of the open group[15].

  • The changes after radiotherapy come with startling numbers: a meta-analysis of 17 observational studies and 2,156 men found 85% had reduced ejaculate volume (95% confidence interval 81 to 89%), 18% no ejaculation at all (11 to 36%), and 24% found ejaculation uncomfortable (16 to 35%)[16]. All three are proportions of men affected, not how far the volume fell. That 85% is a figure almost nobody is told beforehand.

Things can be done — for climacturia, emptying the bladder before sex, a constriction ring, surgical options too. What it takes is saying it out loud, with the words "climacturia" and "dry orgasm" to hand. If you do not raise it, the clinic will not guess.

Where Taiwan actually stands: no coverage clause for this class of drug

I read section 5 of the current National Health Insurance (健保) drug coverage rules (hormones and drugs affecting endocrine mechanisms, updated 115.08.21) end to end: there is no coverage clause anywhere for a PDE5 inhibitor used for erectile dysfunction[17]. The tadalafil product the scheme does list is Adcirca, provisionally reimbursed under an announcement of 114 (2025), its rule in section 2, cardiovascular and renal drugs, indication pulmonary arterial hypertension, not erectile dysfunction[18]. In practice you pay out of pocket; prices vary by hospital and are in no public document I found, so ask the pharmacy or billing office (醫務課). For vacuum devices, intracavernosal injections and penile implants I likewise found no formal document on coverage; ask your case manager (個管師). Sphincters and male slings are covered, and those conditions are in "Why continence rates never agree".

One safety point, not a formality: if you take nitrates for your heart — sublingual tablets and long-acting forms alike — you must not use this class of erection drug. The age range for prostate cancer overlaps heavily with coronary disease, and I have more than once met a man who bought it at a pharmacy and told nobody. Tell the doctor who prescribes your cardiac medication first.

One set of symptoms has nothing to do with sexual function, but hold on to it while you are on hormone therapy: new back pain together with leg weakness, numbness, or a change in bladder or bowel function means being seen the same day, not at your next appointment. That is how spinal cord compression presents; the window is measured in hours, and function lost past it does not reliably come back. Say the words "spinal cord compression" and emergency-department triage speed changes completely. The full section is in "Back pain plus a weak leg: do not wait".

Some of this belongs with your partner in the room, and some does not

Before surgery: "given where my tumour sits, can both nerve bundles be spared? If only one, what difference does that make?" That question decides more than any rehabilitation plan.

After surgery or radiotherapy: "is this drug for my sex life now, or has someone told me it will grow the function back?" Aligning the expectation makes disappointment at six months less likely.

And one with your partner there: "what will change about ejaculation, and will I leak urine at orgasm?" Both are easier said in advance than explained afterwards.

From the physician

Where the nerves run relative to your tumour decides more than any rehabilitation plan, so ask about it before surgery, not after. And if you take nitrates for your heart, this class of drug is off the table — tell the doctor who prescribes them before you take anything.
