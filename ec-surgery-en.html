WHAT THE EVIDENCE SAYS FOR EACH TREATMENT

What surgery removes, and where the stomach goes

The randomised trials on operation type, anastomosis site, minimally invasive and robotic surgery, plus the real leak rates and 90-day mortality from the large registries — my surgical colleagues' operation, explained clearly.

Oesophageal Cancer Guide · What the Evidence Says for Each Treatment · 15 references
#Oesophagectomy#Surgery#Side Effects#Evidence

Join in the chest or the neck, open or thoracoscopic, clear the neck nodes or not — each has randomised data; a leak rate above 10% and 90-day mortality of 4.5% are high-volume-centre figures. This article lines them up so that when the surgeon explains, you know what each term is asking.

"What exactly does the operation take out? Once the stomach is pulled up, do I still have a stomach?" My surgical colleagues explain this once in their clinic, and patients often arrive at mine still hazy about it — not because it was explained badly, but because that operation involves too many organs to absorb at the first hearing. Below I take it in order: what is removed, where the join is made, how the different approaches differ, and what the complication and death rates in the registries actually are, every number with its primary source so you can take it back to your surgeon. The decision about whether to operate is not in this article; it is in "After chemoradiotherapy, do you still need surgery".

What goes is a length of oesophagus plus the surrounding nodes, and then the stomach is made into a tube

An oesophagectomy does not remove just the segment with the tumour. The standard operation takes out most of the thoracic oesophagus together with the surrounding lymph nodes, then tailors the stomach into a tube, pulls it upwards and joins it to the remaining oesophagus. Depending on which parts of the body are opened and where the join is made, the operation has three names: Ivor Lewis is abdomen plus right chest, with the join (surgeons call it the anastomosis) inside the chest; McKeown is abdomen, chest and then neck, with the anastomosis in the neck; transhiatal does not open the chest, drawing the oesophagus out through the abdomen and the neck. The French MIRO trial's definition is clear: a two-field abdominal plus thoracic operation, that is Ivor Lewis, suited to mid or lower oesophageal tumours[1]. On whether to open the chest there is one old dataset: a Dutch randomised trial in NEJM in 2002 (220 patients with adenocarcinoma, mid-to-lower oesophagus and cardia) compared extended transthoracic resection with transhiatal; the transthoracic group had more complications, in-hospital death was no different, and five-year overall survival of 39% against 29% showed a trend that did not reach statistical significance[2]. In Taiwan most disease is squamous cell carcinoma and the tumour is often in the mid oesophagus, so what you will hear about is mostly the first two. What eating is like once the stomach has become the oesophagus is a whole article, "Eating after an oesophagectomy".

Join it in the chest or in the neck — there is randomised data on this

For the same length of oesophagus, where the anastomosis went used to be a matter of each unit's habit; now there are two randomised trials. The Dutch ICAN trial (9 high-volume centres, 245 analysable patients, mid-to-lower oesophagus and junction, minimally invasive surgery): intrathoracic against cervical anastomosis, anastomotic leak needing re-intervention 12.3% against 31.7%, all leaks 12.3% against 34.1%; the intrathoracic group had 11.3 percentage points fewer severe complications and 7.3 percentage points less recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy (vocal cord problems), and did better on three quality-of-life domains — swallowing, choking and speech; intensive care days and mortality were no different[3]. A 2026 single-centre Chinese randomised trial (272 patients with mid-to-lower squamous cell carcinoma, minimally invasive): Ivor Lewis against McKeown, leak 8.1% against 16.9%, anastomotic stricture 6.6% against 22.8%, recurrent laryngeal nerve injury 0 against 3.7%, progression-free survival no different[4]. The two trials point the same way: where the tumour position allows, joining inside the chest means fewer leaks and fewer vocal cord problems. That does not make a cervical anastomosis wrong — when the tumour sits high and enough safety margin has to be kept, the anastomosis has to go up, and that is a judgement your surgeon makes looking at your images.

Minimally invasive, open, robotic: what each of three randomised trials actually compared

"Is minimally invasive better" depends on what it is being compared with. The TIME trial (the Netherlands and others, 5 centres, 115 patients): thoracoscopic minimally invasive against open thoracotomy, pulmonary infection within two weeks of surgery 9% against 29%, during the hospital stay 12% against 34%; in-hospital death 1 patient in the open group and 2 in the minimally invasive group[5]. The MIRO trial (France, 207 patients): a hybrid approach with laparoscopy for the abdomen but still an open thoracotomy, against fully open surgery, complications within thirty days needing at least medication or an intervention (Clavien-Dindo grade II or above) 36% against 64%, major pulmonary complications 18% against 30%; three-year overall survival 67% against 55%, but survival was a secondary endpoint and cannot be used to say minimally invasive surgery makes you live longer[1]. The ROBOT trial (single Dutch centre, 112 patients): robot-assisted against open thoracotomy, overall surgery-related complications 59% against 80%, fewer pulmonary and cardiac complications, blood loss 400 against 568 millilitres, oncological outcomes comparable at a median follow-up of 40 months[6].

Note that ROBOT's control arm was open thoracotomy, not conventional thoracoscopy. To this day there is no randomised comparison of robotic against thoracoscopic minimally invasive surgery, and the sentence "robotic is better than thoracoscopic" has no evidence I can cite. One more piece of data that honesty requires: in the Japanese nationwide registry of 5,354 cases, the minimally invasive group actually had slightly more complications than the open group (44.3% against 40.8%)[7] — that is 2011 data, early in the learning curve, and its reminder is that the benefit of a technique only materialises through team experience.

Complications and mortality — the registry numbers, put here as they are

Oesophagectomy is major surgery across two body cavities, and its complication rate has to be looked at through real registry numbers, not waved away with "the technique is very good now". The benchmark data of the Esophagectomy Complications Consensus Group (ECCG), 2,704 patients from 24 high-volume centres in 14 countries: any complication 59%; pneumonia 14.6%, atrial fibrillation 14.5%, anastomotic leak 11.4%, chyle leak 4.7%, recurrent laryngeal nerve injury 4.2%; severe complications needing surgery or intensive care 17.2%; 30-day mortality 2.4%, 90-day mortality 4.5%[8]. The same system expanded to 39 centres and 6,022 patients from 2015 to 2018: 30- and 90-day mortality 2.0% and 4.5%, pneumonia down from 15.3% to 12.8%, but leak up from 11.7% to 13.1% — the leak rate has not fallen with technical progress, and leaks needing re-operation run at about 3%[9]. With three large registries side by side, 30-day mortality was 3.2%, 2.4% and 1.7%[10]; the Japanese nationwide 2011 data gave 30-day mortality 1.2% and operative mortality 3.4%[7].

"90-day mortality 4.5%" has to be read correctly: roughly one person in twenty dies within three months of the operation, and that is the high-volume-centre figure. The Japanese registry also found who is at higher risk: needing help with daily activities before surgery, smoking within the year before surgery, and losing more than 10% of body weight within six months carried odds ratios for 30-day death of 4.2, 2.6 and 2.4 — an odds ratio divides the ratio of "happened to did not happen" in one group by the same ratio in the other; it is not "the probability multiplied by that much", but the direction is clear[7]. Weight and stopping smoking are variables in surgical mortality, written into the registry data; what to do before treatment is in "Things to settle before treatment starts", and whether a tube should go in first when weight is falling is in "Can't swallow, weight falling: a feeding tube first?". For length of stay the only data are from three American centres after preoperative chemoradiotherapy: a mean of 9 to 13 days, varying with the radiotherapy technique[11]; for Taiwan's length of stay and mortality I could not find citable official data, and I am not going to infer them.

Do the lymph nodes need clearing up into the neck

Squamous cell carcinoma likes to travel to the neck nodes, hence the argument over "three-field dissection" (abdomen, chest and neck all cleared) against "two-field". A 2020 Chinese randomised trial (400 patients with mid-to-lower squamous cell carcinoma, no preoperative treatment): three-field retrieved a median of 37 nodes against 24, and 21.5% of the three-field group had metastases found in the neck; complications were comparable, with only re-intubation more frequent at 3.0% against 0[12]. The 2025 multicentre NST 1503 (829 patients) used an intra-operative frozen section of the right recurrent laryngeal nerve node to triage: those who were negative were randomised to two-field or three-field, with five-year overall survival 68.8% against 72.2% and disease-free survival 62.8% against 65.1%, no difference in either; those who were positive went straight to three-field, and their rate of neck metastasis was 28.9% against 8.3% in the negative group[13]. The conclusion is that when that node is negative, the neck dissection can be dropped without survival suffering — an example of the surgical world using a randomised trial to pull the extent of an operation back.

Hospital volume, the variable this operation is unusually sensitive to

I do not name institutions, but there is something very hard in the literature that has to be said. In US Medicare data from 1994 to 1999, the adjusted mortality gap for oesophagectomy between low- and high-volume hospitals exceeded 5 percentage points, a level matched among all the operations analysed only by pneumonectomy[14]; in the 2000 to 2009 follow-up, the odds ratio for death at very-low-volume against very-high-volume hospitals rose from 2.25 to 3.68 — in the modern era the relationship has not weakened, it has strengthened[15]. There is no citable public data on the volumes and outcomes of individual Taiwanese hospitals, so this can only become a question you are able to ask out loud — see the next section.

How to use those twenty minutes in the surgical clinic

Time in the surgical clinic is short, so write your questions down and take them with you; these questions are not a challenge, they are how you come to understand that operation. One: with my tumour where it is, will the anastomosis go in the chest or the neck, and why? Two: will it be open, thoracoscopic or robotic, and what is the team's experience with that approach? Three: roughly how many of these operations does your hospital do a year, and where do its leak rate and 90-day mortality fall — these are numbers a surgeon will have, and it is not rude to ask. Four: how far do my weight and my smoking need to be brought into line before surgery? With those four answered, go back to the numbers in "After chemoradiotherapy, do you still need surgery" and take both articles to the discussion together.

From the physician

Losing more than 10% of body weight and still smoking in the year before surgery are independent variables for surgical death in the registry data — the only two things you can change yourself before the operation. Before the discussion, write down four questions: anastomosis site, surgical approach, that hospital's volume and complication rates, and how far I need to be brought into shape before going under the knife.
