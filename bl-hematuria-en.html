DIAGNOSIS AND STAGING

Blood in the urine that does not hurt

One guideline holds two sentences at once: women wait on average twelve days longer from haematuria to diagnosis, and women under sixty should not be put in the high-risk box on age alone.

Bladder Cancer Guide · Diagnosis and staging · 12 references

There is no risk table here for you to tick your way through. What there is instead: the malignancy rates three validation studies measured for each risk stratum, what the guideline says the next step is in each one, and the sentence you can get said out loud in the room — which box am I in?

Painless blood in the urine is the thing I least want to hear a patient say "let's just see how it goes" about. Blood that hurts explains itself on the spot — a stone, an infection — and the pain drives you to a doctor. The painless kind, the kind that comes once and stops on its own, explains nothing, and it will not remind you a second time.

This article answers three things: of the people referred to urology because of blood in the urine, how many actually turn out to have cancer; why I do not use "leave it and it will become advanced" to persuade you; and which tests the first round consists of, including the one that is not for everybody.

Once you have been referred, it is about one in four

IDENTIFY was a prospective cohort across 26 countries and 110 hospitals that enrolled 10,896 people: aged sixteen and over, referred into secondary care with suspected urinary tract cancer, with no previous urological malignancy. After adjustment for age, type of haematuria, sex, smoking, hospital and country, the prevalence of urinary tract cancer was 28.2% (95% confidence interval 22.3–34.1) — bladder cancer 24.7% (19.1–30.2), upper tract urothelial carcinoma 1.14% (0.77–1.52), renal cancer 1.05% (0.80–1.29) and prostate cancer 1.75% (1.32–2.18)[1]. A confidence interval, in plain language: run the same study many times over and the answer lands in that range.

That denominator has to be stated before anything else. These are people who have already been referred into secondary care — not everyone who has ever had blood found in their urine, and nothing like the general population. In primary care the denominator is a completely different one.

In the same dataset, visible haematuria was the strongest single marker, adjusted odds ratio 3.47 (2.90–4.15)[1]. An odds ratio is the odds of having it against not having it in each group, not a multiple of probability. The American Urological Association and SUFU microhaematuria guideline quotes a set of absolute numbers that read more easily: in a prospective observational study of more than three and a half thousand people, urinary tract cancer was found in 10.0% overall — 13.2% of those with visible haematuria, 3.1% of those with microscopic haematuria only[2].

"Leave it and it will become advanced" is a sentence I don't use

Not because it is frightening, but because the shape of the data does not support it. In the SEER-Medicare study of 29,740 people, those whose diagnosis was delayed to nine months had an adjusted hazard ratio for bladder cancer death of 1.34 against those diagnosed within three months, and 1.29 after further adjustment for stage and grade; but the effect was strongest in the low-grade group (hazard ratio 2.11) and in the low-stage Ta/Tis group (2.02)[3]. A mechanism that genuinely worked by "left long enough, it grows into the muscle" would not be at its most visible in the group least able to do that. That is the shape of unmeasured confounding. The guideline's own wording goes no further than saying these delays "have been suggested to contribute to" a 34% increase in cancer-specific mortality[2], and I am not going to upgrade that into a causal sentence.

Another dataset, 1,384 newly diagnosed bladder cancers across several centres, gets misread in the same way: among those presenting with microscopic haematuria 11.6% were T2 or above, against 17.9% of those presenting with visible haematuria, multivariable odds ratio 1.69 (1.05–2.71, P=0.03); but there was no difference in grade between the two groups[4]. That is a difference in the stage distribution of two groups of people at one moment in time, not the consequence of one person waiting.

What holds up "get this settled this month" is two other pillars: a quarter of this group has bladder cancer to begin with[1], and settling the question is something one clinic visit can do. Neither pillar needs to frighten anybody.

The red square on the dipstick is not yet haematuria

The guideline's definition of microscopic haematuria is rigid: more than three red blood cells per high-power field on microscopy of a properly collected urine specimen. It also says in as many words that microhaematuria should not be defined by a positive dipstick alone — a positive dipstick is a reason to do the microscopy, nothing more[2]. The gap between the two has been measured: of 237 women with a positive dipstick, 20.3% had normal microscopy; of 54 people referred on the dipstick alone, only 26% had microscopy that was genuinely positive[2]. Being on an anticoagulant or an antiplatelet drug does not change any of this either: what the guideline says is that the same evaluation is done regardless of the drug and the dose[2].

The same microscopic haematuria, very different odds of finding something

Three validation studies have measured this closely (the figures below are as the guideline's own text reports them)[2]. In the study where all 1,018 people had cystoscopy plus imaging, 34 malignancies were found overall (3.3%); stratified retrospectively, that was 0 of 218 at low risk (0%), 14 of 447 at intermediate risk (3.1%) and 20 of 353 at high risk (5.7%). In the study where all 15,779 had cystoscopy: 3 of 727 at low risk (0.4%), 18 of 1,863 at intermediate risk (1.0%), 836 of 13,189 at high risk (6.3%). In the study of 1,730 women: 322 at low risk, 0%; 463 at intermediate risk, 0.2%; 945 at high risk, 1.3%.

So what the guideline recommends for the low or negligible risk box is a repeat urine test within six months, not a cystoscopy straight away; intermediate risk gets cystoscopy plus renal ultrasound; high risk gets cystoscopy plus axial upper tract imaging, with multiphasic CT urography the first choice where there is no contraindication[2].

I am not going to lay age, pack-years and the amount of blood out as a table you can tick and score yourself. That is a table for a doctor to work through with you, and the guideline itself concedes that the stratification is imperfect — it pools the risk of urothelial carcinoma with the risk of other urinary tract cancers, while using mainly bladder cancer risk factors to do it[2].

The other half belongs here too. Of the 34 malignancies found in that 1,018-person study, 32 (94%) were low-grade Ta. Looking harder has a price, and the guideline lists it — the discomfort and infection risk of cystoscopy, contrast reactions, the possibility of radiation-induced cancer, and false positives that set off further tests[2].

On women, the same guideline says two opposite things

A US claims dataset (2004 to 2010; 5,416 men and 2,233 women, all of whom first presented with haematuria and were later diagnosed with bladder cancer): from the first haematuria claim to diagnosis, women averaged 85.4 days and men 73.6 (P<0.001); 17.3% of women and 14.1% of men waited more than six months; the odds ratio for a woman being diagnosed with a urinary tract infection was 2.32 (2.07–2.59), and for undergoing abdominal or pelvic imaging 0.80 (0.71–0.89)[5]. In England's 2010 National Cancer Patient Experience Survey (41,299 patients, 24 cancers), the female-to-male odds ratio for having seen a GP three or more times before hospital referral was 2.31 (1.98–2.69) in bladder cancer — the strongest sex effect of the 24[6]. The SEER registry statistics the guideline draws on here also carry a row broken down by stage: five-year relative survival in localized disease is 62.0% in women against 71.8% in men (Cancer Statistics Review 1975–2016, Table 27.8; SEER 18; diagnoses 2009–2015)[7]. "Localized" is a registry box holding both T1 and T2; and the gap is still there inside a single stage stratum, so it is not an artefact of how stage is distributed.

The same guideline's 2025 amendment goes the other way. On the strength of three validation studies it widened the low-risk age band for women from under fifty to under sixty, made sixty and over intermediate risk, and states in as many words that women should not be categorised as high risk on age alone[2][8]. The reason is that in that 1,730-woman dataset, 11 of the 13 malignancies were in women aged sixty or over[2]. "Women get investigated later" and "women should not be investigated more simply because of their age" sit side by side in one document; I am not going to reconcile them.

The box with no blood in it is not an empty box

Carcinoma in situ (CIS) is the flat kind, the kind that does not grow into a lump. The European Association of Urology guideline is direct about it: CIS "may be suspected" in patients with lower urinary tract symptoms, particularly irritative voiding symptoms[9]. Frequency, urgency and burning, treated as cystitis over and over again, is the commonest shape this box takes.

NICE's referral thresholds can be read off word for word: aged 45 and over with unexplained visible haematuria without urinary tract infection, or with visible haematuria that persists or recurs after successful treatment of infection; or aged 60 and over with unexplained non-visible haematuria plus either dysuria or a raised white cell count on a blood test — those go down the suspected cancer referral pathway. There is a further box: aged 60 and over with recurrent or persistent unexplained urinary tract infection, consider a (non-urgent) referral[10]. The corresponding rule in the American guideline is that where haematuria has been attributed to a urinary tract infection, microscopy is repeated after treatment to confirm that the haematuria has gone[2]. Finishing the antibiotics, feeling better and never going back is the saddest miss I see.

What the first round is, and which part of it is not for everyone

The European guideline's three recommendations can be copied out directly. Initial assessment of haematuria uses "renal and bladder ultrasound and/or CT urography"; cystoscopy (with biopsy where needed) is done where there are symptoms suspicious of bladder cancer, and "it cannot be replaced by cytology or by any other non-invasive test"; cytology is an adjunct to cystoscopy, used to catch high-grade tumours[9].

What cytology can do has a clear edge to it: sensitivity 84% for high-grade/G3 tumours, and only 16% for low-grade/G1. A positive result means urothelial carcinoma may be sitting anywhere along the urinary tract, and a negative does not exclude it. The specimen needs to be at least 25 mL of fresh urine; first-morning urine is not suitable. Ultrasound, for its part, "cannot exclude upper tract urothelial carcinoma and cannot replace CT urography"[9].

One line gets repeated as though it were a general rule: "find a bladder tumour and you must have a CT urogram" is not what the guideline says. The text reads: once a bladder tumour has been detected, perform CT urography "in selected cases" (e.g. tumours located in the trigone, multiple or high-risk tumours); the reason given is that the incidence of upper tract tumours in patients with bladder tumours is only 1.8%, rising to 7.5% for trigone tumours[9]. The first round of tests for haematuria looks at the upper tract anyway; whether to look again once a bladder tumour is on the screen is conditional.

Four sentences to get said out loud at the next appointment

What can be looked up at the Taiwan end, first. The NHI fee schedule has 28019C, cystoscopy, 1,800 points; urine cytology goes under 15001C, cytological examination of body fluid, 480 points, whose item description includes urine[11]. The term "CT urography" returns zero hits in a full-text search of the fee schedule — I could not find a listed item, so how it is claimed and whether anything extra falls to you is a question for the medical affairs office or your case manager, and I am not going to infer it in either direction[11]. On the catastrophic illness certificate, bladder cancer falls under "other malignant neoplasms" within the heading "cancers requiring active or long-term treatment", and the certificate is valid for five years[12]. For the cancer detection rate in haematuria clinics in Taiwan I could not find an official statistic; every proportion above belongs to somebody else's population.

  • Is this visible haematuria or microscopic? If microscopic, was it confirmed on microscopy, or is it only the dipstick?

  • Which risk box am I in, and on which items? Does that box mean repeating the urine test, or a cystoscopy?

  • Is the upper tract being looked at this time round? If not, what is the reason?

  • If this round is all normal, what brings me back — blood again, passing urine that stays uncomfortable, an infection that clears and returns? Which of those counts?

What happens at the resection is in "The first resection decides everything after it"; how the words on the report are read is in "Ta, T1 and CIS on the report"; which question each scan answers is in "How staging is done, and what the scan can see".

From the physician

If this was microscopic haematuria and you were put in the low-risk box, what the guideline says is a repeat urine test within six months, not go home and forget it. Put the repeat date in a calendar while you are still in the room; that is more reliable than keeping it in your head. Blood again, passing urine that stays uncomfortable, or an infection that clears and comes back — any one of those three and you do not wait for that date.
