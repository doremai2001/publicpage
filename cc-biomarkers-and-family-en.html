AFTER THE DIAGNOSIS

Your gene report decides more than your treatment

Which fields on the report are heritable and which are not, why MMR is tested in everybody rather than only in people with a family history, and — once Lynch syndrome is confirmed — what age your relatives should start colonoscopy at.

Colon Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 13 references
#Biomarkers#Genetics & Family#Screening#NHI Coverage#Decisions

The NHI-covered RAS and BRAF tests are tied to the targeted drugs used in metastatic disease, once per person per lifetime, and are not run simply because you were diagnosed. The item that can change a whole family is MMR — it may point to a colonoscopy schedule that starts when your brothers and sisters are 25.

The last page of a pathology report is often a string of abbreviations: MMR, MSI, KRAS, NRAS, BRAF. Most people remember only that the doctor said it has something to do with which drugs come later, and turn the page.

Turning the page is a shame. There are two completely different things on it: one set concerns only you, and the other concerns your brothers and sisters, your children and your parents. Not telling them apart is the easiest way to lose the second set.

Half of this page is heritable and half of it is not

KRAS, NRAS and BRAF are tested on the tumour cells. Those variants were acquired by the cancer as it grew; they are not in your sperm or your eggs and they do not pass to your children. They are also common: across 12 real-world data sources and 4,431 tested tumour samples, the pooled prevalence of RAS mutation was 43.6% (95% confidence interval 38.8%–48.5%, meaning the true proportion plausibly falls somewhere in that range), and the spread between sources was considerable[1]. BRAF is much less frequent — 8.2% of primary tumours in a pooled set of 3,063 patients with metastatic colorectal cancer from four phase III trials[2]. What share of patients are mismatch repair deficient (dMMR) depends entirely on which group you are asking about, and I have left that number to "Immunotherapy is not an option for everyone", because the denominator is precisely what that article is about.

MMR is different. Loss of a mismatch repair protein, or microsatellite instability, found on the tumour is a signal. It may come from the tumour itself (acquired), or it may come from a variant you were born with — and that is the heritable one, Lynch syndrome. Telling those two apart requires germline testing on a blood sample. This fork in the road is what this article is about.

Whether these results change treatment is handled by two other articles: what dMMR means for treatment is in the one above, and what this box means specifically in stage II is in "Stage II: chemotherapy or not?". Here I am only dealing with the tests themselves and what they mean inside a family. When this page gets ordered and how long it takes to come back I have written up in "What the first month after diagnosis looks like".

Why everyone is tested, not only the people with a family history

Because picking people by family history misses them. In a pooled analysis of four large cohorts and 10,206 newly diagnosed colorectal cancer patients, 312 (3.1%) carried a pathogenic MMR gene variant. Comparing screening strategies: testing every patient's tumour for MMR had a sensitivity of 100% (95% confidence interval 99.3%–100%); selecting people by the Bethesda criteria dropped sensitivity to 87.8%; and the compromise of testing everyone under 70 and testing those over 70 only if they met the criteria came to 95.1%, doing a third fewer tests at the cost of missing 4.9% of carriers[3].

The earlier Ohio study makes it more concrete: among 1,066 unselected patients with colon adenocarcinoma, 208 had microsatellite instability and 23 of those were confirmed to carry a pathogenic germline MMR variant. Of those 23, ten were over 50 and five did not meet the Amsterdam criteria or the Bethesda guidelines[4]. Screen by "young" or by "somebody in the family had it" and these people are waved through. The original paper does not say whether those two groups overlap, so I am not adding them together — but either group on its own is enough to show why everyone should be tested.

The same study is a reminder of something else: microsatellite instability analysis alone and immunohistochemistry alone each missed 2 carriers[4]. So "the tumour's MMR report is normal" does not translate into "the family can relax". Where the family history is strong enough, germline testing is still worth discussing even when the tumour testing was unremarkable.

Find one patient and you find, on average, more than two relatives who had no idea

This is the number I most want you to keep. In the 21 proband families in that Ohio study, 117 at-risk relatives were tested; 52 of them carried a Lynch variant and 65 did not[4]. Confirming one patient opens the answer for more than two people, on average, who did not know — and the 65 who did not carry it got what they needed too: no more intensive surveillance.

Among the 42,828 participants at one medical centre in Taiwan's precision medicine programme, 89 carried a pathogenic MMR variant, a prevalence of about 1 in 481; cumulative cancer incidence in carriers was 40.9% for MLH1, 29.8% for MSH2 and 40% for MSH6, and the authors state directly that Lynch syndrome is markedly underdiagnosed in Taiwan[5]. This is a biobank population at a single medical centre, and it is not a national prevalence figure. The 30% to 40% needs reading properly as well: carrying a variant is not a sentence to cancer, it is a probability, and it is one that surveillance can push down.

What your relatives need is a colonoscopy, not a faecal test

Once Lynch syndrome is confirmed, the surveillance schedule for relatives looks nothing like the general population's, and there is a specific text to follow. The third edition of the Mallorca guidelines, from the European Hereditary Tumour Group and the European Society of Coloproctology, recommends that path_MLH1 and path_MSH2 carriers start colonoscopic surveillance at 25 and path_MSH6 and path_PMS2 carriers at 35; on intervals, every 2 to 3 years for MLH1, MSH2 and MSH6 carriers (every 2 years for those who have had colorectal cancer), and every 5 years may be considered for PMS2 carriers. Start age and interval do not differ by sex[6].

Why not simply as often as possible? A comparison across Germany (annual), the Netherlands (1 to 2 years) and Finland (2 to 3 years), covering 2,747 Lynch patients and 16,327 colonoscopies, found no significant difference between the three countries in cumulative colorectal cancer incidence or stage at diagnosis; 10-year cumulative incidence ranged from 4.1% to 18.4% depending on the risk group[7]. Nor have the societies converged: a 2025 review comparing guidelines across countries found that most put the interval at 1 to 2 years, while their recommendations on the age to start contradict one another[8]. So what your relatives should walk away with is not "you should get yourself checked too" — it is a plan with an age to start and an interval written on it.

Germline testing goes through informed consent, and what it touches is not only medical: insurance, marriage and childbearing, and who inside the family wants to know, all get moved around by it. So I am not going to write "get tested" as the self-evident next step. It is an option you can raise in clinic and decide on after a genetic counsellor has talked it through with you. What germline testing and genetic counselling cost under Taiwan's NHI, I could not find a provision I could cite, so that is one to confirm with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.

The publicly funded faecal test is for people who don't have this lead

Taiwan's bowel cancer screening policy widened from 1 January 2025: the subsidy now covers people aged 45 to 74, and people aged 40 to 44 with a family history (a parent, child or sibling diagnosed with bowel cancer), for one free faecal immunochemical test (FIT) every 2 years[9]. If your first-degree relatives do not have Lynch syndrome, this box is their starting point; the age band and interval follow whatever the Health Promotion Administration has announced.

The test works. In the cohort study of Taiwan's One Million Screening Programme, 1,160,895 people aged 50 to 69 took part in biennial screening, and colorectal cancer mortality among those screened was 62% lower than among those not screened; after adjustment for self-selection bias, coverage of 21.4% produced a 10% fall in mortality across the whole population[10].

But it misses things. In data from the same programme between 2004 and 2012, 4,018 of 15,386 colorectal cancers (26.2%) were interval cancers: 2,782 of them arose after a negative faecal test and 1,236 after a positive test where the colonoscopy that followed did not make the diagnosis. That second group had markedly higher incidence and mortality, and the higher a hospital's adenoma detection rate, the fewer post-colonoscopy interval cancers[11]. A negative faecal test is not a pass: if a change in bowel habit or blood in the stool appears between rounds, the thing to do is go back, not wait for the next round. Colonoscopy follow-up after your own diagnosis is a separate matter again — see "Is closer follow-up worth it?".

The NHI-covered RAS and BRAF tests are not done just because you were diagnosed

Almost every patient gets this one wrong. In the NHI fee schedule, items 30104B and 30106B, "All-RAS and BRAF mutation analysis", are 11,878 points each, with the indication restricted to "patients with metastatic colorectal cancer meeting section 9.27 Cetuximab and 9.53 Panitumumab of the drug reimbursement regulations"; the test must cover KRAS and NRAS exons 2, 3 and 4 and BRAF V600E, is limited to companion testing before those drugs are used, and is reimbursed once per person per lifetime. Item 30107B, "BRAF mutation analysis", is 3,006 points and, when used in metastatic patients, is restricted to those who are KRAS and NRAS wild type[12]. It is tied to the targeted therapy indications in metastatic disease; it is not a report everybody receives at diagnosis. When stage II and stage III patients ask me where their RAS result is, the answer is often that we have not reached the step where it is needed.

Next-generation sequencing also depends on the cancer type first. The NHI Administration's question-and-answer document states that the expert consensus for 7 major cancer types including colorectal cancer recommends single-gene testing, and that the relevant medical societies have been submitting applications for additions and revisions[13]. That document is the August 2024 version and may have changed since; what the NHI Administration announces takes precedence.

As for how MMR and MSI testing is reimbursed in Taiwan, I searched the item file of the fee schedule and could not find a dedicated item code. So I am not going to tell you it is covered, and I am not going to tell you it isn't — this is one to confirm with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.

When it's your turn to speak in clinic

Turn to the last page of the report and ask these three things:

  • Has my tumour had MMR or MSI testing? What did it show? If it hasn't been done, when will it be?

  • If the result is abnormal, should I have a blood test for germline testing? Can you refer me for genetic counselling?

  • If Lynch syndrome is confirmed, at what age should my siblings and my children start colonoscopy, and how often?

The third question is worth raising as soon as your own treatment is scheduled, rather than waiting until treatment is over. Surveillance for your relatives is a line that starts from this report of yours and runs for years; the earlier it starts, the less rushed it is.

From the physician

Variants in the tumour are not passed on and germline MMR variants are, and on the same page those two are easy to confuse. At your next appointment, ask one thing: has my tumour had MMR or MSI testing, and if it is abnormal, can you refer me for genetic counselling? Your relatives' colonoscopy schedule starts from there.
