AFTER THE DIAGNOSIS

Which lines of the report actually change treatment

Splitting what patients call "genetic testing" into three lines: proving you can have less chemotherapy, finding a target to aim at, and the inherited risk you were born with.

Breast Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 9 references
#Biomarkers#Genetics & Family#Decisions#NHI Coverage

Several boxes will not change your treatment today: variants of uncertain significance, a Ki-67 that lands in the middle ground, and a HER2-low grade that does not yet exist on the report at all. Put those lines down and there are far fewer questions left to ask.

"Genetic testing" is a code word in clinic, and different people use it to mean different things. What the person asking usually has in mind is one thing: run a test, see whether I can suffer a bit less, or get one more chance. Clinically it is not one thing. It is three, with entirely different purposes, using different specimens, applying to different people, answering different questions. Working out which of the three lines the report in your hand belongs to matters more than any number printed on it — because using the wrong report to make a decision is the most expensive misunderstanding I see in clinic.

Line one: this report is asking "does adding chemotherapy buy anything"

This kind of test measures how strongly a few dozen genes are expressed in the tumour that was removed, and uses that to estimate whether adjuvant chemotherapy would buy you anything extra. Its conclusion is often "you don't need it". The names that may appear on the report are Oncotype DX, MammaPrint, Breast Cancer Index, EndoPredict and Prosigna.

ASCO's 2022 biomarker guideline is rigid about who it applies to: these platforms may be used in postmenopausal patients or those over 50 with early ER-positive, HER2-negative disease that is node-negative or has one to three positive nodes; in premenopausal patients, clinicians may use one of the platforms only in those who are node-negative with ER-positive, HER2-negative disease; current data indicate that premenopausal patients with one to three positive nodes benefit from chemotherapy whatever the test result; and for patients with four or more positive nodes there are no data to support using this kind of test to guide adjuvant chemotherapy. The same guideline carries one sentence to hold on to: none of these tests is recommended for treatment decisions in HER2-positive or triple-negative breast cancer[1].

In other words, this line answers one particular question for one particular group of people. How its score is read, which trials stand behind it, and why age and ovarian function change what the same score means are all in "What this test is actually trying to prove". I am giving no numbers here.

Line two: this report is asking "is there a target to aim at"

This kind is tumour genomic testing, what people usually hear about as next-generation sequencing. What it looks for is whether the tumour carries an alteration that corresponds to some drug. Its conclusion is not "you can do less", it is "there is one more option" — and usually in the metastatic setting.

In the 2024 recommendations of the ESMO Precision Medicine Working Group, the cancers for which tumour next-generation sequencing is recommended in routine practice are advanced non-squamous non-small-cell lung cancer, prostate cancer, colorectal cancer, cholangiocarcinoma and ovarian cancer, and this 2024 version extended the recommendation to advanced breast cancer and a few rare tumours; the whole set of recommendations is written for advanced and metastatic disease, not for breast cancer that has just been diagnosed early[2]. (The colorectal cancer on that list has its own guide on this site; the logic there differs from breast cancer and I am not opening it here.)

Finding an alteration does not mean there is a drug, does not mean that drug can be obtained in Taiwan, and does not mean the NHI pays for it. Every target has its own preconditions attached to the drug that goes with it, and those are in "After metastasis, genomic testing is about finding a drug" — if you have just been diagnosed with early disease, that article is not about what you should be doing now.

Line three: this report is asking "was this inherited"

Germline testing uses blood, and what it looks for is a variant you were born with and may pass on to your family, not one the tumour grew. It changes two things at once: your surgical decision, and the age at which your relatives start being screened.

The threshold is wider than most people assume — the 2024 ASCO and Society of Surgical Oncology guideline no longer requires a family history before testing is offered[3]. Who should be offered it, when to have the conversation, and what to do about the family side are set out in full in "Family history: should you test for BRCA now?". Only one sentence about this line has to be said here: it is time-critical, it may change how much surgery you have, so it is discussed before the operation, not after treatment is over.

Where the three lines overlap is where the misunderstandings happen

Putting them side by side once: the report about avoiding chemotherapy will not tell you whether there is a drug to aim at; the report about finding a drug will not tell you whether you can be spared chemotherapy; and the inherited-risk report does not answer "will I recur". Have all three done and there will still be no report that tells you "you do not need treatment".

There is a misunderstanding running the other way too. Because line one's conclusion is often "no chemotherapy needed", many people equate "having genetic testing" with "being able to avoid chemotherapy". It holds only for that small group above, and there are two possible answers.

In Taiwan the three lines are reimbursed in completely different ways

Line three has an explicit provision in the NHI fee schedule, but what it covers is only the triple-negative box[4]. How many people sit in the gap between the guideline's threshold and the provision's threshold, and what to do about the difference, is what a whole section of "Family history: should you test for BRCA now?" works through, and I am not recalculating it here.

Where lines one and two stand in Taiwan between reimbursement and self-pay, and how to think about when it is worth paying for one yourself, is in "This test is self-pay. Is it worth it?". One thing first: do not decide whether to pay before you know whether the report would change your treatment.

The lines on the report that will not change your treatment today

This section may be more useful than the three above, because what it saves you is useless anxiety.

  • Variants of uncertain significance. The guideline states plainly that such results should not influence treatment management, and that people carrying one should be followed up for possible reclassification of the variant[3]. It is not bad news and it is not good news; it is "nobody knows yet".

  • Ki-67 when it lands in the middle ground. The International Ki67 Working Group supports using 5% or less, or 30% or more, to estimate prognosis only in the ER-positive, HER2-negative, T1-2, N0-1 group[5]; and when 30 laboratories stained the same batch of tumours, the median labelling index ranged from 0.65% to 33.0%[6]. The middle stretch cannot be taken away and used for a decision.

  • HER2-Low or HER2-Ultra-Low. The 2023 ASCO and College of American Pathologists update states in writing that establishing such new result categories now would be premature, and added only a reporting comment distinguishing immunohistochemistry 0 from 1+[7]. It is a category the treatment side uses, not a grade on the pathology report, and the two have to be kept apart.

  • Decisions about preoperative treatment. The 2021 ASCO guideline states that beyond histological type, grade, stage and ER/PR/HER2, the evidence is insufficient to support using other markers or gene expression profiles for clinical decision-making[8].

  • Long-term recurrence risk after year five. The EBCTCG analysis of 62,923 women shows that once tumour size and nodal status are given, PR status and HER2 status have no independent predictive value[9].

When a report arrives, first ask which question it is answering

At the next visit, before you push the report across the desk, ask these:

  • "Was this run on tumour or on blood? Is it answering chemotherapy, finding a drug, or inheritance?"

  • "With my combination of ER, PR and HER2 and my nodal status, which of the three lines apply to me now and which do not?"

  • "If this report came back the other way round, would your treatment advice change?"

The third is the one most worth asking in this whole article. Whether a test whose result would not change treatment either way is worth doing, worth waiting for, or worth paying for is usually clear the moment that question is answered.

From the physician

The three lines apply to different groups of people, so a test someone else has had is not necessarily yours to have. At the next visit, first ask "was this run on tumour or on blood, and which question is it answering", then ask "if the result came back the other way round, would your advice change" — the second one often settles whether to do it at all.
