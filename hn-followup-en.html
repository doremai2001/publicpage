Why the first year of follow-up is so crowded

Why year one is booked so tightly, when the post-treatment PET should be done, and why more scans do not make people without symptoms live longer.

Head & Neck Cancer Guide · After Treatment · 9 references
#Follow-up#Survivorship#Imaging#Recurrence#NHI Coverage

"I would feel better with a PET every year" is the request I turn down most often, and this page explains why. It also covers the three intervals people most often let drift: the thyroid, the dentist, and the carotid arteries.

Most people treat the last day of treatment as the finish line. In clinic I say something else: from today you get used to being watched, for years on end. The schedule prints as a handful of dates, but there is logic behind them — which stretch is tight, which loosens, which test genuinely looks for something, and which only buys reassurance while costing money and a dose.

Why year one looks so crowded

The American Head & Neck Society (AHNS) sets follow-up out like this: every 1 to 3 months in year one, every 2 to 6 months in year two, every 4 to 8 months in years three to five, then every 12 months — with no end date written in[1]. A 2024 review in Onco of follow-up methods and timing gives almost identical intervals: every 2 to 3 months for the first two years, every 3 to 6 months for years three to five, annually thereafter[2].

The front is crowded because recurrence is at the front. The same review puts local recurrence at about 40%, "most of which occur within the first 2 years after the initial diagnosis"[2]. Concentrating your leave, your travel and our clinic slots on the two years most likely to go wrong is that table's only design principle.

The timing of that one post-treatment scan is not arbitrary

The first imaging after radiotherapy is booked within 3 to 6 months of finishing — PET, CT or MRI, depending on primary site and treatment[1]. If it is a PET-CT, it should not be early: 8 to 12 weeks after therapy[2]. Freshly irradiated tissue is inflamed, and inflamed cells take up the tracer as tumour does. Scan too early and neither of us will believe the report.

That scan carries its weight because of a trial that changed practice. PET-NECK randomised 564 patients with advanced nodal disease to planned neck dissection or to PET-CT 12 weeks after chemoradiotherapy, operating only if the scan called for it. Two-year overall survival was 84.9% (95% CI 80.7–89.1) with surveillance and 81.5% (76.9–86.3) with planned surgery; hazard ratio for death 0.92 (0.65–1.32), meeting non-inferiority. Neck dissections numbered 54 in the surveillance arm against 221 in the surgery arm, saving about £1,492 per patient[3]. (The brackets give the statistical range the true value likely sits in.)

That conclusion has edges. PET-NECK enrolled chemoradiotherapy patients with advanced nodal disease, not everyone, and 54 in the surveillance arm still ended up in theatre. A negative PET reassures; it is not a discharge letter, only one point in a years-long schedule.

"An annual PET would put my mind at rest"

This is the self-funded item I am asked for most often, so, plainly: beyond that one baseline scan after treatment, more imaging has not been shown to make asymptomatic patients live longer. The 2024 review says so directly — in patients with no symptoms and no high-risk features, follow-up should be clinical and endoscopic, imaging reserved for the onset of symptoms[2]. What finds recurrence is the examining hand, the nasendoscope, and the symptoms you report.

Taiwan's rules point the same way. The National Health Insurance Administration (健保署, NHIA) covers PET in head and neck cancer (excluding brain tumours) for staging and for suspected recurrence or restaging; using PET as a cancer screening test is spelled out as self-pay. The same document records the dose: roughly 6 to 10 mSv per study, the equivalent of 100 to 160 chest X-rays[4]. A peace-of-mind PET is not free peace of mind; it is a bill and a dose. Scan when there is a clinical question; when there is not, you keep both.

The routine imaging that does stand up is chest CT, for patients 50 and over with more than 20 pack-years[1].

The thyroid: the one you cannot feel

NCCN's head and neck cancers guideline, version 1.2026, leaves no give: thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) every 6 to 12 months if the neck was irradiated and/or after partial thyroidectomy[5]. AHNS gives the same interval — if you have had radiation, every 6 to 12 months[1].

Why a blood test? Because radiation-induced hypothyroidism is mostly silent. Nobody wakes up thinking their thyroid has failed; they feel tired, cold and heavier, and put it down to a serious illness. This one is lifelong — it does not graduate at year five. Detail is in the piece on late effects.

Dental is settled. The carotid is where the guidelines lag the data.

Dental follow-up is lifelong, particularly for anyone with a dry mouth[1]. The reasoning is in the piece on dental work before radiotherapy; one line bears repeating: extracting a tooth from an irradiated mandible is the main trigger of osteoradionecrosis. That piece covers the weeks before radiotherapy, this one year five after — the dental column has no end date, deliberately.

The carotid is less tidy. NCCN goes no further than "consider ultrasound of the neck every 3 years if the neck was irradiated" (its own words are venous duplex ultrasound)[5]. But a cohort of 628 patients with a median follow-up of 4.8 years put the 10-year cumulative incidence of asymptomatic carotid stenosis at 29.6% (95% CI 23.9–35.5) and of stroke or transient ischaemic attack at 10.1% (7.0–13.9); the authors state outright that carotid screening is not routinely performed after radiotherapy[6].

Both are true at once: the cohort data show more risk than most people assume, and the guidelines have only reached "consider". I say that gap out loud, then put the weight on blood pressure, lipids and smoking — three things damaging the same artery, in your hands and your family doctor's, worth more than extra ultrasounds.

Quitting, and who owns which part

AHNS writes smoking and alcohol into the surveillance pathway itself: anyone still smoking or misusing alcohol gets a referral for help quitting[1]. Not a moral aside as the visit ends — a follow-up item, level with checking a TSH. The numbers are in the piece on getting back to normal life.

The division of labour: cancer surveillance belongs to the head and neck multidisciplinary team — radiation oncology, surgery and medical oncology each taking a stretch; the dentist runs lifelong oral follow-up; general health, immunisations, blood pressure, glucose and lipids pass to your family doctor, which NCCN places explicitly under a primary care physician[5]. The same guideline builds distress screening into follow-up, using the NCCN Distress Thermometer and Problem List[5]. So "my oncologist never checked my blood pressure" is not an oversight. It was always someone else's job.

Between visits, you are the only one watching

The symptoms you report between visits are the main way recurrence is found. Any of these is worth a call to bring the appointment forward, not waiting for the date on the card:

  • A new lump in the neck, or an existing one getting bigger.

  • A mouth ulcer, or a white or red patch, unhealed at two weeks.

  • New or worsening pain in ear, throat or jaw.

  • Hoarseness lasting more than two weeks.

  • Swallowing that has become difficult or painful; coughing on food or fluids; repeated chest infections.

  • Blood in saliva or sputum.

  • Unintentional weight loss, or new numbness of lip or chin.

Two things you can sort out before the next visit

First, the catastrophic illness certificate (重大傷病證明), legal basis Article 48 of the National Health Insurance Act. Holders pay no co-payment for the illness named on it, or for treatment the treating physician judges related to it; the same covers other treatment from that physician at the same catastrophic-illness visit, and other specialties during a related admission. If the certificate is approved only during an admission, the exemption backdates to day one of that admission — or to the emergency department visit, if you were admitted from the ED[7]. The list runs to 30 categories, published and periodically revised by the NHIA[8].

The second is for your family. People aged 30 and over who chew betel quid (including those who have quit) or smoke, and Indigenous people aged 18 and over who chew betel quid, get a government-funded oral mucosal examination every 2 years[9]. Your follow-up schedule is already booked. Theirs is not.

From the physician

These intervals are population averages, and your stage, treated volume and whether chemotherapy was given all change the shape of the schedule. At your next visit, ask for the thyroid, dental and carotid intervals and write all three on the same sheet of paper; what usually gets missed is the gap between them.
