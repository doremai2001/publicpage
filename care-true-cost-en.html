MONEY, PEOPLE AND TIME

The cost that isn't on the receipt

The carer, a family member's leave, the travel — what this illness really costs, and which of it can be applied for.

Patient Education · Money, People And Time · 19 references
#Cost & Insurance#Caregivers#Work & Travel

Who is looking after you at home? Most people take a moment to answer. This is the half of the bill that never gets printed, and the point of writing it down is to apply early rather than find out from the next bed.

At the end of a consultation I often ask: who is looking after you at home? Many take a moment to answer. The receipt has a column for medical fees and nothing else, yet over half of what this illness costs never gets printed — the family member taking leave, the person by the bed all day, the round trip every week. This piece lays them out, and says what can be applied for.

The costs that never reach the receipt are measured seriously in the literature

A US national caregiver survey estimated informal caregiving time in the two years after diagnosis, across nine cancers and non-Hodgkin lymphoma: an average of 8.3 hours a day, sustained for 13.7 months[1]. American data from twenty years ago: the figures cannot be moved to Taiwan, the idea can — caregiving is a job with hours in it, and nobody pays for them.

From Taiwan, a survey on the oncology ward of a medical centre in the south enrolled 137 patient–carer pairs. Seven in ten carers were women; of four factors associated with carer burden, one was having nobody to take over[2] — whether anyone can relieve you can be arranged.

I am laying this out so you can plan, not so you can weigh treatment against money. Whether to treat, and which treatment, is a medical decision and not a financial one; this piece has one purpose — that you know what can be applied for before it happens, instead of hearing it from the next bed three months later.

"Financial toxicity" has a scale, and it must not be read as "no money will kill you"

The scale: 147 items screened to 30, then finalised as the 11-item COST measure in 155 patients with metastatic cancer[3]; three years on the same team validated it in 233 patients with stage IV solid tumours, found it correlated with quality of life, and argued it is therefore a clinically relevant patient outcome[4]. How common it is cannot be pinned down: 30 COST studies pooled to a prevalence of 48%, but with heterogeneity of I² = 99.4% — far too much variation for 48% to be used as a number[5].

Taiwan has its own figures. A nationwide study of 400,229 working-age cancer patients aged 20 to 63 diagnosed 2007 to 2018 defined "severe financial hardship" as household net income falling below the poverty line after diagnosis: where that happened within a year of diagnosis, the adjusted hazard ratio for all-cause death was 1.64 (a hazard ratio compares how fast events occur in two groups; 1 means the same speed)[6]. A western Washington study linking the cancer registry to bankruptcy records gave 1.79 for those who filed, after matching[7].

Both are easy to write up as alarming; I will not. Both are observational, and neither excludes reverse causation — people who are more seriously ill are already more likely to lose income and life at once. The authors' word is "associated", not "caused". One proper use: financial hardship is worth handling as a clinical problem, not left as a private matter for you to sort out at home.

Carer costs: I could not find an official rate, because there isn't one

I have been through the Ministry of Health and Welfare, the NHI Administration, the long-term care pages and the health bureaus: no competent authority publishes a daily or hourly carer rate — a free-market price, not an approved one. So I will not print a figure; printing one would mislead you.

Three things instead. Before admission ask the hospital social work office (社工室) or the ward nursing station; most have a fee statement for the carers they use. Ask whether your hospital joins the integrated inpatient care service — an NHI Administration press release of August 2024 calls it, "for inpatients, alongside privately hired carers and care by family members, the hospital also provides auxiliary care staff"[8]; but funding changed in 2025 and the page now reads "from 2025 funded by the general government budget (114 年移由公務預算支應)"[9], so ask about beds and your share, not an old release. Third, the long-term care programme (長照) publishes respite and care payment schedules on the 1966 pages — a reference, but government prices, not the market.

The law gives a family member's leave less than most people assume

Article 20 of the Act of Gender Equality in Employment (性別平等工作法) allows family care leave where a family member has a serious illness or other major event needing personal care, and the days taken "count within personal leave, up to seven days a year", pay following the personal-leave rules[10]. Read both halves: not seven extra days, but days carved out of personal leave — and at most companies personal leave is unpaid. Remembering it as "seven days of paid care leave" only starts an argument with HR.

On the patient's side, Article 33 of the Labor Insurance Act (勞工保險條例) pays from the fourth day of incapacity for ordinary injury or illness where the insured is hospitalised for treatment, unable to work, receiving no original salary, and under treatment[11]. Those words are the crux: radiotherapy or outpatient chemotherapy where you report to the clinic daily usually will not meet this article. The occupational side is broader — benefit under Article 42 of the Labor Occupational Accident Insurance and Protection Act (勞工職業災害保險及保護法) runs up to two years[12] — but cancer is generally not an occupational disease. Working during treatment has its own piece; this is the money side.

The Catastrophic Illness Certificate waives co-payments, not self-pay charges

The misunderstanding I unpick most often. Article 48 of the National Health Insurance Act (全民健康保險法) exempts catastrophic illness status holders from the charges payable by the insured under Articles 43 and 47[13] — what is waived is the co-payment on outpatient, emergency and inpatient care. Not self-pay items, and not the difference on a private room.

The scope has edges. Article 6 of the Regulations on Exemption from Co-payment under the National Health Insurance (全民健康保險保險對象免自行負擔費用辦法) confines the exemption to three sub-paragraphs, all turning on relatedness to the catastrophic illness: the illness on the certificate or a condition the physician judges related; other treatment at the same visit by the same physician; and, in an admission, another specialty's treatment that must run alongside, or catastrophic-illness care the clinical situation requires[14]. A cold seen the same day in another clinic still carries co-payment. The same regulations make a diagnosis certificate valid for thirty days from issue, not accepted after that; too many let theirs expire in a drawer. Which Annex 1 item your cancer falls under, and how long your certificate runs, is for your case manager (個管師) or the NHI regional division.

Whether long-term care can help you turns on a threshold worth reading first

Long-term Care 2.0 has been succeeded by the Ten-Year Long-term Care Plan 3.0 (長期照顧十年計畫 3.0), approved by the Executive Yuan on 31 December 2025 and in force from 2026; two of its eight strategies are relevant here — stronger discharge planning with active long-term care reablement, and stronger family support with expanded inpatient care[15]. The system is mid-transition; details will change.

Now the part I must be straight about. Long-term care needs assessment by the county or city care management centre reaching care need level 2 or above, and the applicant must also be one of: aged 65 and over; indigenous and aged 55 and over; with dementia and aged 50 and over (extended from 2026 to those under 49); holding disability status; or, added from 2026, enrolled in the post-acute integrated care programme[16]. A 45-year-old on chemotherapy, too weak to walk far but without a disability certificate, will mostly not qualify, so I will not tell you that ringing 1966 gets you long-term care. What works: while still an inpatient, ask the hospital's discharge-planning team for long-term care to assess you, and ask whether the new post-acute programme applies.

If you qualify, respite care maps onto "the family is close to breaking": home, institutional and community forms, rates and procedures on the long-term care pages, reached through 1966, the care management centre, or the discharge-planning team[17]. The other route is disability status: Article 5 of the People with Disabilities Rights Protection Act (身心障礙者權益保障法) requires a team from medicine, social work, special education and vocational evaluation to assess before a certificate is issued[18]. Cancer does not automatically constitute a disability category; that depends on functional impairment. Where treatment has caused clear and lasting impairment, ask in clinic whether an assessment is worth applying for.

None of this has to wait for the day you cannot pay

First, at your next visit ask to talk to the social work office — not a last resort but the first step: accommodation, carers, grants and referrals all run through them.

Second, get the documents together. The HOPE Foundation for Cancer Care (癌症希望基金會) divides its resource grants into six categories — nutritional supplements, travel to treatment, accommodation and care staffing among them — all needing the same core documents — a cancer diagnosis certificate issued within the last three months, a household registration transcript, and income and property records[19]. Your attending physician issues the certificate, and it photocopies; that can be done today. The free line is 0809-010-580.

Third, have the "who can take over" conversation with whoever at home carries the most. Travel and accommodation grants have their own referral rules, in "Getting through treatment from out of town"; the policy side is in "When will insurance pay, and who decides?"; the self-pay form is in "How to ask about a self-pay item".

From the physician

Eligibility here turns on your age, your household and your functional status, so treat every threshold as something to check rather than assume. The one step worth taking now is a ten-minute conversation with the hospital social work office, with your diagnosis certificate in hand.
