IS LETTING GO EASIER

Palliative care is not another word for the last stop

The 2017 ASCO guideline's word is "concurrent". Every trial here compares palliative care added to oncology care against oncology care.

Situation Guides · Is Letting Go Easier · 11 references
#Palliative care#Decisions#How to ask

If you take one sentence to clinic, take this one: could the hospital's palliative team come and see me once? On the evidence, I have included three carefully built trials that missed their own primary endpoints, because that is closer to the truth than "research confirms" is. Whether to switch is not this article's decision.

Most people hear the words "palliative care" for the first time on the day they least wanted to hear them, and after that the words carry the meaning of that day for ever. This article is about a different possibility: in the overseas randomised trials, palliative care is something added alongside oncological treatment, and added early, soon after metastatic disease is first diagnosed. There is evidence for that. There is also a place where the evidence has been oversold. I will write both.

Every trial compares "with palliative care added" against "without". None pits it against treatment.

Every trial I cite compares palliative care added to oncological care against oncological care. Not one of them pits palliative care against treatment. That sentence has to come first, because most people assume the opposite. The 2017 American Society of Clinical Oncology guideline puts it the same way: patients with advanced cancer "should be offered dedicated palliative care services early in the disease course, concurrent with active treatment"[1]. "Concurrent" is the guideline's word.

The trial everyone quotes: four things that have to be read together

A randomised trial published in 2010 enrolled 151 patients with newly diagnosed metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer. One arm received care from a palliative care team early after diagnosis alongside their oncology care; the other received standard oncology care. The primary endpoint of that trial was quality of life at twelve weeks, and the early-integrated arm did better on it (FACT-L 98.0 versus 91.5, p=0.03), with fewer depressive symptoms as well (16% versus 38%, p=0.01). The investigators also observed that median survival in the early-integrated arm was 11.6 months against 8.9 months (p=0.02)[2].

Survival was not the question that trial was built to answer. And the work that came after it does not agree with itself. A meta-analysis of 43 randomised trials and 12,731 patients (enrolling life-limiting illness generally, not only cancer) detected no survival difference (hazard ratio 0.90, 95% confidence interval 0.69 to 1.17)[3]. A hazard ratio compares the rate at which deaths occur in two groups over the same period; 0.90 sits close to 1, and in plain terms this data cannot tell the two groups apart. The Cochrane version (literature search closing in October 2016) had survival data from only four trials and 800 people, and also detected no difference (hazard ratio 0.85, 0.56 to 1.28), with the authors grading their own certainty for that outcome as "very low"[4]. A different meta-analysis, restricted to outpatient specialty palliative care, did detect a difference: three high-quality trials, 646 patients, one-year survival 56% versus 42%[5]. Change what you enrol and you change the answer.

So the honest statement, and I will not go beyond it: early integrated palliative care improved quality of life, mood and symptom burden; whether it lets people live longer, the evidence contradicts itself and the question is unsettled. How large those three improvements are, and how much of them survives once you isolate the trials at lowest risk of bias, is in the next two sections — that is the complete answer. I will not use the survival finding to persuade anybody of anything, because it will not hold the weight.

Three carefully built trials, and all three missed their own primary endpoint

I think this belongs in the article, because it is closer to what this actually looks like than the words "research confirms" are. A cluster-randomised trial across 24 Canadian outpatient clinics, 461 patients, primary endpoint a spiritual and quality-of-life scale at three months: not significant (3.56, −0.27 to 7.40, p=0.07)[6]. A 2017 trial in 350 patients with newly diagnosed incurable lung and gastrointestinal cancers, primary endpoint the change in quality of life at twelve weeks: not met (p=0.339), with a difference appearing only at week 24 (p=0.010)[7]. A Danish trial across five oncology departments, 297 patients with metastatic cancer, primary endpoint the symptom each patient had nominated as most bothersome: no effect either (−4.9, −11.3 to +1.5, p=0.14), and the secondary endpoints, survival included, showed no difference[8].

The firmest ground is quality of life, and the effect is not large

Pooling the 43 trials, the standardised mean difference in quality of life at one to three months is 0.46 (0.08 to 0.83). (A standardised mean difference converts different scales onto one ruler; 0 means the two groups are the same, and the larger the number the wider the gap.) Keep only the five trials at lowest risk of bias and it shrinks to 0.20 (0.06 to 0.34), with symptom burden becoming non-significant[3]. Cochrane's pooled value is 0.27 (0.15 to 0.38), which converts to roughly 4.59 points on the FACT-G scale (2.55 to 6.46), certainty graded "low"; pooled depression showed no difference detected (−0.11, −0.26 to 0.03), certainty "very low"[4].

And something that is rarely mentioned: these trials barely looked for harms at all. Of the seven trials Cochrane included, only one reported possible adverse events (a higher proportion of worse scores for severe pain and poor appetite in that trial); the other six did not report them in the paper, and their authors, when asked, replied that none had been observed[4]. "Not reported" and "no harm" are not the same thing.

"Early palliative care cuts admissions and emergency visits" — in some trials, not others

The American ENABLE II trial enrolled 322 patients with advanced cancer. Quality of life and mood improved, symptom intensity was not significant (p=0.06), and days in hospital, days in intensive care and emergency visits showed no difference[9]. An Italian trial in 207 patients with pancreatic cancer compared early palliative care provided systematically against provided on demand: overall survival showed no difference; in the accompanying analysis, chemotherapy in the last thirty days of life ran at 18.7% versus 27.8% (adjusted p=0.036), and time spent in palliative care was longer[10]. The honest version is: some trials, not others.

How early is "early" in the trials, and how that differs from Taiwan's payment threshold

"Early" in these trials means within eight to twelve weeks of a new diagnosis of metastatic disease — those were people about to start treatment, not people about to stop. And the service they received was a specialty palliative care outpatient clinic, once a month.

The form of care under Taiwan's NHI that comes closest to "still an inpatient under treatment, with a palliative team joining in" is hospice shared care (安寧共同照護). Its programme document states its purpose as giving "terminally ill patients who are inpatients (other than in a hospice ward) or under treatment in the emergency department the opportunity to receive hospice care services", and enrolment is limited to terminally ill patients as the Hospice Palliative Care Act (安寧緩和醫療條例) defines them[11]. Those two things do not fully overlap: the international trials enrolled people just diagnosed with metastatic disease, while the Taiwanese payment threshold says "terminal". I could not find any Taiwanese official document setting out when a palliative referral should be made, so I do not write "Taiwan recommends referral at diagnosis". The reimbursement rule above was checked on 2026-08-30; for the criteria actually in force, ask your case manager (個管師), the medical social work office (社工室) or the hospital billing office (醫務課). How the three forms differ in practice and how to start one is in "Three kinds of hospice care: how they differ, how to start".

This does not have to be asked as "should I be switching to hospice now"

You do not have to ask "should I be switching to hospice now". That is not something this article can answer, and it is not something any article should decide for you. What you can ask is: "Given my situation, could the hospital's palliative team come and see me once? If so, who comes, when, and is there anything to sign first?" That is a question about how a service is arranged. It is not a question about whether to stop treatment. Why "no longer having a particular treatment" and "giving up" are not the same sentence is in "Giving up, and stopping one particular treatment".

From the physician

The most that can honestly be said is an improvement in quality of life, mood and symptom burden, and the effect is not large; whether it lets people live longer, the evidence contradicts itself and the question is unsettled. One gap to keep in mind: those trials enrolled people newly diagnosed with metastatic disease, while Taiwan's shared-care threshold says terminal.
