WHAT THE EVIDENCE SAYS FOR EACH TREATMENT

Can endoscopy alone deal with an early lesion

Whether it can be the end point is decided by the depth and vascular invasion of the tissue that comes out: a metastasis ladder of 0.4%, 8.7% and 36%, and the second primary in head and neck cancer patients.

Oesophageal Cancer Guide · What the Evidence Says for Each Treatment · 14 references
#Endoscopy#Screening#NHI Coverage#Evidence

In early oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma, ESD is both the treatment and the most accurate staging tool: only by removing the whole piece and sectioning it layer by layer do you learn which layer the tumour reached. This article lays out the lines drawn by the European and Japanese guidelines, JCOG0508, ESD's own perforation and stricture rates, and the fact that 14% of Taiwan's head and neck cancer patients have a synchronous oesophageal cancer.

"The gastroenterologist says it can be scraped off with the endoscope — so does that mean no operation and no chemoradiotherapy?" When I hear this, my first move is usually a question of my own: what does the report say about how deep it goes? Endoscopic resection of early oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma is one of the few treatments in this topic that involves neither opening the chest nor irradiating, and I would very much like more people to qualify for it. But whether it can be the end of the story is not decided at the moment of resection; it is decided by the pathology report on the piece of tissue that comes out. So below I start from "how deep", and go all the way to what may still need adding after the resection; the last section is for Taiwan's head and neck cancer patients, and it is something I bring up at every clinic visit.

Endoscopic resection is answering one question: how deep does it actually go

There are two kinds of endoscopic resection: endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) snares the lesion off in one or several pieces; endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) uses an electrosurgical knife to peel the whole lesion off along the submucosal layer in one piece. The European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy's 2022 guideline recommends ESD as the treatment of choice for most superficial oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma lesions; a meta-analysis of 22 studies showed that ESD has higher en bloc and complete resection rates and lower local recurrence, at the cost of taking longer and perforating more often, and that ESD's advantage only shows for lesions over 2 cm — under 1 cm, EMR is enough[1][2]. Why does "in one piece" matter so much? Because the whole specimen is sectioned layer by layer by the pathologist, who measures the deepest layer the tumour has reached — and that number is more accurate than any pre-treatment test. For the same reason the European guideline does not recommend routine endoscopic ultrasound, CT or PET before endoscopic resection[1]: for a superficial lesion, ESD itself is the most accurate staging tool.

Which layer it reaches changes the chance of metastasis by tens of times

From the inside out, the oesophageal wall is the mucosal epithelium (EP, called m1 in Europe), the lamina propria (LPM, m2), the muscularis mucosae (MM, m3), and then the submucosa, divided into the upper one-third (SM1) and deeper (SM2, SM3). The deeper the growth, the greater the chance of meeting lymphatics and blood vessels, so depth translates directly into the chance of lymph node metastasis. An Osaka series of 402 patients with squamous cell carcinoma followed long term after endoscopic resection (mean 50 months) measured out this ladder: five-year cumulative metastasis rate, EP/LPM 0.4%, MM 8.7%, SM1 7.7%, SM2 36.2%; among mucosal cancers, those with lymphovascular invasion had five-year metastasis of 46.7% against only 0.7% without; five-year overall survival EP/LPM 90.5%, MM 71.1%, submucosal 70.8%[3]. Two readings need care. The MM and SM1 numbers are close, so the sentence "once it reaches the submucosa it is necessarily more dangerous than the muscularis mucosae" cannot be written from this dataset; and the presence or absence of lymphovascular invasion pulls the numbers apart more than depth itself does. That is why the "vascular invasion" line next to "depth" on the pathology report matters just as much.

Which lines of the pathology report decide "done" versus "more to add"

The European guideline sets its curative threshold very concretely: en bloc, clear margins, depth no deeper than m2, well to moderately differentiated, no lymphovascular invasion, no ulceration — meet these and no further staging or treatment is generally recommended. One step down is "low risk", still counted as curative: superficial submucosal invasion (sm1), well to moderately differentiated, no lymphovascular invasion, and for oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma a lesion no larger than 20 mm. Below that is high risk: lymphovascular invasion, deeper than sm1, a positive vertical margin, or undifferentiated — here full staging is needed and additional treatment should be "strongly considered" in a multidisciplinary discussion[1]. The Japan Esophageal Society's 2022 guideline draws a more conservative line: pT1a-EP/LPM can be followed; pT1a-MM or pT1b-SM "should be considered for additional treatment (surgery or chemoradiotherapy)"; for MM with positive vascular invasion, or any SM lesion, there is evidence to recommend additional oesophagectomy or chemoradiotherapy, with a note that people with positive vascular invasion tend to do worse after chemoradiotherapy than after surgery, so surgery may be the more suitable additional option for this high-risk group[4]. The two guidelines use different terms (m3 is MM), but they draw the line in nearly the same place: the muscularis mucosae is the watershed. Once the cut reaches there, ESD is the first step towards a pathological answer, not the end point.

Not clean enough, or deeper than expected: add chemoradiotherapy — but that trial was single-arm

"More treatment needed" does not necessarily mean surgery. Japan's JCOG0508 was a prospective trial: 176 patients with thoracic squamous cell carcinoma clinically judged T1b (SM1 to SM2) without lymph node metastasis had endoscopic resection first and were then triaged on pathology — the 74 whose specimen was pT1a with negative margins and no lymphovascular invasion had no further treatment; the 87 with pT1b and negative margins, or pT1a with lymphovascular invasion, had prophylactic chemoradiotherapy of 41.4 Gy to the locoregional lymph nodes with 5-FU plus cisplatin; the 15 with a positive vertical margin had 50.4 Gy plus a 9 Gy boost. Three-year overall survival was 90.7% in the middle group and 92.6% overall, and the authors concluded it was comparable to surgery[5]. Let me give this trial its full label: it is single-arm, Japanese, three-year data, with no direct randomised comparison against surgery; the Japanese guideline also gives only a "weak evidence" grade to both surgery and chemoradiotherapy for T1b[4]. So the correct sentence is: endoscopic resection plus selective chemoradiotherapy is a road with prospective data behind it that keeps the oesophagus — not a road proven to replace surgery. Which road to take depends on your age, your heart and lung function and whether you could withstand an oesophagectomy — what that operation looks like is in "What surgery removes, and where the stomach goes".

ESD is not zero-risk: where perforation and stricture come from

ESD is not a zero-risk minor procedure. Data from 307 patients at 11 Japanese hospitals: en bloc resection 96.7%, complete resection 84.5%; perforation (including mediastinal emphysema) 5.2%, postoperative pneumonia 1.6%, stricture 7.1%, all healed with conservative management and no procedure-related deaths; an early era and low-volume institutions were independent risk factors for perforation[6]. The key to stricture is how much of the oesophageal circumference the lesion occupies: in a Tokyo analysis of 65 patients, the odds ratio for stricture when more than three-quarters of the circumference was involved was 44.2 — an odds ratio divides the ratio of "happened to did not happen" in one group by the same ratio in the other, and cannot be read directly as a multiple of probability, but a number this large leaves no doubt about the direction; lesions under three-quarters of the circumference produced no strictures[7]. The Japanese guideline accordingly writes: when the post-resection ulcer is expected to cover three-quarters of the circumference or more, stricture prevention should be considered, with oral prednisolone or local triamcinolone injection strongly recommended; for lesions of three-quarters or more but not the full circumference, the stricture rate without prevention is 50% to 80%, with oral steroids 8.6% to 23.1%, and with local injection 9.2% to 36.2%[4]. Before ESD it is worth asking one question: what fraction of the circumference does my lesion cover? How a stricture is dilated afterwards is in "Swallowing worse again: stricture or recurrence".

Oral and hypopharyngeal cancer patients: the oesophagus should be looked at while you are there

There is a group in Taiwan to whom this article is especially relevant: patients with cancer of the oral cavity, oropharynx or hypopharynx. The same stretch of mucosa has been soaking in tobacco, alcohol and betel quid for years, and the oesophagus and the head and neck are one carcinogenic field. Taiwan's data all point to the same thing: a 2024 publication on 717 newly diagnosed head and neck squamous cell carcinoma patients screened for oesophageal disease before treatment found 14.4% with a synchronous oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma, odds ratio 4.19 for alcohol drinking and 1.68 for a pharyngeal primary[8]; a prospective cohort of 100 patients found an odds ratio of 18.75 for alcohol and 2.53 for facial flushing after drinking, and five-year survival of 30% against 70% in those found to have a concurrent oesophageal cancer[9]; in the NHI database, among 68,131 head and neck cancer patients whose first oesophageal endoscopy was negative, the cumulative incidence of oesophageal cancer "growing later" was 1.4% at five years and 2.7% at ten, 3.3% at ten years for oropharyngeal and hypopharyngeal cancer, and still appearing beyond ten years[10]. The screening tools are dye and narrow-band imaging: in a meta-analysis of 16 studies, the sensitivities of white light, narrow-band imaging and Lugol's iodine staining were 53%, 87% and 88%, with specificities of 95% for narrow-band imaging and 63% for Lugol[11]; for patients who cannot open their mouths, a Taiwanese team used transnasal ultrathin endoscopy with sequential narrow-band imaging and Lugol screening, with an estimated false-negative rate for the sequential method of 1.2%[12]. In one Taiwanese hospital registry, head and neck cancer patients who had image-enhanced endoscopic screening with a negative result had five-year overall survival of 44%, against 39% in those not screened[13]. Lesions caught this early are exactly ESD's home ground. The alcohol-metabolism gene behind flushing after drinking is written up in "Squamous and adenocarcinoma are two different diseases"; the reverse question, whether oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma patients should have their throat looked at regularly, is in "How follow-up is scheduled, and the second cancer people forget". As for how often a head and neck cancer patient's oesophagus should be screened, I could not find a citable recommended interval; that is something for your ENT surgeon and gastroenterologist to schedule together.

What to sit down and discuss once the pathology report is back

Reimbursement first: the NHI fee schedule contains an item "endoscopic mucosal resection" (72050B), whose indications include early oesophageal cancer; but "submucosal dissection" appears nowhere in the full schedule — zero entries[14]. So I am not going to say ESD "is" or "is not" covered by the NHI — how ESD itself is priced, and whether the consumables are self-pay, is a question for your gastroenterologist and the medical affairs office. On the day the pathology report comes back after resection, ask about four lines: how deep did it reach? Is there lymphovascular invasion? Are the margins (lateral and vertical) clear? What is the differentiation grade? If all four are on the safe side, what follows is surveillance; if any one of them crosses the muscularis mucosae line, that is the moment to call gastroenterology, thoracic surgery and us in radiation oncology into one discussion — and that meeting is something you can ask for.

From the physician

On the day the pathology report is back, ask about four lines: depth, lymphovascular invasion, margins, differentiation. All four safe, and what follows is surveillance; any one crossing the muscularis mucosae, and you should ask for gastroenterology, thoracic surgery and radiation oncology to discuss it together. Head and neck cancer patients: if you have not yet had oesophageal endoscopic screening, that is the question for your next clinic visit.
