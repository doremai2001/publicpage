Dilators and sex: the question nobody raises first

The stenosis numbers have to be read in three layers; the dilator evidence is told as it is — including where it is only consensus. Timing, frequency, and what to do when you cannot keep going, all in one article.

And fewer than half keep it up for a full year — the pain, the lack of teaching, the embarrassment are all real, and every one of them has an adjustment that can be made.

My position first: definitive chemoradiation and brachytherapy are the treatments I deliver every day. So every sentence about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it — not more warmly.

This is not a question the patient should have to raise first; the medical side should speak first — so I will. In the education sheets handed out after radiotherapy, the dilator often gets one line; sex gets none. The result: two years later, at a follow-up visit, the stenosis has already made the pelvic examination painful — and only then does someone ask, for the first time, "have you been using the dilator?" This article covers the timing, the frequency, the evidence and the difficulties in one place — including where the evidence stands firm and where it is really only expert consensus.

Split the numbers into three layers first, or they will either terrify you or lull you

The data from EMBRACE, the international prospective study, have to be read in three layers. Mild vaginal change of grade 1 or above — narrowing, shortening, dryness — accumulates to 89% within two years, most of it appearing within six months of treatment[1]; stenosis of grade 2 or above, the kind that needs management, has a two-year actuarial incidence of 21%[2]; the most severe, grade 3, was 3% in a substudy that followed patients for more than four years[3]. The three sentences together: mild change is close to the norm, moderate is about one in five, severe is in single digits. The literature has reported stenosis rates anywhere from 1.2% to 88%, chiefly because assessment methods are not standardised[4] — so whenever you hear any single number, first ask which grade it was measuring.

The dilator evidence, reported as it stands

The universal advice is "everyone should use one"; the evidence actually runs behind the advice. The 2014 Cochrane review says it plainly: there is no reliable evidence to support routine dilation "during radiotherapy"; after radiotherapy, observational data associate regular dilation with lower self-reported stenosis, but causation cannot be established[5]. The largest dataset with the longest follow-up now available is EMBRACE-I's report published in 2025: 882 analysable patients, median follow-up five years; those who used dilators regularly or were regularly sexually active had grade 2 or higher stenosis of 23% against 37%, multivariable hazard ratio 0.63 — read 0.63 as: in the regular group, stenosis arrived distinctly more slowly[6]. The cost belongs in the same paragraph: regular users had more mild dryness and mild bleeding, with no increase at grade 2 or above[6]. This is still an observational association — the people who manage to keep it up may have been in better shape to begin with — but it is the largest body of data there currently is, and its direction agrees with the other studies.

There is exactly one randomised trial, from Brazil, and its result is honest: 195 women were divided into four groups — topical oestrogen, topical testosterone, lubricant, dilator — and after one year the average vaginal volume had shrunk by about a quarter in all four groups, with no difference between them; but graded by severity, the dilator group was the only one of the four without significant worsening[7]. An open-label design, uneven group sizes, only 29 women in the dilator arm — "there is a randomised trial" and "there is a high-quality randomised trial" are two different sentences, and both have to be written.

When to start, how often — this is consensus, not trial results

What the ESGO 2023 guideline writes is: after completion of chemoradiation and brachytherapy, patients should be educated in sexual rehabilitation measures, including vaginal dilators; topical oestrogen is indicated — evidence level IV, which is the expert-consensus level[8]. For a concrete protocol, only consensus can be cited: the UK International Clinical Guideline Group's version is to start about 4 weeks after radiotherapy ends, 2 to 3 times a week, 1 to 3 minutes each time, continuing for 9 to 12 months; the Brazilian consensus of 2019 is 5 to 10 minutes each time, 2 to 3 times a week, with no agreement reached on when to start or how long to continue[4]. The two consensuses differ from each other, so it is normal for hospitals' education sheets to differ too — go by your own treatment team. As for starting during radiotherapy itself, there is currently no evidence to support it[4][5].

From 42% down to 25%: the people who cannot keep it up are the majority, and most of the reasons can be dealt with

The prospective study at MSKCC in New York asked for 3 times a week for a full year (that was the institution's in-house protocol, not a universal standard): mean adherence over the 12 months was 42% — still 56% in the first quarter, down to 25% by the fourth[9] — keeping going is hard, and that is not a fault in you; it is what this task is like. The ways of making it easier are my clinic's experience, no citation attached: start with a small size, with plenty of water-based lubricant; tie it to something you already do every day, say the ten minutes after a shower; if it hurts, report it to the radiation oncology nurse and adjust the size and frequency — do not grit your teeth, and do not simply give up either. A little bleeding is a known mild side effect of regular use[6] and does not mean you did it wrong; but heavy or unstoppable bleeding is an emergency, and the threshold is in the master table of "Getting through five or six weeks of pelvic radiotherapy". On the Taiwan side: I could find no corresponding provision for dilators anywhere in the NHI's coverage — they are self-purchased devices, and hospitals differ in where they are bought and at what price; confirm with the radiation oncology nurse or your case manager. I could not find a reimbursement provision for vaginal oestrogen either — ask about it at the same time.

The return of sex: keep the numbers and the instructions apart

In the EMBRACE substudy — a comparatively mild group, with vaginal involvement no more than 5 millimetres — 47% were still not sexually active two years after treatment[10]. That number cannot be applied directly to everyone, but it says two things: recovery is slow, and it is not only you. What needs spelling out is that EMBRACE's definition of "regular" was dilator use "or" sexual activity[6] — that is a study's composite definition, not a doctor's order that "you should be having sex"; and conversely, anyone who already has a regular sex life does not owe an additional piece of homework on top of it. Hormones split into two layers: systemic replacement is written in "Early menopause: hormone therapy is not forbidden"; topical oestrogen is, in the guideline's word, indicated[8] — not some furtive folk remedy — and the formulation is a conversation for gynaecology or your clinic.

Whether it is allowed during treatment, whether a partner is affected — those questions live in another article

The general layer — whether sex is possible during treatment, changes in desire, the full argument for why "dryness and stenosis are not psychological problems" — is on the site in "Sex during treatment: who raises it?"; the changes already beginning in the vagina during treatment are in "Changes already under way during treatment". This article's only responsibility is the protocol specific to the period after pelvic radiotherapy ends.

Nine to twelve months of homework: how to see it through

Spread the UK consensus timeline across a calendar: start at week 4 after the end of treatment, 2 to 3 times a week, through to month 9 to 12[4] — which means that for nearly the whole of the coming twelve months, this is on the schedule. Set yourself three checkpoints: at week 4, confirm "has it started, and who teaches me"; at month 3, report the frequency and the difficulties at review, and have pain adjusted on the spot; at month 9, discuss with your doctor "can I stop, or one more quarter". The adherence data say most people have already stopped before the fourth quarter[9] — not because it cannot be done, but because nobody asked about it again. Put those three checkpoints into your phone's reminders, so that it keeps being asked.

From the physician

Hospitals' dilator protocols genuinely differ — your own team's version is the one that counts. Today's homework is a single item: confirm "who teaches me, and when do we start". If it hurts, do not grit your teeth; report it to the radiation oncology nurse and adjust the size and frequency — this can be adjusted, and giving up does not have to be the ending.
