CHOOSING THE TECHNIQUE

TOMO and ordinary IMRT: where the difference is

The dosimetric difference for a complex field is real; in the Taiwanese data the mean heart dose with VMAT is in fact lower than with TOMO — technique is chosen by looking at your treatment field, not at the price list.

Breast Radiotherapy Guide · Choosing the Technique · 11 references
#TomoTherapy#IMRT#Costs & Self-Pay#NHI Coverage

There is no randomised comparison of clinical outcomes between TOMO and ordinary IMRT; everything that can be compared is dose distribution. This article lays out the situations where rotational modulation comes out ahead, the Taiwanese evidence in which the wins between it and VMAT go both ways, and the price paid in the low-dose bath. On self-paid amounts I could find no announcement, so I write it as I found it.

The way TOMO comes up in clinic is usually very direct: "I hear the self-paid one is more expensive — does that mean it is better?" The question is already pointed the wrong way. The difference between TOMO and ordinary intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) is not on the price list; it is in what your treatment field looks like. If your field is simple, the dose distributions the various techniques produce differ little. If your field is complex, rotational modulation does have things it can do that the others cannot. This article separates "where the difference is meaningful" from "where the difference is small", so that you can tell which box you are in.

My position first: every technique compared in this topic — ordinary intensity-modulated radiotherapy, TOMO, deep inspiration breath-hold — has a self-paid component among the more expensive options, and all of them are delivered in my own department; protons mean a referral elsewhere. So every "better" here comes with its primary source and the level of evidence behind it, and on cost I cite the official schedule where one exists and tell you to ask the medical affairs office where it does not. Concluding that the basic option is enough for you is a completely reasonable conclusion.

First, what TOMO is and what it is not

Helical tomotherapy (TOMO) is a rotational member of the intensity-modulated family: the treatment head turns around you and treats as it turns, which puts it in the same rotational category as volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT); what is usually meant by IMRT delivers from a handful of fixed angles. How the whole family of techniques fits together, and why deep inspiration breath-hold is a posture rather than a machine, is the skeleton in "The machine, and the breathing"; here I only write about where the difference lies. The sentence to set down first is this: technique choice is decided by the indication — by how complex the field is, not by the budget.

When the field is complex, rotational modulation has things it can do

Everything below is a dosimetric conclusion — a comparison of planned dose distributions, not of clinical outcomes. I take the three most-cited scenarios separately, because they do not carry the same weight for you.

The first is chest wall plus regional nodes, internal mammary chain included. A three-way comparison in 30 patients having post-mastectomy radiotherapy showed that with a target this complex, helical TOMO and VMAT gave better target coverage and organ-at-risk sparing overall than the tomotherapy unit's own fixed-field mode (TomoDirect) — and note that this one compares two ways of delivering on the tomotherapy unit, not TOMO against IMRT on an ordinary linear accelerator[1]. In another planning study of left-sided post-mastectomy treatment including the internal mammary nodes (26 patients), TOMO with a directional block gave a mean heart dose of 4.79 Gy against 6.39 Gy for fixed-field IMRT, with more complete coverage of the internal mammary chain as well[2] — that gap appears only once that particular directional blocking scheme is added; it does not appear automatically when you change machine.

The second is the reconstructed chest wall. A prospective analysis of 120 patients having hypofractionated radiotherapy after immediate reconstruction found that 70% of plans met the optimal standard for chest wall and nodal coverage and organ sparing at the same time, with acute toxicity reaching grade 2 at most (36.7%) — single-arm feasibility data, with no comparison group[3].

The third is synchronous bilateral treatment, which I meet very rarely in my own clinic. In a planning comparison of four techniques, TOMO gave the lowest mean heart dose (3.53 Gy, against 5.6 for VMAT, 3.80 for fixed-field IMRT and 4.84 Gy for field-in-field), at the cost of the longest treatment time (11.0 minutes, against 3.9 minutes for VMAT)[4].

With the three laid side by side, I want to hold down a misreading that happens very easily: this section cannot be read as "TOMO is better for the heart". The heart dose gap in the 26-patient study holds only once that specific directional blocking scheme is added; synchronous bilateral treatment is a rare situation; and in the box closest to the ordinary patient — left-sided breast cancer plus regional nodes — the Taiwanese data in 108 patients point the other way, and they are in the very next section. The only conclusion this section will carry is one sentence: rotational modulation has a role for complex targets.

There is evidence pointing the other way too — and it is Taiwanese

TOMO is not better across the board. Its built-in cost is written up in the literature: helical delivery gives conformal dose and effective organ sparing for complex targets, "but increases the low-dose bath to non-target volume" — low dose over a large volume, with the low-dose regions in the contralateral breast and lung potentially larger than with fixed-field techniques[5]. Taiwanese single-centre data measured something else: a dosimetric comparison in 108 patients treated for left-sided breast cancer with regional nodal irradiation (2017 to 2020) found VMAT gave a lower mean heart dose than TOMO (3.82 against 5.13 Gy, p<0.001 — the smaller the p value, the less the difference looks like a coincidence of sampling), with lower mean doses to the lung, the contralateral breast and the oesophagus as well, and the subgroup that included the internal mammary nodes went in the same direction; without the internal mammary nodes, the two were similar for heart, lung and contralateral breast[6]. So the correct statement is this: rotational modulation (TOMO or VMAT) is better for complex targets, and between TOMO and VMAT the wins go both ways, depending on the situation and on the planning. One more thing has to be said honestly: the results of planning comparisons depend heavily on each centre's planning goals and planning skill — that same blocking-strategy study shows that changing how TOMO is blocked changes the heart dose a great deal[2]. The same machine can produce very different dose distributions in different hospitals; what you want to be asking about is "my plan", not "that machine".

Change the label to clinical outcome and the randomised box is empty

Change the label from dosimetric to clinical outcome and the box empties out: there is no randomised clinical comparison of TOMO against ordinary IMRT — I could find no completed trial. The only randomised trial containing TOMO is TomoBreast, single-centre in Belgium (121 to 123 per arm), but what it compares is "hypofractionated image-guided TOMO" against "conventionally fractionated conventional technique" — the fractionation schedule is tied to the machine in the comparison and the two cannot be pulled apart. The primary quality-of-life analysis found no significant difference between the arms in any domain after statistical adjustment[7]; the composite lung and cardiac function endpoint at 12 years favoured the TOMO arm, and the authors themselves position it as proof-of-concept, stressing that the performance of hypofractionation is conditional on the use of advanced technique[8]. A Chinese retrospective comparison (315 patients, followed for only 3 months) points both ways: less radiation pneumonitis in the TOMO group (0% against 4.3%), and more grade 3 to 4 skin toxicity (16.2% against 7.6%) — retrospective, short-term, take it as a reference only[9]. A French long-term single-arm series over 9.1 years (179 patients with difficult anatomy, median mean heart dose 7.04 Gy) had cardiovascular events in 4.5%, all of them in people with pre-existing risk factors or obesity[10] — single-arm data cannot hold up a comparative conclusion. The full argument on heart dose and cardiac events is in "Heart dose: the core question on the left side", and I am not reopening it here.

The basic box is real, and so is the gap in the complex box

This has to be written as one paragraph, because both sentences are true at the same time. For a simple field such as whole breast alone, the heart and lung doses the various techniques produce differ little, and the heart-sparing of ordinary intensity-modulated radiotherapy does real work — the modern left-breast mean heart doses and the figures for respiratory control I have laid out in "Heart dose: the core question on the left side", and those results come from 3D conformal and ordinary intensity-modulated technique — the class of technique the NHI pays for. The other way round, the dosimetric difference for a complex field — nodes to be treated, internal mammary chain included, reconstructed chest wall, both sides at once — is real too; it is measurable, it is in the literature, and it must not be shrunk down to "they are all the same". So the conclusion has only one possible shape: whether your field is complex decides whether rotational modulation has a role — and that is a discussion about the indication, not about the budget.

On the money, I write as far as I could check and no further

The full NHI fee schedule table (the version effective 1 May 2025, 6,013 items) has no treatment item specific to TOMO: helical tomotherapy has no code of its own on the NHI side, the design of intensity-modulated technique is already inside the wording of the provision for "computerised treatment planning — complex" (36015B), and the treatment itself is claimed as linear accelerator field items[11]. So do individual hospitals set up self-paid items of their own, under what name and at what amount? I could find no official announcement from any hospital, and none nationally either — prices circulating in the media I do not cite. No dedicated NHI code does not mean there must be a self-paid difference, and it does not mean there is not one; this box can only be answered by your own hospital, so ask the medical affairs office to confirm the item name and the amount. What this article can give you is which situations give you dosimetric grounds to ask; on the money, take this article to the medical affairs office.

Rather than asking about the machine, get the field pinned down first

Back to the question at the top. I would ask you to settle two things, and not in the other order. The first is the field: ask your treatment team whether your treated volume includes the regional nodes, the internal mammary chain, the reconstructed chest wall, and then ask them to tell you what heart, lung and contralateral breast doses the plan for that field actually comes out at — those numbers exist the moment the plan is finished, and you can ask for them. Money is the second thing: if there is a self-paid difference, ask the medical affairs office to put the item name and the amount in writing for you. If your field is simple, hearing "the basic option is enough" is an answer with literature behind it; if your field is complex, the dosimetric advantage of rotational modulation has literature behind it too. Both answers stand. The only thing that does not stand is letting the price decide for you.

From the physician

At your appointment ask first, "which areas does my treatment field include?", then ask the team to tell you what heart, lung and contralateral breast doses that field plans out to. If there is a self-paid difference, ask the medical affairs office to put the item name and the amount in writing, and make the decision once you have the numbers in your hand.
