HYPERTHERMIA: THE EVIDENCE, SITE BY SITE

Breast: the best evidence hyperthermia has

Five randomised trials, one that waited thirty years to be published, and a meta-analysis. They point the same way — and every endpoint is whether the tumour shrank.

Next-Generation Therapy Guide · Hyperthermia: the evidence, site by site · 6 references
#Hyperthermia#Evidence

One trial here opened to recruitment in 1987 and was not fully published until 2024. Those thirty years are not an administrative delay. They are a description of where this field stands.

Warning 1 (what this displaces): Most of the treatments in this topic are paid out of pocket, and most sit outside standard treatment. Before you decide, you need to know that what they displace is usually not time but three things of real value: standard treatment you have not yet used up, money you will need for later lines, and your eligibility for clinical trials (many trials exclude people who have recently received other experimental treatments). And there is one more, harder to see — physical reserve. The strength burnt through in a course that does not work may no longer be there when a genuinely useful option appears. So the question is not whether this is worth trying — it is what you give up by doing it.

The situation this article deals with usually arises after a recurrence. By then some of the money and some of the physical reserve have already gone, and whether anything is left for later lines is a real question.

Warning 2 (finding the right doctor): Do not make this kind of decision alone, and do not make it on the word of the person offering the treatment. The doctor you are looking for is one willing to tell you "this is not right for you" — if they never once say who this is NOT for, that is not a consultation, it is a sale.

My position first: proton therapy and hyperthermia are both technologies within my own field, and the hospital I work at has the equipment for them. So I draw every line in this topic tighter, not looser.

Let me fence off the scope first. The evidence for hyperthermia in breast cancer sits in two situations: locally advanced tumours that cannot be operated on, and locoregional recurrence in the chest wall — particularly the group who have already had radiotherapy once and need reirradiation. Someone newly diagnosed, operable, walking the standard pathway, was not in the recruitment of any of these trials.

Three layers of comparison, spread across nearly forty years

Three layers. First, five randomised trials, each opened between 1988 and 1991; each recruited too slowly, so in the end they were pooled and published together. 306 patients randomised, primary endpoint local complete response — 41% with radiotherapy alone, 59% with hyperthermia added, stratified odds ratio 2.3[1].

Second, the ESHO 1-85 trial, run by the European Society for Hyperthermic Oncology. It opened to recruitment in February 1987 and closed in November 1993: 151 patients, 155 tumours, radiotherapy of 65 to 70 Gy, the heated arm once a week aiming at 43 degrees for 60 minutes, primary endpoint a persistent complete response inside the treated area[2]. What is remarkable is this: it is one of the largest studies in the whole hyperthermia field, and it was not fully published until 2024. A trial that saw daylight thirty years after it finished is itself a statement about where this field sits.

Third, a systematic review and meta-analysis of locoregionally recurrent breast cancer: 34 studies, of which eight were two-arm (five of those randomised), 627 patients in the two-arm part, plus another 1,483 from single-arm studies. In the two-arm data the complete response rate was 60.2% with heating against 38.1% with radiotherapy alone, odds ratio 2.64. The radiotherapy dose these patients received averaged only 38.2 Gy — because most of them had been irradiated once already[3].

All of these patients were recruited around 1990

The dates are the point. The five randomised trials recruited between 1988 and 1991, the ESHO trial through to 1993. The studies in the meta-analysis span 1981 to 2015. Which means most of the data comes from an era when both the systemic and the local treatment of breast cancer looked different from today.

40% to 60% is the share of tumours that vanished, not the share of people alive

Read this part slowly. Every number above is a complete response rate — the proportion in whom no tumour could be seen after treatment. It is not a survival rate. For someone with extensive ulcerating recurrence across the chest wall, local control is a serious goal in its own right: the wound, the discharge, the pain, the smell are real suffering. But reading “complete response went from 40% to 60%” as “people lived longer” is the misreading this article most needs to prevent.

And an odds ratio of 2.64 does not mean it works more than twice as well. Look at the absolute numbers: 38% becomes 60%, which is roughly one more person in every five reaching a complete response.

Whether enough heat was delivered shows up in the results

The systematic review in recurrent breast cancer found that the temperature and thermal dose during treatment were related both to clinical outcome and to heat-related skin toxicity[4]. More recently, data from reirradiation combined with hyperthermia has been used to model the probability of locoregional control[5]. Put the two together and the conclusion is: this treatment has a dose-effect relationship, and the effect and the side effects come out of the same thing. You cannot have only the first.

And here is what the ESHO trial recorded about its own delivery: among the patients who were heated, 84% reached the target temperature on at least one occasion, but the variation in temperature was large; tolerance was acceptable, but 15% of treatments were accompanied by moderate to severe pain and discomfort[2]. The positive results above were produced at that standard of delivery.

Why breast cancer, of all sites, got studied the most

There is a very practical reason: chest wall lesions are shallow. A superficial lesion can be handled with microwave-type heating devices, the energy acting within a few centimetres under the skin, and temperature probes are easy to place on the tumour surface[6]. Deep tumours in the pelvis or the abdomen are much harder to heat and much harder to measure. So the distribution of hyperthermia's evidence reflects, to some degree, where it is easier to do rather than only where it works better. That holds when you read the articles that follow, too.

Which gives you an inference you can use. If your lesion is superficial and sits where a heating device can be applied to it, the delivery conditions of those trials are closer to your situation; if it is deep, those numbers transfer less well.

Those control arms were the radiotherapy of about 1990

A great deal has changed. The control arms of those trials were the radiotherapy of around 1990. Since then breast cancer has gained long-term endocrine therapy, targeted therapy, antibody-drug conjugates and entirely different systemic strategies; radiotherapy itself has moved from two-dimensional planning to intensity-modulated and image-guided delivery. So “it beat the radiotherapy of its day” cannot be read straight across as “it would add as much on top of modern treatment today”. No new large randomised trial has answered that question.

Chest wall recurrence is where it belongs; a new diagnosis is not

The reasonable situation is specific: locoregional recurrence in the chest wall, already irradiated once, not suitable for or not willing to have further surgery, and where local control is itself the goal of treatment — managing the wound and the pain, for instance. This is one of the most defensible positions hyperthermia holds anywhere in oncology, and the reason is that the radiation dose available has been capped by the previous course, so something else has to make that dose count for more.

The unreasonable situation is just as specific: newly diagnosed, operable breast cancer on the standard pathway; or the thought that “adding it can't hurt” laid on top of treatment that is already complete. As I said above, what this treatment costs is time, money and physical reserve, and its evidence does not support that use.

Before the conversation, align the expectations: what is on offer here is the wound, the discharge, the pain and the smell, not length of life. Confirm that sentence with your treating team, and then talk about the number of sessions and the cost — only then are the two of you discussing the same thing.

One last note on scope. Where in Taiwan this can be done and what it costs is in the nineteenth article, and I will not put a figure in there until the checking against official sources is finished. This article deals only with the evidence.

From the physician

If you have just been diagnosed and are about to start standard treatment for breast cancer, none of this article applies to you. It is about chest wall recurrence, in someone already irradiated once, who is not suitable for further surgery. Confirm that this is your situation before you take the conversation any further.
