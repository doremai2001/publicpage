HYPERTHERMIA: CLOSING

Taiwan: the list I could not write

I could not find an official compilation of machine distribution, and I could not find a reimbursement provision I could cite. So this article gives no list — it gives you a way of checking for yourself.

Next-Generation Therapy Guide · Hyperthermia: closing · 6 references
#Hyperthermia#Costs & Self-Pay#Decisions

By the last article of a set like this I ought to have a list in my hand. I do not have one. So what this article gives you instead is six questions and a phone call — the part you can get the answer to yourself, and the part that will not go out of date.

Warning 1 (what this displaces): Most of the treatments in this topic are paid out of pocket, and most sit outside standard treatment. Before you decide, you need to know that what they displace is usually not time but three things of real value: standard treatment you have not yet used up, money you will need for later lines, and your eligibility for clinical trials (many trials exclude people who have recently received other experimental treatments). And there is one more, harder to see — physical reserve. The strength burnt through in a course that does not work may no longer be there when a genuinely useful option appears. So the question is not whether this is worth trying — it is what you give up by doing it.

What this article asks you to spend is time: a few more questions, one more document in writing. It is the lowest-cost, highest-return stretch in the whole topic.

Warning 2 (finding the right doctor): Do not make this kind of decision alone, and do not make it on the word of the person offering the treatment. The doctor you are looking for is one willing to tell you "this is not right for you" — if they never once say who this is NOT for, that is not a consultation, it is a sale.

My position first: proton therapy and hyperthermia are both technologies within my own field, and the hospital I work at has the equipment for them. So I draw every line in this topic tighter, not looser.

When I set out to write this article I intended to put together a picture of hyperthermia in Taiwan: which classes of machine, how they are distributed, whether Taiwan's National Health Insurance (NHI) covers it, roughly what it costs. After checking, I changed how I was going to write it, and the reason needs stating first.

What I could not find out, I do not write

First, there is no official compilation of the distribution of machines. The numbers I have come from lectures and internal material, not from an official source I can cite publicly. The rule in this topic is that what cannot be found is not written, so this article carries no list.

Second, for NHI reimbursement status I could not find a formal provision I could cite. I am not going to infer from that that it is not covered, and I am not going to infer that it is — those are two guesses that would both mislead you. The right thing to do is to confirm with the medical affairs office or the case manager at the hospital where you will be treated, and to ask for it in writing.

Third, I do not write costs at all. Hospitals differ in what they itemise and how they price it, and the total for hyperthermia is decided by the number of sessions, so a single-session figure is of no use as a reference.

Those three things look like evasion, but they are in fact exactly what the first article in this topic teaches: do not treat what you cannot find out as something you know.

But there is one thing I do want to warn you about, and it matters

The efficacy numbers you read online may come from a class of machine that is not the same one as the one on offer near you. I could not find an official compilation of the distribution of machine types in Taiwan, so I cannot tell you which class is more common — but I can tell you how to find out which class the machine in front of you belongs to, and that is the part that is of use to you. The eighth article is about this from start to finish, and if you only read this article, please at least read that one too — it is the crossing point in this whole set where the misreading is easiest.

So the first question to ask about hyperthermia in Taiwan is always: which class of machine are you going to treat me with? Not the brand — what its claimed mechanism of action is, and whether the temperature is measured.

Incidentally, that question applies just as well when you are already having hyperthermia. Finding out halfway through a course that the machine is not the type you thought it was is something you are allowed to ask about — and the sooner the better, because it changes how you read the meaning of every remaining session.

Then ask these six things

  • Can this machine reach the depth of my tumour? Ask them for the figure in centimetres. Different heating technologies differ enormously in the depth they penetrate[1].

  • During treatment, is the temperature measured, at how many points, and where are they placed? Without thermometry there is no way of checking what this session achieved, which the ninth article went through.

  • Which quality assurance document does your procedure follow? This field has consensus documents for superficial[2] and for deep[3] hyperthermia.

  • How many sessions in the whole course, and how long is each one? The common arrangement in published trials is once or twice a week, 40 to 60 minutes each time[4][5]. The number of sessions decides the total cost, and it decides how many days off work you need.

  • Who is responsible for controlling the interval between hyperthermia and radiotherapy or chemotherapy? Trials usually require it to be completed within two hours after radiotherapy[5]. If the two treatments are in different units, this has to be agreed in advance.

  • What is the total cost of the whole course, and how is it counted if I cannot finish? Get it in writing. This question is a great deal easier to ask before you sign than after.

And one question that matters more than those six

“For my cancer and my stage, which study is this recommendation based on? Was that study randomised?”

This whole set of articles exists so that you can ask that sentence. The six cancer-site articles earlier give you the range the answer ought to fall in: the evidence is thicker for chest wall recurrence in breast cancer and for re-irradiation in head and neck cancer; the two randomised trials in cervical cancer do not agree with each other; the largest randomised trial in bladder cancer is negative[6]; and the data in anal and prostate cancer are not randomised at all. If the answer you are given falls a long way outside that range, that is a signal.

Why I am not listing names

Besides not being able to find an official compilation, there are two other reasons. First, the Medical Care Act regulates advertising by medical institutions, and a list of “which hospitals have it” drawn up by a practising doctor is in substance a list of recommendations — even if I am only stating facts. Second, the accuracy of a list like that lasts only until the next equipment update, and a patient education article stays online a long time. The misleading done by an out-of-date list is worse than having no list at all.

If you need to know where it can be done, the most direct route is to ask your current attending doctor to refer you or to enquire on your behalf — they have both the duty and the ability to answer that question, and they know your notes. The second route is to telephone the hospitals you are considering and ask the six questions above directly; whether they can answer clearly is information in itself.

Finally, about “our hospital has that machine”

My own hospital has hyperthermia equipment, and I deliver hyperthermia myself. So this is a sentence I say with particular care: having the equipment is not a reason; the indication is. The second standing warning at the top of this topic is about exactly this — the doctor you are looking for is one willing to tell you “this is not right for you”. In the hyperthermia box, the people it suits are in fact fewer than most people imagine: mostly local recurrence, tumours that have already been irradiated once, or situations where standard treatment cannot be given for some reason.

If you ask the questions above and the answers you get are clear, can be put in writing, and include “here is when I would advise you not to do it”, then whatever you decide in the end, the quality of that decision will be better.

One last role that is easy to overlook: your current attending doctor. Hyperthermia is added on top of existing treatment, so it will inevitably affect the scheduling of the original course, how your energy is distributed, and even how complications are read. Wherever you have it done, your existing treatment team should be told, and preferably before you start. If the two sides each go their own way, nobody has the full picture when something goes wrong — a risk shared by every add-on treatment, not just hyperthermia.

From the physician

That this article has no list of hospitals and no prices is deliberate. Lists go out of date, prices change, and I am not going to use a patient education article to influence which hospital you choose. What you need is the ability to ask the right question, and that does not expire.
