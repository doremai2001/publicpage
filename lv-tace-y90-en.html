HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

TACE and Y-90: the intermediate-stage workhorses

TACE's randomised evidence dates from 2002; when embolisation stops moving the tumour, it is time to change roads. Y-90 lost both of its duels with targeted therapy, but it has a box of its own.

Liver Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 13 references
#TACE & Y-90#Decisions#NHI Coverage

"Let's embolise once more and see" is sometimes inertia, not a plan. Refractory patients who keep embolising fare worse than those who change roads early; radiation after incomplete embolisation is a formal handover written into the NHI clause. Every embolisation spends from the liver-function account — this article shows you what to ask before the next one.

More than one tumour, or one too large to ablate and not resectable, but still confined to the liver, with liver function still holding up — the staging chart calls that box intermediate stage (BCLC B; the full account of staging is in "Where the staging chart puts you"). For twenty years the workhorse of this box has been transarterial chemoembolisation — TACE. This article covers three things: where TACE's evidence came from; why you change roads when embolisation stops moving the tumour; and where Y-90 radioembolisation's real place is.

My position first: stereotactic body radiotherapy and proton therapy for liver cancer are treatments I deliver myself — and much of it is self-paid. The other side of every comparison in this topic is my colleagues' surgery and ablation. That is why every comparison comes with its primary source, so you can take it to the surgical and hepatology clinics for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

TACE's randomised evidence comes from two trials in 2002

What put TACE in the intermediate-stage driving seat was a pair of randomised trials in 2002. The Barcelona group screened 903 patients to select 112 with Child-Pugh A/B, inoperable liver cancer, randomised to embolisation or conservative management; the trial ended early at interim analysis because the gap was already clear: hazard ratio for death 0.47 (a hazard ratio divides the speed at which deaths occur in one group by the speed in the other; 0.47 means deaths came distinctly more slowly in the embolisation group), two-year survival 63% against 27%[1]. Hong Kong's trial the same year (79 Asian patients) pointed the same way: one-, two- and three-year survival with TACE of 57%, 31% and 26%, against 32%, 11% and 3% in the control group[2]. Two things to hold together: this is evidence from more than twenty years ago, and the patients were strictly selected — 112 picked out of 903. What it proved is "for the right patients, embolisation beats no treatment", not "embolisation is enough for every intermediate-stage patient".

When embolisation stops working, embolising again is not a plan — it is inertia

TACE can be repeated, but not indefinitely. The definitions of "TACE refractory" differ in their details from group to group, but the direction is shared: if the tumour keeps progressing after several sessions, or liver function drops a grade after each one, it is time to admit this road has run its length[3]. A Japanese retrospective dataset (56 patients with confirmed refractoriness) showed that patients who switched treatment once refractoriness was confirmed survived longer than those who kept embolising — retrospective data, and the switch in that era was to the targeted drug sorafenib[4]. The Taiwan Liver Cancer Association's 2025 consensus on intermediate-stage liver cancer has also written the concept of "TACE unsuitability" formally into place — rather than embolise until the liver function is spent, change roads early[5]. Every embolisation spends from the liver-function account; that is the rule TACE shares with every local treatment (see "You have two diseases: the cancer and the cirrhosis").

Where embolisation is incomplete, radiation can take over — and here is the evidence exactly as it stands

This is my own field, so I write it more conservatively. An Italian phase III randomised trial enrolled patients with an incomplete response after TACE and compared adding SBRT with embolising again: local control was significantly better with SBRT (median not reached, against 8 months), one-year progression-free survival 37% against 13%, with no grade 3 or higher toxicity — but it closed early for slow accrual with only 40 patients, and in overall survival no difference could be seen[6]. The Dutch TRENDY trial (treatment-naïve, unresectable; TACE against SBRT) likewise ended early for insufficient accrual: among its 30 patients, local control favoured SBRT (100% against 43.8% at two years), and in time to progression no difference could be seen[7]. The meta-analysis result cited by Taiwan's 2025 intermediate-stage consensus reads: overall survival comparable between SBRT and TACE (hazard ratio 0.83), local control better with SBRT (hazard ratio 0.25)[5]. Three things must be written in the same paragraph: the local-control direction consistently leans towards SBRT; in overall survival no difference could be seen; and neither randomised trial filled its enrolment. "SBRT has been proven superior to TACE" is a sentence beyond the evidence, and I do not say it; but "radiation after failed embolisation" is written into the indication clause of the NHI's SBRT reimbursement (37047B: unable to undergo, or having failed, surgery, embolisation or ablation)[8] — the handover is a formal road, not a folk remedy.

Y-90: lost both head-to-heads, but one box is its own

Y-90 radioembolisation (radioactive microspheres delivered into the hepatic artery) has challenged targeted therapy for its position, and both phase III trials failed: France's SARAH (459 patients with locally advanced or TACE-failed disease) gave a median overall survival of 8.0 months against 9.9 months on sorafenib — not better[9]; the Asia-Pacific SIRveNIB (360 patients) came in at 8.8 against 10.0 months — again not[10]. Where the two trials agree is on lower toxicity (grade 3 or higher adverse events in SIRveNIB, 27.7% against 50.6%)[10]. So "Y-90 can replace systemic therapy" does not hold. The box where it lives looks like this: highly selected patients, tumour confined, the goal set at local control or at bridging and downstaging. The single-arm retrospective LEGACY study (162 patients with single tumours within 8 centimetres, Child-Pugh A, good performance status; median tumour 2.6 cm): objective response rate 88.3%, three-year survival 86.6% — single-arm and highly selected; those two labels do not come off[11]. On that basis BCLC 2022 wrote: Y-90 may be considered for single tumours within 8 centimetres[12]; the wording of Taiwan's 2025 intermediate-stage consensus is that "Y-90 can serve as an alternative to TACE"[5]. Taiwan's own multicentre prospective registry (83 liver cancers): objective response rate 55.41%, median survival 24.07 months, 13.79% downstaged into subsequent curative-intent treatment; and 2 deaths possibly related to treatment[13] — the benefits and the risks are both real.

In Taiwan: one is reimbursed, the other is self-paid

Conventional TACE is covered by the NHI: fee-schedule item 33144B, "vascular embolisation – Lipiodol", whose indication names conventional TACE for liver cancer, general materials included[8]. For drug-eluting-bead TACE (DEB-TACE), I could not find a citable official page on its reimbursement status — whether it is self-paid, confirm with your hospital. Y-90 is a different matter: searching the full NHI medical services fee schedule for "yttrium" or "microsphere" returns zero entries[8] — I could find no listed item (special materials have a separate list of their own, which is why I cannot write "the NHI has announced it is not covered"); in practice it requires self-payment, and I could not find an officially published price, so I am not putting a number here — ask the hospital's medical affairs office directly for the amount.

Before the next embolisation, put these on the table

Three things: how many TACE sessions you have had, and the imaging response assessment after each (complete, partial, or incomplete); this round's liver-function numbers (how they become a Child-Pugh score is in "You have two diseases: the cancer and the cirrhosis"); and a record of your fitness and symptoms. Two questions to bring into the clinic: "What is the goal of this embolisation — control, bridging, or waiting for downstaging?" "If the response is incomplete again, is the next step another embolisation, radiation, Y-90, or systemic therapy — and at which point does that call get made?" Take the references at the end of this article, and follow the hear-all-three-specialties routine recommended in "Three roads for a small liver cancer". If you meet transplant criteria, ask the extra question about a transplant assessment (see "Transplant: the one option that treats both diseases") — intermediate stage does not mean out of the running: patients who downstage successfully and go on to transplantation or surgery exist, and that story belongs to "The days on the waiting list".

From the physician

Before your next visit, gather how many TACE sessions you have had, each one's imaging response and this round's liver-function numbers, and ask: what is this session's goal? If the response is incomplete, at which point is the next step decided? Y-90 is self-paid — confirm the amount with the medical affairs office; do not extrapolate from someone else's quote.
