SURGERY AND AFTER

Temporary is a plan, not a promise

What a defunctioning stoma prevents, how many “temporary” stomas are never reversed, and the one ileostomy complication that sends people back to hospital.

Rectal Cancer Guide · Surgery and After · 19 references
#Stoma#Surgery#Daily Life#Quality of Life

The single most dangerous thing about a new ileostomy is not the wound — it is dehydration, and there is a number that tells you when to phone. Everything else in this piece is about who gets reversed and who does not.

When "stoma" appears on the consent form, most people hear one question: temporary or permanent? Both words get used far too loosely in clinic. "Temporary" is not a promise, it is a plan — and a proportion of plans do not come off. There is a number for that proportion. Few people volunteer it.

"Temporary" and "permanent" are two different operations

A temporary stoma is usually a loop ileostomy in the right lower abdomen, diverting the stream upstream of the join. It does not make the anastomosis heal better; it makes the consequences milder if the join leaks. Once healing is confirmed and adjuvant treatment done, a second operation closes it.

A permanent stoma is usually an end colostomy of the sigmoid after abdominoperineal resection. When the sphincter comes out with the tumour there is nothing left to join to, so there is no closing it back up. Which tumours end up there is covered in the piece "Will I need a stoma, and when will anyone know"

Either way, insist on preoperative stoma siting — the American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons' ostomy guideline lists siting and marking as a standard step[1]. Two centimetres in the wrong place is the difference between a baseplate that sticks and one that does not.

What the extra stoma actually prevents

A Swedish multicentre randomised trial enrolled 234 patients with an anastomosis within 7 cm of the anal verge (79.1% had had preoperative radiotherapy). With a defunctioning stoma, symptomatic anastomotic leak was 10.3% (12/116); without one, 28.0% (33/118) — odds ratio 3.4 (95% confidence interval 1.6–6.9, P<0.001). Urgent reoperation ran 8.6% against 25.4% (P<0.001)[2]. A meta-analysis of 13 studies and 8,002 patients points the same way: relative risk 0.47 for leak, 0.36 for reoperation[3].

The trial left an honest footnote. At a median of 42 months, the proportion still carrying a stoma of any kind was 13.8% against 16.9% — no significant difference[2]. What it prevents is a catastrophe, not the question of whether you end up with a stoma.

The figure nobody volunteers: how many "temporary" stomas are never reversed

Taiwan's own data is worth reading first. Among 1,020 Chang Gung patients with non-metastatic mid and low rectal cancer given a temporary stoma between 2000 and 2015, 17.5% (179 patients) ended up with a permanent one. Predictors visible before the operation: older age, male sex, CEA ≥10 ng/mL, T4, nodal involvement, a low tumour, ASA ≥III. Scored up, patients above 2 points ran at 29.9%, those at 2 or below at 11.3%[4].

Overseas figures are of the same order, and two Swedish cohorts must be kept apart. In the Swedish Colorectal Cancer Registry, across 4,648 anterior resections, a permanent stoma was present two years after surgery in 13.6% of the 2007–2013 cohort and 15.2% of the 2014–2015 cohort[5]. In a separate Swedish single-centre series of 134 patients, 21% were never reversed, most closely tied to stage IV disease and symptomatic leak (both P<0.001)[6]. A pooled analysis of 14 studies and 14,265 patients adds preoperative chemoradiotherapy, ASA ≥3 and recurrence[7].

One counterintuitive finding belongs beside those. In 4,529 Swedish anterior resections, having a defunctioning stoma was itself associated with a higher absolute risk of still having a permanent stoma two years later: a risk difference of +0.11 after total mesorectal excision[8]. This is observational data, and diversion goes more often to higher-risk patients anyway, so it cannot be read as "the stoma made you permanent". It does say the decision deserves a reason you have heard before the operation, rather than arriving as part of a set menu.

But 17.5% is not your probability; it is a group average. Take your own CEA, stage and tumour height and ask: with my numbers, which side of that am I on?

When does it get closed? The commonest reason for delay is not medical

Of the 106 reversals at the Swedish centre above, only 19% happened within four months; 81% were later. The commonest single reason was "low medical priority given to this procedure", 58%, then non-surgical complications 20% and leak 12%[6]. More than half the waiting is not the body being unready; it is that nobody is chasing. "Where am I on the list for my reversal?" is worth asking at every visit.

The other direction is closing early. The EASY trial, across eight Danish and Swedish hospitals, randomised patients with no clinical or radiological sign of leak to closure at 8 to 13 days or after 12 weeks (127 randomised, 112 analysed). Mean complications over twelve months were 1.2 against 2.9 (P<0.0001); the conclusion was that this is safe in selected patients[9]. Keep the limits alongside it: anyone with signs of a leak was excluded, and the analysis rests on 112 patients.

The ileostomy complication to memorise is not the wound. It is dehydration.

Have your family read this section too. In 603 loop ileostomies the 60-day readmission rate was 16.9% (102 patients), and dehydration was the commonest single cause, 43.1% of all readmissions. That study defined dehydration as output of ≥1,500 mL over 24 hours with a BUN/creatinine ratio ≥20. The only independent risk factor was still being on a diuretic after surgery (p=0.0001)[10].

The red line is concrete: more than about 1,500 millilitres emptied in a day, or thirst with urine that is scanty and dark, dizziness on standing, two kilograms lost in a few days — call the team that day, do not wait for the next clinic. Left alone this becomes acute kidney injury. Anyone already on a diuretic or a blood pressure drug should ask before discharge whether it needs adjusting.

One combination gets missed. Many people carry a stoma through adjuvant chemotherapy, and capecitabine and oxaliplatin themselves raise the risk of diarrhoea and dehydration. During chemotherapy contact the team the same day for any of these: fever above 38°C, severe diarrhoea or a sudden surge in stoma output with dehydration, mouth ulcers bad enough to stop you swallowing, or clearly worse numbness in the hands and feet. With capecitabine, watch for hand-foot syndrome — redness, peeling and pain on palms and soles. Oxaliplatin causes cold-induced neuropathy: fingers or throat feel as though shocked on contact with cold water or cold air. Avoid cold things for those few days and report it; do not stop the drug on your own.

The change you put off is why the skin breaks down a fortnight later

Taipei Veterans General Hospital's patient education is specific: the ileostomy baseplate changes about every 3 to 5 days, a colostomy every 5 to 7 days; high output needs fluid and electrolyte replacement; for wind and odour, less milk, fewer beans, sweet potato and onion, and more activity[11]. Taichung Veterans General Hospital's version says every 5 to 7 days, or immediately if it leaks or the skin around the stoma turns white[12]. Skin problems nearly always begin with a leak.

Parastomal hernia, prolapse, stenosis and retraction get full chapters in the ostomy guideline above[1]; what you notice first is usually a bulge and a baseplate that will not hold. Come back to clinic and change the appliance rather than adding more layers of tape.

Taiwan has this expertise. Certification as a wound, ostomy and continence nurse takes more than 320 hours of theory and placement plus an examination, and close to 80 nurses here are internationally certified by the World Council of Enterostomal Therapists[13]. Find out before discharge who yours is.

A stoma closes off nothing, but ask about a few things first

This section has no trial data behind it; it is how I practise. Once the bag is on securely you can get in the water; swimmers usually add waterproof tape and a smaller pouch. Work is not restricted — the awkward part is somewhere and some time to change, worth raising with your employer early rather than gritting through. Exercise is fine; the issue is intra-abdominal pressure, so heavy lifting and heavy core work in recovery go past your stoma nurse (造口護理師) first.

On sex I will not dress things up. In the analysis from the Dutch TME trial, the presence of a stoma was itself an independent risk factor for sexual dysfunction in both men and women, alongside autonomic nerve damage, intraoperative blood loss, anastomotic leak and preoperative radiotherapy[14]. There is a physical component and a body-image one; both can be worked on, and both are in the piece "Fertility questions have no next month".

Taiwanese status and subsidies: what is settled, and what you must go and ask

Catastrophic illness status (重大傷病) is settled. Under Article 48 of the National Health Insurance Act, a holder pays no co-payment for "the illness recorded on the catastrophic illness certificate, or treatment the treating physician judges related to that illness"[15]. One application requirement is a medical certificate issued by a contracted hospital within the previous thirty days; the certificate expires, so renew it before it lapses[16].

Disability certification (身心障礙證明) needs caution. Taichung Veterans General Hospital's official patient-education page states that a patient with a permanent colostomy may "collect a disability assessment form from the district office, have a physician complete it, and apply"[12]. But I could not find the corresponding item and grade in the Ministry of Health and Welfare's assessment criteria, so I will not tell you which category or level you would fall under. Take it to the hospital social work department to file.

The cost of ostomy supplies is the part I am least willing to be vague about. In the official documents I could find, the national schedule of assistive-device subsidies for people with disabilities does not list ostomy pouches or baseplates[17], and the National Health Insurance schedule of medical service payment items makes no mention of ostomy consumables[18]. The schedule I cite is an older version, and counties and cities add their own top-ups. Do not take anybody's spoken word that this is or is not covered — have the hospital social work department and your household-registration social affairs bureau each check it once. This is a long-running expense, and getting it wrong costs real money.

Last, other people who have done this. The organisation I can verify here is the Colorectal Cancer Alliance of R.O.C. (社團法人中華民國大腸直腸癌關懷協會), registered in 2015, with the 1987 Veterans General Hospital ostomy club among its predecessors, offering medical and psychological support to people with stomas and their families on (02) 2875-7544 extension 67313[19]. The first time you are sweating through a bag change, two sentences from someone five years in beat ten videos.

From the physician

The permanent-stoma rate quoted here is a cohort average; take your own CEA, stage and tumour height to a preoperative conversation and ask which side of it you sit on. Ask at every clinic visit where your reversal sits on the list, since more than half of delays are administrative rather than medical. On appliance costs and disability status, get the hospital social work department and your local social affairs bureau to check separately.
