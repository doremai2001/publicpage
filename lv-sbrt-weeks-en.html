DURING TREATMENT

What the two weeks of SBRT actually look like

The NHI clause fixes the course at two weeks and no more than six fractions; from simulation, respiratory control and fiducial markers to the imaging changes after treatment, this walks through what is normal and what means going back to hospital.

Liver Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 12 references
#SBRT#Treatment Pathway#Daily Life

This article has two honest gaps: how many days sit between simulation and the first treatment, and how many minutes a single session takes — I could not find a citable source for either, so I give no numbers. What I can give has sources: the fractionation schemes, the shape of the side effects in two very different populations, and why the scans do not go clean straight after treatment.

A full course of liver SBRT is over within two weeks and six fractions — against the five to six weeks of conventional radiotherapy, short enough to make many people uneasy. This article lays those two weeks out segment by segment: what the simulation session is preparing for, how the machine keeps up with your breathing, what the side effects look like, and why the scans do not go clean straight after treatment ends.

My position first: stereotactic body radiotherapy and proton therapy for liver cancer are treatments I deliver myself — and much of it is self-paid. The other side of every comparison in this topic is my colleagues' surgery and ablation. That is why every comparison comes with its primary source, so you can take it to the surgical and hepatology clinics for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

"Two weeks" is not my estimate — the NHI clause fixes it in writing

When people hear the whole course takes only two weeks, the commonest reaction in clinic is "that fast — are you cutting corners?" I usually just read the clause out. The payment rules under which Taiwan's National Health Insurance (NHI) covers stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) of the liver say it in black and white: "the full course is two weeks with no more than six fractions"; it is a bundled payment requiring prior review, and for liver tumours the coverage conditions are a single lesion, 5 cm or less, Child-Pugh A to B, in a patient unable to undergo, or having previously failed, surgery, embolisation or ablation[1]. The Ministry of Health and Welfare's announcement when it brought the treatment into coverage put it more plainly still: a course takes about one to two weeks, with one treatment every one to three days, six fractions at most — against the five to six weeks and roughly thirty fractions of conventional radiotherapy[2]. The international definition is drawn tighter yet: the AASLD's 2023 liver cancer guidance defines SBRT as stereotactic radiotherapy completed "in five or fewer fractions"[3]. Short is not skimping — it is the native shape of this treatment: a high dose per fraction, few fractions, trading precision for time.

The actual regimens in prospective trials fall in the same range: a Korean phase II trial of 50 patients with small tumours of 5 cm or less delivered 45 Gy in three fractions on three consecutive days[4]; a Canadian series of large tumours, over half with vascular invasion, used six[5]. Your number of fractions will land somewhere between three and six, decided by tumour size, position and liver function. What the Child-Pugh score is, and why it decides so much, is in "You have two diseases: the cancer and the cirrhosis". One further note: the two-week course definition is how the NHI bundle is written — self-paid arrangements or particular platforms may differ, so go by your hospital's treatment plan.

Before the first beam, the preparation is what takes the time

Before treatment proper there is a simulation session: you lie in the position you will use for every treatment, an immobilisation mould is made, and planning images are taken so the computer knows where the tumour sits relative to the surrounding organs. How many days from simulation to the first treatment? Honestly, I could not find any citable standard number of days, and the differences between hospitals' scheduling are real — this preparation period is usually counted in weeks, and the actual dates go by what your treatment team schedules. The time is not empty waiting: the doctor is contouring the treatment volume and the physicists are calculating the dose and running quality checks — it is the most labour-intensive part of the whole course; you just do not see it.

The liver moves with every breath — how the machine keeps up

The liver moves up and down with each breath, and this is the single most important technical question in liver SBRT. The American Association of Physicists in Medicine's TG-76 report sorts the approaches into five categories: motion-encompassing methods (including the whole range of movement in the treated volume), respiratory gating (delivering the beam only during a set phase of the breathing cycle), breath-hold, forced shallow breathing with abdominal compression, and respiration-synchronised tracking; its clinical recommendation is to measure each patient's tumour motion where possible, and to use respiratory motion management when the movement exceeds 5 mm and the patient can cooperate[6]. The NHI announcement itself expects the treatment to be delivered "with respiratory control and image guidance"[2]. Which category you get depends on your hospital's equipment and how well you can cooperate with the breathing routine — no one category is the single right answer.

Fiducial markers first? The risk is close to a biopsy's, but it is not zero

Some treatment platforms need a few rice-grain-sized metal markers implanted near the tumour first, so the machine can track its position in real time during treatment; not every machine needs them, and whether you have them is decided by the platform and the imaging conditions. The implantation itself is like having a percutaneous needle procedure. A series of 139 implantations (34 of them in the liver) reported major complications in 5% and minor complications in 17.3%, with 4.3% of markers migrating and needing further attention; the authors' conclusion was that the risk is comparable to a conventional percutaneous organ biopsy[7]. But there is a number I cannot leave out: in a high-risk prospective series of 36 liver cancer patients, all with portal vein tumour thrombus, 72% scored their pain at only 0 to 1 and 14% needed daytime observation — but one patient (3%) died of a haemothorax; in patients with poor liver function and poor general condition, the complication risk really is higher[8]. That is something worth asking about properly before you sign the consent form, not a line of small print to skim past.

What happens once you are on the treatment couch

Every treatment runs the same way: positioning, imaging to confirm, then the beam. How many minutes you actually lie there per session — I could not find a citable figure, and the differences between platforms are large, so I will only give you the shape of it: the positioning and image checks usually take longer than the irradiation itself, the irradiation is completely painless with no sensation at all, and you can walk out of the hospital the same day and eat normally. Across a course of six fractions or fewer, most people carry on with their ordinary lives.

How big the side effects are depends almost entirely on which group you are in

The same treatment differs in toxicity by an order of magnitude between two populations, so I am putting the two sets of numbers side by side — find your own seat. At the small-tumour, good-liver-function end: that Korean phase II trial (50 patients, all Child-Pugh 5 to 6, median tumour 1.3 cm) saw mostly mild fatigue and loss of appetite, grade 1 (mild, needing no intervention) liver enzyme rises in 32% of patients and grade 2 rises in 2%, no grade 3 or higher side effects at all, and every patient completed the course without interruption; the Child-Pugh score worsened by 2 points or more in 2 patients (4%)[4]. At the large-tumour, advanced end: the Canadian series (102 patients, median tumour volume 117 ml, 55% with vascular thrombosis) had grade 3 or higher toxicity in 30%, with 7 deaths possibly related to treatment[5]. Which end you belong to decides which set of numbers you should use to imagine your own two weeks. There is randomised data on the comparison too: a randomised trial published in 2025 (recurrent, single liver cancers of 5 cm or less; 83 patients each on SBRT and ablation) found acute and late adverse event rates comparable between the two arms[9]. And there is a window that only opens after treatment finishes: the typical onset of radiation-induced liver disease is two weeks to three months after treatment[10] — whether a swelling belly or growing drowsiness in that period means going back to hospital the same day is covered in the full list in "When to come back to hospital the same day"; please bookmark that one.

Afterwards, it does not vanish from the scans straight away

This is the biggest difference between SBRT and ablation in how you "see the result", and the place where most people worry for nothing. Ablation shows you a cavity immediately; with SBRT you watch the tumour go out slowly. An American imaging series followed 10 liver cancers whose successful treatment had been confirmed by pathology or by tumour marker (alpha-fetoprotein): 4 still showed arterial-phase enhancement 3 to 12 months after treatment, and 9 still showed wash-out — note that all of these were tumours already confirmed as successfully treated[11]. The same team's larger series (67 tumours, three years of follow-up) showed 58% with persisting arterial-phase enhancement after treatment; assessed at 3 to 6 months with mRECIST, the standard imaging response criteria, only 25% could be called a "complete response" and the remaining 75% read as "stable disease" — yet almost all of those were controlled tumours, and the authors say directly that these criteria should be used with caution early after SBRT[12]. The radiotherapy quality literature adds the same caution: transient tissue density changes on CT in the two to three months after treatment are common and carry no clinical meaning, and should not be confused with tumour progression or irreversible liver injury[10]. So when a follow-up report says "enhancement still present", do not pass sentence on yourself — what the radiologist and the radiation oncologist are looking at is whether it has grown, not whether it enhances. Steady growth is a different matter; that does call for reassessment.

Three things you can start preparing today

First, ask exactly how many fractions you will have and roughly when, and put them in your phone's calendar — three or six, consecutive or alternate days, the answers are all in your treatment plan; there is no need to guess. Second, how to prepare on simulation day (what to wear, whether to eat) differs with every hospital's information sheet — go by the one you are given, and photograph it as soon as you have it. Third, open a page of notes: after each treatment, one line on fatigue, appetite, any nausea. Spread it out at the follow-up visit and those ten minutes become remarkably efficient. As for situations outside the NHI conditions (multiple lesions, larger than 5 cm, re-irradiation), which are self-paid: I could not find a citable official published price, so ask the hospital's medical affairs office directly — with numbers like this, the one you ask for is always more reliable than the one you overhear.
