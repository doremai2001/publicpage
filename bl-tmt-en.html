MUSCLE-INVASIVE: TWO ROADS

Trimodality therapy: how it is done, and who it suits

How resection, radiotherapy and concurrent chemotherapy fit together, where the three guidelines differ in their wording about who is suitable, and why BC2001's three numbers have to be read together.

Bladder Cancer Guide · Muscle-invasive: two roads · 13 references
#Organ Preservation#Chemotherapy#Evidence#NHI Coverage

On the question of who is suitable, three guidelines produce three versions: EAU allows unilateral hydronephrosis, AUA has no such sentence, and NICE gives no tumour criteria at all. That is not one of them getting it wrong — this decision has never had a single agreed table behind it.

What patients hear in the phrase "trimodality therapy" is usually "no operation". The actual sequence is: scrape away as much of the visible tumour as possible, then four to six and a half weeks of radiotherapy, with radiosensitising chemotherapy running alongside it. This article is about who it suits, how it is actually delivered, which sentences the evidence will carry, and the things it cannot do.

My position first: the radiotherapy inside trimodality therapy is the part I deliver myself, and this topic argues that Taiwan under-uses bladder preservation — an argument that suits me, and you have every reason to discount it accordingly. So every comparison here comes with its primary source, and I have written down the things bladder preservation cannot do as well. Take it to the urology clinic for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

First, the shape of the evidence

The 2026 EAU guideline puts it in its own words: "No successfully completed RCTs are available comparing the outcomes of TMT with RC." — no successfully completed randomised trial compares trimodality therapy with radical cystectomy[1]. The one trial that closed for want of patients was called SPARE, and it enrolled only 45[2]. There was one randomised trial in Denmark in 1991 that did finish (DAVECA 8201, 183 patients, T2 to T4a), but what it compared was preoperative radiotherapy plus planned cystectomy against full-dose radiotherapy with salvage surgery where needed; it accrued between 1983 and 1986 and gave no concurrent chemotherapy — so it is not the trimodality therapy we are talking about today[3]. How the two roads compare is in "Remove the bladder, or keep it"; this article covers only what happens on this side of the fork.

Who it suits: three guidelines, three different wordings

EAU 2026 gives the most specific tumour criteria, word for word: "solitary, unifocal cT2-T3a tumours, absence of extensive or multifocal CIS, no or unilateral hydronephrosis, and good baseline bladder function" — a single, unifocal cT2 to T3a tumour, no extensive or multifocal carcinoma in situ, no hydronephrosis or unilateral only, and good bladder function at baseline; the same passage also says that patient selection is the key to good outcomes[1].

The AUA/ASCO/SUO 2024 wording is different: "Patients with large tumors unable to be resected by TURBT, multifocal CIS, T3/T4 tumors, and/or hydronephrosis are not ideal candidates for any type of bladder preserving therapy." The ideal candidate is described as one in whom "complete resection is feasible and who have no hydronephrosis and no CIS"[4]. The differences are easy to see: EAU allows unilateral hydronephrosis and AUA has no such sentence; EAU says no extensive or multifocal carcinoma in situ, while AUA writes no carcinoma in situ at all and recommends random biopsies to confirm it[1][4].

NICE NG2 gives no tumour criteria at all. Its recommendation 1.5.5 says only "Offer a choice of radical cystectomy or radiotherapy with a radiosensitiser"; 1.5.6 requires that the discussion include a urologist who performs radical cystectomy, a clinical oncologist and a clinical nurse specialist; and 1.5.7 requires the discussion to cover the fact that the limited evidence cannot establish whether surgery or radiotherapy with a radiosensitiser gives better outcomes, along with the effects of each on sexual and bowel function and the risk of death from the treatment itself[5]. That cell is empty; I have not left it out by accident. All three documents share exactly three things: a multidisciplinary team, shared decision-making, and that radiotherapy alone will not do — AUA's clause reads "clinicians should not offer radiation therapy alone as a curative treatment"[4][1].

Histology is a threshold too. EAU writes that urothelial carcinoma with squamous, glandular or micropapillary variant histology has complete response, survival and salvage cystectomy rates close to those of pure urothelial carcinoma and may be considered; but that pure squamous cell carcinoma or adenocarcinoma does worse after trimodality therapy and should be advised to go straight to surgery[1]. AUA is firmer: adenocarcinoma, sarcoma and squamous cell carcinoma were not included in any prospective radiation-based bladder preservation study, and unless surgery is unsuitable these patients should not receive this treatment[4].

The resection is not a warm-up

EAU is explicit: when trimodality therapy is planned, the visible tumour should be resected as completely as possible, and on a second resection residual disease is found in more than half of cases[1]. AUA's clause 22 likewise requires maximal debulking resection in anyone being considered for bladder preservation, along with assessment for multifocal disease and carcinoma in situ[4]. "They got it all last time" is less reliable than it sounds — the quality markers for resection are in "The first resection decides everything after it".

Concurrent chemotherapy is not a "boost": BC2001's three numbers go together

BC2001 was a multicentre phase III trial in which 360 patients were randomised to radiotherapy with or without concurrent chemotherapy (fluorouracil and mitomycin C), with a median follow-up of 69.9 months[6]. All three numbers are needed; the 95% confidence intervals in the brackets below mean that if you ran the same study many times over, the answer would land in that range:

  • The primary endpoint, two-year locoregional disease-free survival: 67% (95% confidence interval 59–74) against 54% (46–62), hazard ratio 0.68 (0.48–0.96), p=0.03 — significant[6].

  • Five-year overall survival: 48% (40–55) against 35% (28–43), hazard ratio 0.82 (0.63–1.09), p=0.16 — not significant[6].

  • Grade 3 to 4 adverse events: during treatment 36.0% against 27.5%, p=0.07; during follow-up 8.3% against 15.7%, p=0.07, and here it was the chemoradiotherapy arm that was lower. The conclusion sentence in the NEJM paper reads "with no significant increase in adverse events"[6].

The ten-year follow-up (median 9.9 years) makes it plainer still: locoregional control holds up, hazard ratio 0.61 (0.43–0.86, p=0.004), but none of the four endpoints of disease-free survival, metastasis-free survival, overall survival and bladder cancer-specific survival reached significance; the five-year cystectomy rate was 14% (9–21) with chemoradiotherapy against 22% (16–31) with radiotherapy alone, hazard ratio 0.54 (0.31–0.95), p=0.034[7]. So what the concurrent chemotherapy buys is local control and one fewer cystectomy, not a proof of longer survival — which is exactly why it cannot be written off as "just a boost".

Choosing the radiosensitiser: there is no ranking

Whether cisplatin can be used comes down to the same set of Galsky criteria, written up in "Why chemotherapy comes before the operation". For those who cannot have it, EAU's options are fluorouracil with mitomycin-C, or low-dose gemcitabine[1]; AUA's clause reads "Carboplatin should not be used as a radiosensitizer unless there are contraindications to cisplatin, 5-FU, and gemcitabine."[4]

RTOG 0712 is often brought out to compare two radiosensitiser combinations, but the authors themselves write that the trial was not powered for a between-arm comparison: 66 patients were analysable, the three-year distant-metastasis-free rate was 78% in one arm and 84% in the other; treatment-related grade 3 to 4 toxicity was 21/33 (64%) against 18/33 (55%), mostly haematological[8]. These figures can be used to gauge the order of magnitude of toxicity, not to rank the arms.

The second sensitising route is hypoxia modification. BCON randomised 333 patients; the primary endpoint, cystoscopic control at six months, was 81% against 76%, p=0.3 — the primary endpoint did not reach significance, and only slightly more than half the patients actually had a cystoscopy at that time point. In the same trial, three-year overall survival was 59% against 46%, p=0.04, and three-year locoregional-recurrence-free survival 54% against 43%, p=0.06; late urinary and gastrointestinal complications were similar in the two arms[9]. The primary endpoint failed and survival passed: both sentences have to be said together to be honest.

Four weeks or six and a half

An individual-patient-data meta-analysis of BC2001 and BCON, 782 patients, median follow-up 120 months: 55 Gy in 20 fractions over four weeks carried a lower risk of invasive locoregional recurrence than 64 Gy in 32 fractions over six and a half weeks, adjusted hazard ratio 0.71 (95% confidence interval 0.52–0.96), establishing non-inferiority and reaching superiority as well; late bladder and rectal toxicity was comparable (absolute risk difference −3.37%, −11.85 to 5.10)[10]. A non-inferiority test on its own only proves "not much worse", not "just as good"; this one measured superiority too. But one label cannot be dropped: that cohort enrolled high-grade T1G3 non-muscle-invasive disease as well as T2 to T4 N0M0, not muscle-invasive disease only[10].

NICE writes both fractionations into its recommendation (for example 64 Gy in 32 fractions over six and a half weeks, or 55 Gy in 20 fractions over four weeks) and notes that as of February 2015 both radiosensitiser combinations were off-label uses[5]; EAU describes 40 to 45 Gy to the whole pelvis or whole bladder to start with, boosted to 60 to 66 Gy, with moderate hypofractionation of 55 Gy in 20 fractions an accepted alternative when the bladder alone is treated[1]. What happens on the day of the planning scan, how the daily set-up works and how to prepare the bladder and bowel during the course are already written up in the pelvic radiotherapy series on this site: "What happens on the day of the planning scan", "Daily imaging: the underrated half", "Those weeks of bladder and passing urine" and "Bladder and bowel: copying the same state each day". I am not rewriting them here. The separate line of hyperthermia combined with intravesical chemotherapy is in "Bladder: two routes, not one treatment".

Trials that are running get written as running

Two phase III trials are currently testing the addition of immunotherapy to trimodality therapy. SWOG/NRG S1806 (NCT03775265) compares concurrent chemoradiotherapy with or without atezolizumab, with a planned enrolment of 475, started in June 2019, primary endpoint bladder-intact event-free survival, status active but no longer recruiting, estimated primary completion 1 June 2027[11]. KEYNOTE-992 (NCT04241185) compares concurrent chemoradiotherapy with or without pembrolizumab, planned enrolment 520, started in May 2020, the same primary endpoint, likewise active but no longer recruiting, estimated primary completion 31 January 2027 and whole-study completion estimated in 2031[12]. EAU's wording is that the integration of immunotherapy with trimodality therapy remains under study: "Outside of trials, combined use remains investigational."[1] Until there are results, these two names are only "trials worth asking whether anyone is still enrolling for", not an effect.

The Taiwanese side, and what to take into the clinic

Every component of trimodality therapy has an item in the NHI fee schedule: on the resection side, 78008C endoscopic resection of a bladder tumour including cystoscopy, 8,027 points; on the radiotherapy side, 36018B simulation imaging at 3,619 points, 36015B computerised treatment planning – complex at 11,483 points (the note states that it covers conformal, intensity-modulated and stereotactic techniques), the simple and complex field charges under 36011B and 36012B, and 36019B dose calculation; on the follow-up side, 28019C cystoscopy at 1,800 points[13]. But trimodality therapy itself is not a bundled item, and a full-text search of the whole fee schedule for 「膀胱癌」 returns zero hits, while 「低分次」 returns only three — two breast cancer items and one rectal cancer item, none for bladder cancer[13]. So how the four-week schedule gets claimed where you are, and whether it needs prior review, is something I could not find a listed item for; please ask the medical affairs office or your case manager, and do not infer from "there is an item" that "it is certainly paid".

Four sentences to take to the clinic: which clauses of which guideline does my tumour meet? Does the resection need doing again? Can my kidney function take cisplatin, and if not, what are you planning to use? Is trimodality therapy done here, and if not, where would I be referred? Writing that last one down matters — this is not a treatment every hospital provides.

From the physician

If you are considering this road, ask your doctor to point out in front of you which clauses of which guideline your tumour meets, and to answer plainly whether it is done here and where you would be referred if not. Not every hospital provides this treatment, and the selection criteria and the quality of the resection decide the whole stretch of road that follows.
