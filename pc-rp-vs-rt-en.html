HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

Surgery or radiotherapy: different bills

One randomised trial found no difference in cancer outcomes; the observational data disagree, and the side effects are not fewer on either side.

Prostate Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 17 references
#Prostate#Decisions#Surgery#Quality of Life#Clinical Trials

I am a radiation oncologist writing a comparison that includes my own specialty. So the figures that go against radiotherapy are all in here — the fifteen-year randomised results, the observational data favouring surgery, the faecal leakage, the second cancers.

This was the hardest piece in the series to write, because I am a radiation oncologist. Most surgery-versus-radiotherapy comparisons online were also written by someone with a position. So I will put mine on the table first, then both sides' numbers on the same table — including the ones that go against radiotherapy.

Something my own specialty does not much enjoy saying

A national survey published in JAMA in 2000 went to urologists and radiation oncologists across the United States; 504 replied (64%) and 559 (76%). Asked about a man with moderately differentiated localised prostate cancer and a life expectancy over ten years, 93% of urologists chose radical prostatectomy, while 72% of radiation oncologists regarded surgery and external beam radiotherapy as equivalent. The authors put it bluntly: specialists overwhelmingly recommend the treatment they themselves deliver. Both specialties quoted much the same complication risks — the disagreement was not in the figures, it was in the recommendation[1]. The survey was fielded in 1998 and I found no recent replication, so it records a structural bias historically rather than commenting on any doctor practising today. And that 72% is not my specialty's certificate of innocence: the finding is that physicians favour the treatment they perform, and that sentence includes me, and it includes the piece you are reading. Apply the same discount here — I have put every figure unfavourable to radiotherapy into this piece so that you need not rely on my judgement alone. What the survey explains is why you hear two different tones in two clinic rooms.

The NCCN patient guideline handles this directly: because long-term cancer control is similar after surgery and after radiotherapy, you should see both a radiation oncologist and a urologist before deciding which suits you[2].

Only one trial has actually randomised the two

ProtecT randomised 1,643 men with localised prostate cancer to active monitoring (545), radical prostatectomy (553) or radiotherapy (545), with a median follow-up of 15 years and 98% follow-up completeness. There were 45 prostate-cancer deaths (2.7%): 3.1% in the monitoring group, 2.2% after surgery, 2.9% after radiotherapy, P=0.53; all-cause mortality was 21.7% and similar across the three. Metastases were 9.4%, 4.7% and 5.0%[3]. The ten-year results pointed the same way[4].

Between surgery and radiotherapy there was no significant difference on any cancer endpoint, at ten years or at fifteen. A P value of 0.53 means no difference was demonstrated, which is not the same as the two having been proved equivalent. And ProtecT enrolled PSA-screen-detected men aged 50 to 69, not men with cT3b or Gleason 9 disease — "the two come out the same" is not a sentence that extends indefinitely[3].

Then why does a whole body of research say surgery lives longer

Because it does. A 2016 systematic review and meta-analysis in European Urology pooled 19 observational studies and up to 118,830 men, radiotherapy against surgery: adjusted all-cause mortality hazard ratio 1.63 (1.54–1.73) and prostate-cancer mortality hazard ratio 2.08 (1.76–2.47), both favouring surgery; stratifying by risk group, radiation technique, era and follow-up length did not change the direction[5]. A hazard ratio compares how fast events accrue over the same stretch of time: 1.63 means deaths came sooner in the radiotherapy group, not that 63% of anyone dies.

The authors say so themselves: this is observational data at low to moderate risk of bias, and there is no level-1 evidence on the question[5]. The reason is not obscure. Men sent for radiotherapy in a registry are older, carry more comorbidity and sit in higher risk groups; adjustment does not repair that kind of difference, and randomisation does — and ProtecT found none[3]. I am not going to bury that 1.63. But posting it without explaining how it arises is misleading in the other direction.

The cost is not more on one side and less on the other. It is two different costs

ProtecT collected patient-completed questionnaires year by year, response rates above 80% for most items[6]:

  • Years 7 to 12, leakage requiring pad use: 18–24% after surgery, 3–8% after radiotherapy, 9–11% with monitoring. The gap did not narrow with time.

  • Year 7, erections firm enough for intercourse: 18% after surgery, 30% with monitoring, 27% after radiotherapy; by year 12 all three had fallen to low levels. One thing has to be said here: the ProtecT radiotherapy group also had three to six months of neoadjuvant hormone therapy[4], so part of that group's sexual and hormone-related figures is on hormone therapy's account.

  • Year 12, getting up to pass urine twice or more a night: radiotherapy 48%, monitoring 47%, surgery 34% — here surgery does better.

  • Year 12, faecal leakage once a week or more: 12% after radiotherapy, 6% in the other two groups.

The shape of the first six years is worth keeping too: surgery hit sexual function and continence hardest, with partial recovery afterwards but a persistent deficit; radiotherapy's sexual effect peaked at six months, then recovered somewhat and stayed stable, with little effect on continence; bowel function worsened at six months and mostly recovered, except for bloody stools, which rose year on year[7].

So "radiotherapy has fewer side effects" is a sentence I cannot write; half of it is wrong. The honest version: surgery charges you continence and erections, radiotherapy charges you bowel function and long-term urinary irritation. How long each runs and what can be done about them is set out in "Why continence rates never agree", "Does rehabilitation work, and what happens to ejaculation" and "That 13.7% is being read wrong".

What the large non-randomised datasets add

The American CEASAR cohort followed 2,445 men for a median of 9.5 years, comparing EPIC-26 questionnaire scores. First, how to read a score: these are adjusted mean differences, and the smallest difference a man can actually feel is about 6 to 9 points for incontinence, 10 to 12 for sexual function, 4 to 6 for bowel and hormonal domains. A 4-point difference can be statistically significant and still go unnoticed[8].

In the unfavourable-prognosis group, surgery against external beam radiotherapy with hormone therapy: surgery's incontinence score was 26.6 points worse (−35.0 to −18.2); sexual function differed by −1.4 (−11.1 to 8.3), which is no difference. It runs the other way too: at ten years the radiotherapy-plus-hormone group was worse on bowel function, −4.9 (−9.2 to −0.7), and on hormone-related symptoms, −4.9 (−9.5 to −0.3)[8].

This is not randomised data: in CEASAR's three-year analysis the radiotherapy group averaged 6.6 years older and started with worse sexual function scores (52.3 against 65.2)[9]. PROST-QA, a prospective study of 1,201 patients and 625 partners, concluded that the three treatments have three shapes rather than a ranking — urinary irritation after brachytherapy lasts longest, and after surgery incontinence appears while obstructive voiding improves, particularly in men with a large prostate[10].

Second cancers belong on the page, but with the denominator attached

A 2016 BMJ analysis pooled 21 observational studies: against men who had no radiotherapy, the adjusted hazard ratio after radiotherapy was 1.67 (1.55–1.80) for bladder cancer and 1.79 (1.34–2.38) for colorectal cancer; haematological malignancy and lung cancer did not reach significance. The absolute proportions are low — the highest rates reported in any study were 3.8% for bladder and 4.2% for colorectal cancer. External beam radiotherapy was consistently associated with raised odds while brachytherapy was not, and 13 of the 21 studies used surgical patients as the comparison group[11].

The GEC-ESTRO PROBATE group's systematic review converts this into absolute risk: radiation-induced second primaries run at roughly 1 in 220 to 1 in 290, and about 1 in 70 in cohorts followed beyond ten years — but those figures come from an older era of non-conformal, large-field treatment, and for intensity-modulated radiotherapy and brachytherapy the available data are insufficient to draw a conclusion[12].

A man of 72 should not turn down radiotherapy over this — the latency runs beyond ten years and the absolute excess is small. A man of 55 should not pretend it is not there.

If it fails, the two ways back are not symmetrical

Definitions first, because these two thresholds get confused. After radiotherapy, biochemical failure is a rise of 2 ng/mL or more above the PSA nadir (the Phoenix consensus) — PSA does not fall to zero after radiotherapy anyway. After surgery it is a detectable, rising PSA[13].

When surgery fails, salvage radiotherapy is a well-worn path, but no guarantee: in a multi-institutional series of 1,540 men, the six-year probability of freedom from progression was 32% overall, and only 48% even when started at a PSA of 0.50 ng/mL or below[14].

When radiotherapy fails, salvage surgery is possible, at a considerably higher price: in an international multi-institutional series of 404 men, ten-year biochemical recurrence-free survival after salvage prostatectomy was 37%[15]. The MASTER analysis pooled 150 studies of local salvage after radiotherapy: five-year recurrence-free survival was much the same across methods (50% to 60% adjusted), but severe urinary toxicity was not — 20% adjusted for salvage prostatectomy, under 10% for SBRT and the brachytherapy techniques[16].

So "if surgery fails you can still have radiotherapy, and if radiotherapy fails you can still have surgery" is not two symmetrical sentences. The full set of options after biochemical recurrence sits in "What salvage treatment actually achieves".

Whose decision is this

The guideline written jointly by the EAU and five other societies puts it in its treatment chapter: the patient's own values and preferences must be incorporated, and shared decision-making includes what to expect of changes in quality of life[17].

This decision does not belong to urology and it does not belong to radiation oncology. It belongs to you — but you only hold the two sets of numbers you need after you have talked to both. If you have seen only one specialty, say so now; booking the other clinic is your right, not you being difficult.

Three questions get you further than most. For my risk group, how do the cancer control figures differ between the two? Given my current urinary, bowel and sexual function, which set of costs would I find harder to live with? And if I choose this side and it later fails, what is the next step? Write the answers down and think at home. Do not decide in the room.

From the physician

Which set of costs you can live with depends on your current urinary, bowel and sexual function, and nobody can weigh that for you. If you have seen only one specialty so far, book the other before you decide; bring back both sets of numbers and a written note of what each said about failure.
