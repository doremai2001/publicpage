THE DECISIONS THAT ARE YOURS

Treatment ended. Why am I more afraid?

One person in five reaches clinical level, and it does not fade with time. Where the peak sits, and which signals mean seeing someone.

Patient Education · The Decisions That Are Yours · 20 references
#Mood & Sleep#Recurrence#Quality of Life

One practical thing first: the days waiting for a result are worse than the day of the scan, so ask on the day when the report will be ready and who will tell you. And a counter-intuitive finding — the more frightened people are, the more likely they are to miss surveillance they should have.

On the day the course ends, a lot of people expect relief and do not get it. The gaps between appointments stretch out, the people around you start saying "you're better now", and you find yourself waking at three in the morning more often than before, counting days from the moment your hand finds a spot you would never previously have noticed. That is what this article is about: fear of cancer recurrence. I am not going to reassure you by saying your risk of recurrence is low, because that sentence is almost useless here, and the reason comes later.

58.8% and 19.2% are not the same thing

The most solid prevalence data we have is an individual participant data meta-analysis: raw data were requested from 87 studies, 46 supplied them, giving 11,226 participants across 13 countries, with 9,311 people in the main analysis. On the short form of the Fear of Cancer Recurrence Inventory (maximum 36), 58.8% scored 13 or above, 45.1% scored 16 or above, and 19.2% reached 22 or above; fear fell as age rose, was higher in women, and was present in every cancer type, on every continent, and at every time point after diagnosis[1].

Those two numbers get used interchangeably, and the difference between them is large. Thirteen and above means "measurable, some of it present"; 22 is the band approaching clinical level, and that one is a fifth of people, not most of them. Where does the cut-off of 22 come from? Two clinician-rated samples went through receiver operating characteristic analysis — 43% of one sample of 167 were rated as at clinical level, and 25% of another sample of 40 — and both pointed to a short-form score of 22 as identifying clinical-level fear with acceptable sensitivity and specificity[2].

Prevalence data for Taiwan I could not find. The closest Chinese-population data come from Hong Kong: 285 patients with breast or colorectal cancer answering eight weeks after surgery, of whom 26.0% fell in the subclinical band of 13 to 21 and 11.2% reached 22 or above[3]. Those are Hong Kong figures, not Taiwanese ones.

Why "your stage was early, there's nothing to worry about" does not help

I used to say that sentence too. What changed my wording was a review of 130 quantitative studies: survivors' self-reported fear is mostly low to moderate, yet they list it among their greatest concerns and it is the most frequently endorsed unmet need; and it stays stable across the whole survivorship trajectory rather than fading with time. The factors that correlate with the level of fear are younger age, having physical symptoms and having them more severely, higher psychological distress, and lower quality of life[4].

Stage is not on that list. Which is why offering risk figures to someone who is frightened is like answering a child afraid of the dark with the weather forecast — the information is correct, and it is answering a different question. It shows most clearly in younger patients: a Dutch adolescent and young adult cancer service surveyed 73 patients diagnosed between 18 and 35, and 62% fell into the high-fear group, above the 31% to 52% found in general mixed adult samples, and significantly associated with poorer social and psychological functioning and lower quality of life[5].

The days waiting for the result are harder than the day of the scan

Scanxiety deserves its own section, because the peak is usually placed in the wrong spot. A multicentre survey of 222 people with advanced cancer having surveillance CT found 55% had scan-related anxiety and 70% waited more than 2 days for results; among those with anxiety the mean severity was 6 out of 10, and the peak came while waiting for the result, not while having the scan. Those with anxiety were younger (62 against 66), and more of them lived further from the hospital[6].

Another study measured 83% reporting some degree of scan-associated distress in 103 patients with recurrent or metastatic lung cancer, with the level of distress significantly associated with impaired quality of life (P=.004); and the key point — it bore no relation to whether a scan had been done recently, whether the disease had progressed, or how long it had been since diagnosis. This is not an emotion that surfaces in the week of the scan; it is a continuous problem[7]. Worth adding that reported prevalence varies hugely between studies: a scoping review of 57 studies found 81 different measurement instruments in use in this area, a fifth of them designed by the investigators themselves[8].

Knowing where the peak sits makes the things you can change concrete: aim at shortening the wait and the uncertainty. Ask on the day of the scan when the report will be visible and who will tell you. Book the follow-up appointment for after the report is out. Put something that needs concentration into the waiting days. None of that removes the anxiety; it stops it growing unchecked.

Fear will not stand guard for you — it makes you miss the surveillance you should have

This is the section I most want people to keep. Among 218 women aged 18 to 45 with stage 0 to 2 breast cancer, those with higher levels of fear made more unscheduled visits to a general practitioner, examined themselves more frequently, and used more complementary therapies and counselling; but the same women were more likely to have gone the past year without a mammogram, an ultrasound or other indicated screening[9]. Healthcare use gets pulled in two directions at once: more of what is unnecessary, avoidance of what is needed. A systematic review of 11 studies also points to increased primary and secondary care use[10].

I am not going to suggest checking yourself more often for peace of mind, and I am not going to suggest bringing scans forward. Finish the surveillance as scheduled, and deal with the fear separately. Two lines, and they should not be cancelling each other out.

Things can be done, and I have to be straight about the effect size

A meta-analysis pooling 23 controlled trials, 21 of them randomised, plus 9 open trials, found psychological interventions had a small effect on fear of recurrence: standardised effect size g=0.33 (95% CI 0.20–0.46) at post-intervention, and g=0.28 at follow-up; the post-intervention effect of contemporary cognitive behavioural therapy (g=0.42) was larger than that of traditional cognitive behavioural therapy[11]. In plain terms: it works, and it is not a transformation.

Individual trials look better, provided you look at the premises. The Dutch SWORD trial enrolled 88 survivors scoring 14 or above on the Cancer Worry Scale, and blended cognitive behavioural therapy (5 face-to-face sessions plus 3 online) produced significantly lower fear scores at three months, effect size d=0.76 — d belongs to the same family as the g above, and the bigger the number the bigger the gap — putting it in the moderate-to-large range. But its control arm was usual care, not another active treatment, which inflates the effect size[12]. Australia's ConquerFear used a far stricter control, an active comparator in the form of relaxation therapy, and still came out significantly better than the control on total score and severity, holding to three and six months[13].

On the mind-body side, a meta-analysis of 19 randomised trials and 2,806 people gave a post-intervention g of −0.36, still significant at a median follow-up of 8 months[14]. An analysis of 16 randomised trials in breast cancer survivors has some breakdowns worth noticing: mindfulness-based and acceptance and commitment approaches reduced fear, while the "cognitive behavioural therapy plus education" combination did not; courses of 3 to 8 sessions worked and 9 or more did not; face-to-face worked and online did not — these are exploratory subgroup analyses and cannot be taken as general rules, but they do show that "talking to somebody a few times" is not a format-neutral prescription[15].

Exercise cuts both ways. The ERASE trial put 52 prostate cancer patients on active surveillance through 12 weeks of supervised high-intensity interval training, with significant improvement in total prostate-cancer-specific anxiety and P=.013 on the "fear of disease progression" subscale[16]; but in a dose-response trial of 39 colon cancer survivors, the physical domain, fatigue and sleep quality all improved with the amount of exercise, while the fear of recurrence scale showed no dose response at all[17]. There are many reasons to exercise; reducing this particular fear is not the one we are most sure of. How to judge intensity and when to stop first is in "Move, or rest, during treatment".

What level means seeing someone, and who to see in Taiwan

That cut-off of 22 I would use like this: not as a self-diagnostic tool, but as a reference point you can take to the team. What works better in practice is a handful of everyday signals — losing sleep over this persistently, starting to avoid appointments or scans, being unable to work or look after your family, the same scene replaying in your head and not stopping. With those present, it is not "overthinking". It is something that can be treated.

One misreading to block first. What the Hong Kong study found associated with fear were metacognitive beliefs about worry, and neuroticism[3], which is easily read as "so it is my personality". Those are habits of thinking that treatment can change, not defects of character.

The routes available in Taiwan: start with your case manager (個管師) and ask whether the hospital has a psychiatry or clinical/counselling psychology referral. Whether National Health Insurance (健保) covers this indication, I could not find the rule — check with your case manager or the hospital medical affairs office. Outside the hospital there is one free channel: the HOPE Foundation for Cancer Care (癌症希望基金會) provides up to 6 individual counselling sessions per person (50 minutes each) or up to 4 couple or family sessions (75 minutes each), open to patients and family members, and the topics listed on its site include persistent anxiety and stress and a sense of hopelessness about life; the free line is 0809-010-580, with centres in Taipei, Taichung and Kaohsiung[18]. When the middle of the night is more than you can hold, the Ministry of Health and Welfare's 1925 helpline (依舊愛我) is 24 hours and toll-free[19], with Lifeline 1995 and Teacher Chang 1980 alongside it[20]. Mood and sleep get the full treatment, including when a psychiatric referral is warranted, in "The kind of sleeplessness that won't lift".

The question to ask at your next appointment is short: when will this report be ready, and who will tell me? Turning the wait into a number with an answer is more use than anything reassuring I could say.

From the physician

Whether you have just finished treatment or been in follow-up for years, and whether you still have symptoms, changes what this weighs; the cut-off of 22 is not for diagnosing yourself. If it is keeping you awake or you have started avoiding appointments, say that sentence out loud at your next visit and ask your case manager to assess a psychiatry or psychology referral.
