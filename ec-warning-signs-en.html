DURING TREATMENT

When to come back the same day

Fistula, perforation, bleeding, febrile neutropenia, dehydration, complete inability to swallow — for each one, what you see, what it may be, and why it means today rather than the next appointment.

Oesophageal Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 17 references
#Urgent#Side Effects#Daily Life

Several complications of oesophageal cancer treatment are counted in hours: a perforation treated within 24 hours against one left past a day carries a mortality that differs more than two-fold; the endoscopy for a complete obstruction has to happen within two to six hours. This article is for you and your family to print out and stick on the fridge.

"Should I go back for this, or wait for next week's appointment?" During oesophageal cancer treatment, this is the question I least want a patient dithering over at home, because this disease has a few complications that are counted in hours. I do not write "seek medical attention if you feel unwell". Each warning sign is told in three parts: what you see, what it may be, and why it means coming back the same day rather than waiting for the clinic — and each one comes with its source. Please print this out.

Choking on water, coughing after every meal: this is not "swallowing wrong", it may be a fistula

The oesophagus and the windpipe are two tubes lying against each other. Once treatment has destroyed the tumour, a hole can open between them: a tracheo-oesophageal fistula. Water you drink leaks from the oesophagus into the airway, so you choke every time you drink, cough violently after eating, and get repeated pneumonia. It is more common than most people think: in the follow-up analysis of Japan's JCOG0303 trial (squamous cell carcinoma with T4 disease or unresectable nodes, concurrent chemoradiotherapy, 140 patients), 22% developed a fistula, with a median onset 100 days after chemoradiotherapy began (interquartile range 45 to 171 days), and 6 patients developed one during treatment itself; the only independent risk factor was oesophageal stricture (odds ratio 2.59 — just read it as "clearly higher"), not T stage as such[1]. In Shizuoka's data on 116 T4b patients the figure was 24%; median survival was 8.0 months in those with a fistula against 26.8 months in those without[2]. So two things to remember: first, it does not only happen during treatment — it can happen for months after treatment ends; second, the tighter the stricture the higher the risk, and it is not only T4 disease that gets it. The reason to come back the same day is that what leaks into the lung causes pneumonia, and the earlier a fistula is assessed, the more options there are for dealing with it (a stent to seal it, surgery).

Sudden severe chest or back pain, fever, a swollen neck: a perforation is counted in hours

The classic picture of an oesophageal perforation is sudden severe chest or back pain, followed by fever and a fast heartbeat, sometimes with a bubbly feeling under the skin of the neck or chest (subcutaneous emphysema). A meta-analysis of 75 studies and 2,971 patients with perforation: pooled mortality 11.9%; mortality 7.4% when treated within 24 hours, 20.3% when it went beyond a day[3]. In a study of 194 perforation patients from nine centres, oesophageal malignancy was an independent predictor of early death[4] — these pooled data are mostly non-cancer perforations and highly heterogeneous, but the direction is clear: go past a day and mortality goes from 7% to 20%, a difference of more than two-fold. CTCAE defines grade 4 oesophageal perforation as "urgent operative intervention indicated"[5]. One extra reminder for anyone with a stent: stent placement can itself perforate the tumour — 2 of 38 patients in the French cohort[6].

Vomiting blood, black stools, a large amount of fresh blood: do not wait to see whether it stops

Bleeding during treatment may come from the tumour itself, from an ulcer after radiotherapy, or from the edge of a stent. In the Dutch randomised trial (209 inoperable patients, stent against single-dose brachytherapy), late bleeding was 13% in the stent group and 5% in the brachytherapy group[7]. The worst kind is an aorto-oesophageal fistula — the tumour or the stent eroding into the great artery alongside — which starts with a small "herald bleed" of vomited blood or black stool and is followed by massive haemorrhage; the data on this are only at case-report level, so I give no proportion, but the case-report authors' own words are that without prompt treatment "mortality approaches 100%"[8]. So vomiting fresh or coffee-coloured blood, passing black stools, or bleeding enough to make you dizzy — this is ambulance territory, not tomorrow's clinic. CTCAE defines grade 3 oesophageal haemorrhage as needing transfusion, an invasive procedure or hospitalisation[5].

Fever: one reading of 38.0°C, and you go

Chemotherapy knocks down your white cells, and a fever while the white cells are low is not a minor cold — it is an infection that can worsen within hours. The formal definition first: the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) 2010 guideline defines it as "a single oral temperature of ≥38.3°C, or ≥38.0°C sustained over one hour", and requires every patient with fever and neutropenia to start empirical antibiotics within two hours of arrival[9]; the 2018 ASCO and IDSA update tightened that to within one hour of triage[10]. CTCAE's definition of febrile neutropenia uses the same two thresholds[5]. That is the formal definition; but the rule I give you for home use is the same as this site's "The fever that cannot wait until morning", and it is deliberately set more conservatively: during chemotherapy, one reading of 38.0°C on an ear or axillary thermometer — go. Do not wait for it to persist for an hour — and taking an antipyretic at home before measuring only covers up the signal.

Your prescription determines how high this risk is. In China's NEOCRTEC5010 trial using vinorelbine plus cisplatin (squamous cell carcinoma, 451 patients), grade 3 to 4 neutropenia during chemoradiotherapy was 45.7%[11]; in the Dutch CROSS trial using carboplatin plus paclitaxel (366 patients, three-quarters adenocarcinoma), major neutropenia was only 2%[12]. Two different trials, two different populations — the numbers can only be read for direction, but the direction differs by an order of magnitude. Ask exactly which regimen you are on, and on which day after the infusion your white cells usually hit their lowest. The general handling of fever and the question of antipyretics have already been covered on this site in "The fever that cannot wait until morning"; here I cover only what is specific to oesophageal cancer: fever together with a sudden worsening of swallowing pain means thinking about perforation and candida as well as infection; and for someone who cannot swallow tablets, the very route for giving antipyretics and antibiotics is a problem — another reason to come back rather than manage it at home.

These chemotherapy drugs each have their own signals

The two regimens most used in oesophageal cancer are cisplatin plus 5-FU, and carboplatin plus paclitaxel. What follows is what each drug's prescribing information says, and what I would like you to be able to recognise.

  • Cisplatin's kidney damage accumulates dose by dose: less urine, darker urine, thirst but unable to drink — these are the signals of the kidney and of dehydration. This is also why, before every cisplatin dose, the hospital runs intravenous fluids for several hours and takes blood for kidney function and electrolytes — that is the team's procedure, not an instruction for you to drink more at home. Ototoxicity is cumulative too: after a single dose, up to 31% of patients develop tinnitus or high-frequency hearing loss — report any new ringing in the ears, do not treat it as minor[13].

  • 5-FU causes mucositis of the mouth, throat and oesophagus, which can progress to sloughing and ulceration; it also causes severe diarrhoea and red, swollen, peeling palms and soles (hand-foot syndrome); the prescribing information separately lists cardiotoxicity — chest tightness, chest pain, arrhythmia — with higher risk on continuous infusion and in people with pre-existing coronary artery disease. The boxed warning is that people completely lacking the DPD enzyme can have severe, even fatal, reactions[14]. For the oesophageal cancer patient there is one more layer: 5-FU's mucositis and radiation oesophagitis land on the same oesophagus, so your swallowing pain may be worse than other people's, and come earlier.

  • Paclitaxel causes severe hypersensitivity reactions in 2% to 4% of patients, with fatal cases even with premedication, so breathlessness, a drop in blood pressure, flushing all over or a rash during the infusion means calling the nurse at once. The numbness of hands and feet that builds up over the course is peripheral neuropathy, and when severe the dose has to be reduced[15].

  • Carboplatin allergy is about 2%; anaphylactoid reactions can occur within minutes, and the risk is higher in people who have had platinum drugs before. It drops the platelets more markedly than cisplatin, so bleeding from the gums that will not stop when you brush, or small bleeding spots on the skin, need reporting[16].

Two situations that do not look urgent but mean coming back the same day

The first is dehydration. Oesophagitis stops you drinking, chemotherapy makes you vomit and gives you diarrhoea; stack the three together and two days is enough to leave you dehydrated to the point of being unsteady on your feet. Dizzy on standing, passing urine fewer than three times a day and dark, cracked lips — the problem at this point is not "drink more", because you cannot get it down; CTCAE's very definition of grade 3 oesophagitis includes the need for tube feeding, intravenous nutrition or hospitalisation[5], and the cisplatin prescribing information also lists dehydration as a condition to watch for[13]. Coming back for a day on a drip is far better than holding out at home for three days and then ending up in the emergency department.

The second is suddenly being completely unable to swallow, not even your own saliva. This is usually a piece of food lodged at the stricture (food bolus impaction). The European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy's 2016 guideline is firm: for a foreign body causing complete oesophageal obstruction, therapeutic endoscopy should be "emergent, preferably within 2 hours, at the latest within 6 hours"; without complete obstruction it should still be within 24 hours; and drug treatment must not delay the endoscopy[17]. The reason is that whatever is stuck is pressing on the oesophageal wall, and the longer it presses the more likely it is to press through into a perforation. Drinking cola at home, making yourself vomit, digging with a finger — do none of these.

The sheet of paper to write today and stick on the fridge

Everything above is judgement; this part is preparation. Today, write one sheet of paper and stick it on the fridge, with four things on it: your case manager's phone number and the hospital emergency department's number; the name of your chemotherapy regimen (which two drugs, given on which day); where the thermometer is kept; and this one line — "one reading of 38.0 — go; choking on water; sudden severe chest or back pain; vomiting blood or black stools; completely unable to swallow: come back the same day". Your family must be able to read this sheet too, because when something really happens, the person making the call is often not you. When you come back, bring this sheet and your medication list; it will save the emergency doctor a great many questions.

From the physician

This article is not meant to frighten you; it is meant to take the hesitation over "should I go back" out of your home. Today, write your case manager's number, the emergency department's number and the names of your chemotherapy drugs on one sheet of paper, in a form your family can read. During chemotherapy, one ear or axillary reading of 38.0 means you go — do not wait for it to persist, and do not take an antipyretic before measuring.
