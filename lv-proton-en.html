Protons for liver cancer: the upgrade and the trials

The liver is a mean-dose organ and protons have no exit dose — the mechanism holds especially well in the liver. How far the trials have got, and what published prices exist for fully self-paid adult treatment, are all laid out here.

Liver Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 16 references
#Proton Therapy#SBRT#Costs & Self-Pay#Clinical Trials

A 144-patient randomised trial proved proton local control non-inferior to ablation, with less liver-function deterioration; the photon-versus-proton randomised trial has completed accrual and is unpublished. So it is three sentences: there is reason to expect, there is one non-inferiority randomised trial, superiority is being tested — and not one word more on any of them.

"Are protons better?" In the liver cancer clinic my answer is always the same three sentences: there is reason to expect so; there is one non-inferiority randomised trial; superiority is being tested now. This article unpacks what sits behind those three sentences. The physics of protons and Taiwan's regulatory position I have written up in full in two articles on this site — "Protons: precise. Then what?" and "Is proton therapy actually better?" — I am not rewriting them here; this is the liver question only.

My position first: stereotactic body radiotherapy and proton therapy for liver cancer are treatments I deliver myself — and much of it is self-paid. The other side of every comparison in this topic is my colleagues' surgery and ablation. That is why every comparison comes with its primary source, so you can take it to the surgical and hepatology clinics for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

The foundation first: photon SBRT is already very good

Before any talk of an upgrade, the base model has to stand — that is not politeness; it is the foundation of the proton argument. Photon SBRT holds a formal reimbursed position in liver cancer (code 37047B: single lesion, within five centimetres, Child-Pugh A to B, unsuitable for or having failed surgery, embolisation or ablation)[1]; its prospective-trial results in small tumours are very good: in a population within five centimetres (median 1.3 cm), not amenable to curative treatment, two-year local control was 100% and five-year 97.1%[2]; pooled analysis of primary liver tumours gives two-year local control of 89%[3]; and in advanced liver cancer, adding SBRT has a progression-free survival improvement read out of a randomised trial[4]. The full context of those numbers is in "Why SBRT works in the liver, and when it is the wrong answer", and the division of the three roads is in "Three roads for a small liver cancer" — neither comparison is re-run here. If photons already solve your box of the problem well, then protons are an engineering upgrade of the same treatment — the same treatment, a better dose distribution — and the value of the upgrade has to stand on what follows.

Why the liver, of all organs: a mean-dose organ meets a beam with no exit

The liver's dose logic is "mean dose and volume decide" — what determines liver injury is not the highest-dose point but how much the liver as a whole has taken[5]. Proton physics happens to answer exactly that point: a proton releases its energy at the end of its range (the Bragg peak) and stops, with almost no exit dose[6] — what it saves is precisely the low-dose bath that photons scatter across the normal liver after passing through the tumour. In most organs the low-dose bath is a secondary issue; in the liver it converts directly into liver function. A study that used a neural-network model to analyse liver cancer patients' dose distributions and post-treatment liver function (117 patients for modelling, 88 for external validation) named this in its conclusion: the low-dose bath, and its interaction with poor baseline liver function, is the key predictor of liver-function deterioration[7]. At the level of mechanism, the case for protons is harder in the liver than in most parts of the body.

What the randomised trial proved is "not inferior" — not "superior"

Korea's National Cancer Center ran a phase III randomised trial of protons against RFA, enrolling recurrent or residual liver cancers under three centimetres, up to two lesions, 144 patients in all. It was a non-inferiority design — the question asked in advance was "are protons worse than RFA by more than 15 percentage points", not "which is better". Non-inferiority was met: for two-year local progression-free survival, the per-protocol analysis gave protons 94.8% against RFA's 83.9% (a difference of 10.9 percentage points, 90% confidence interval 1.8 to 20.0 — a confidence interval is the range within which the estimate can reasonably swing), and the intention-to-treat analysis 92.8% against 83.2% (a difference of 9.6 percentage points, 90% confidence interval 0.7 to 18.4)[8]. The numbers lean towards protons — even the lower bounds of the confidence intervals sit above zero; but the question the trial answered was non-inferiority, so what it proved is non-inferiority — writing "protons are superior to RFA" would be overreach, and I do not write it. There is one detail of this trial I particularly want to point out: 19 patients randomised to RFA crossed over to protons because RFA was technically not feasible, against only 6 in the opposite direction[8] — "positions RFA cannot reach" is not an abstraction; it happened, concretely, inside a randomised trial. AASLD 2023, recounting this trial, added one more number: the proportion whose Child-Turcotte-Pugh score rose after treatment was 7.5% with protons against 19.6% with ablation[9].

Which way the retrospective data point, and the labels they must wear

Photons against protons, there are only retrospective comparisons so far. Massachusetts General Hospital, 133 unresectable liver cancers (49 of them treated with protons): protons were associated with better survival — adjusted hazard ratio 0.47 (a hazard ratio divides the speed at which deaths occur in one group by the speed in the other), median survival 31 against 14 months — and with less non-classic radiation-induced liver disease; local recurrence showed no difference, and the authors' own reading was that the survival difference may come from less hepatic decompensation[10]. Retrospective, with selection bias that cannot be excluded — that label cannot be dropped. Prospective single-arm data exist in the Japanese multicentre registry of 576 liver cancer patients receiving protons for the first time: median survival 48.8 months, three-year local control 90%, grade 3 or higher late adverse events 4.7%; survival was significantly associated with tumour size, Child-Pugh class and the tumour's distance from the gastrointestinal tract[11] — protons do not escape the "liver function is the currency" rule either. Another Japanese propensity-score-matched comparison (treatment-naïve, single tumours within five centimetres, Child-Pugh score up to 8; 83 patients each for protons and RFA): in five-year survival, no difference could be seen[12]. Taiwan has a series of its own: in the Linkou Chang Gung system, 83 patients with portal vein tumour thrombus received intensity-modulated proton therapy — median survival 32.4 months, two-year local control 88.8%, radiation-induced liver disease 9.3% — again, retrospective data[13].

The head-to-head trial has finished enrolling, and has not read out

There is exactly one randomised trial of photons against protons: NRG-GI003, phase III, primary endpoint overall survival. As of my verification date, 30 August 2026, it has completed accrual (115 patients, primary completion date 20 January 2026) and has no public results[14]. Superiority is being tested — until it reads out, "protons are better than photons" has no randomised evidence to cite in liver cancer, and so I do not say it.

Cost: fully self-paid for adults — everything I could find is here

From January 2026 (ROC year 115) the NHI added three reimbursed proton radiotherapy items, but every one of them is limited to "patients under nineteen years of age"[15] — proton therapy for liver cancer in adults is fully self-paid. Only one officially published price could be found: the fee schedule announced by the Kaohsiung Chang Gung proton therapy centre — intensity-modulated protons at NT$21,750 to NT$34,500 per fraction, respiratory management an additional NT$5,000 or NT$9,600 per fraction, plus a "proton stereotactic body radiotherapy (bundle)" at NT$330,000, alongside charges for moulds, simulation imaging and treatment planning[16]. Two things must be labelled plainly: this is that hospital's published bundle, not liver-specific — the total for a liver course depends on the number of fractions and on respiratory management, and differs between hospitals; and no other hospital has a citable official published price, so ask the medical affairs office directly. One further fact belongs on the same table: photon SBRT is reimbursed by the NHI[1]; protons for adults are fully self-paid. Whether to pay this money for a gap that stands at "non-inferiority met, superiority still being tested" — the answer lives in your lesion's position, your liver function and your family's financial reality, not in my article.

Before you ask about protons, have these three things ready

One, the imaging: whether your tumour touches vessels, how far it sits from the gastrointestinal tract, whether RFA can actually reach it — the 19 patients in the Korean trial who could not have RFA were stuck on exactly this question. Two, liver function: the components of the Child-Pugh score (see "You have two diseases: the cancer and the cirrhosis") — the tighter the liver function, the more the saved low-dose bath is worth; but at a score of 8 or above, external beam radiotherapy itself should be avoided[9]. Three, a photon treatment plan or assessment: have your radiation oncologist answer "can a photon plan keep the mean normal-liver dose down?" first, and only then discuss where the dose protons save would land. Then take the references at the end of this article — the Korean trial, GI003's current status — to a second opinion; follow the hear-all-three-specialties routine recommended in "Three roads for a small liver cancer", and if you meet transplant criteria, add the transplant assessment. However much of an upgrade protons are, they remain an engineering refinement of the "radiation" road among the three — not a fourth road.

From the physician

Photon SBRT is NHI-reimbursed and its results are already very good; protons are an engineering upgrade of the same road, fully self-paid. Before asking about protons, first have your radiation oncologist answer whether a photon plan can keep the liver dose down, then take the Korean trial's paper to a second opinion.
