Why do mothers who never smoked get lung cancer?

Secondhand smoke is Group 1 and cooking fumes are Group 2A, yet Taiwan's largest prospective cohort saw neither; turning the multiples into absolute numbers changes the sense of proportion.

Carcinogens at work and at home · At Home · 19 references
#At Home#Indoor Air#Lung#Screening#Prevention

Smoking prevalence in Taiwanese women has been under 5% for forty years, and their lung adenocarcinoma incidence still runs higher than men's of the same age. There will not be one cause; and once “risk up 1.5-fold” is rewritten as “2.5% becomes 3.7%”, the decision in front of you is a different one.

"My mother never smoked a day in her life. How did she get lung cancer?" Almost everyone who asks turns it seconds later into a question about themselves: was it the thirty years at the wok? This piece answers that, including the part many will not like — Taiwan's largest prospective study did not see the cooking fumes.

How common is lung adenocarcinoma in never-smoking women in Taiwan?

Start with the denominator. Taiwan recorded 138,051 new cancers in 2023, lung cancer first by case numbers among the ten commonest[1]; smoking prevalence in Taiwanese women has stayed below 5% since 1980[2]. Those two together are the whole problem.

From 2011 to 2019 data the National Health Research Institutes analysed 61,285 first diagnoses of invasive lung adenocarcinoma aged 40 to 84, 32,599 (53.2%) of them women. Incidence in never-smoking women climbs steeply with age: 51.97 per 100,000 person-years at 60 to 64, 99.53 at 70 to 74, 151.32 at 80 to 84. Harder to explain, women exceeded men at almost every age band regardless of smoking — among never-smokers aged 70 to 74 the female-to-male incidence rate ratio for advanced disease was 1.38 (95% confidence interval 1.30 to 1.50, the plausible range for the estimate, wholly above 1, ruling out "men and women are the same")[3].

So the question is not whether women are higher but why, and the answer at present is several threads together, none carrying the whole load.

"Secondhand smoke raises the risk 1.5-fold" — in absolute numbers, what is that?

Not in dispute: the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) finds sufficient evidence that involuntary smoking causes lung cancer in humans, Group 1[4]. Group 1 describes how certain the evidence is, not how dangerous the agent is — cooking fumes below are read on the same ruler, and "Group 1 is not a danger ranking" and the entry page "Carcinogens at work and at home" cover it. Secondhand smoke is a smoker's exhalation plus what burns off the cigarette. Monograph 100E pools it: never-smoking women with a smoking spouse, relative risk 1.27 (95% confidence interval 1.17 to 1.37, 55 studies); workplace 1.24 (1.18 to 1.29, 22 studies); heavily exposed workers 2.01 (1.33 to 2.60)[5].

A Taiwanese case-control study of 1,024 pairs of never-smoking women gave odds ratios of 1.49 (1.24 to 1.80) for household secondhand smoke, 1.41 (1.11 to 1.81) for workplace[6]. An odds ratio is not a multiple of probability, so the same paper converted to absolute risk: the average probability a never-smoking Taiwanese woman develops lung adenocarcinoma over the 40 years from age 40 is 2.5% unexposed, 3.7% with exposure at home or at work, 5.3% with both[6].

What is added is not "one and a half times the dread" but 1.2 to 2.8 percentage points. The same study built a polygenic risk score from 25 genetic loci: lifetime risk 1.0% in the lowest tenth, 10.7% in the highest[6]. Constitution and secondhand smoke combined carry a risk far higher than either alone, and the direction here is more than additive — a poor genetic score plus exposure gains more than the two separate increases added together[6]. The spread from constitution is wider, but constitution cannot be changed and the cigarette in the house can.

Household secondhand smoke exposure in Taiwan was 35.6% in 2024, up from 28.9% in 2022[7]. In legally smoke-free public places it is very low, 4.0% in 2022, while outdoor public places ran to 42.9%[8].

How far has the evidence on cooking fumes actually got?

IARC Monograph 95 classifies emissions from high-temperature frying as Group 2A, probably carcinogenic to humans. Two things: it is not Group 1; and within that evaluation IARC labelled the human evidence limited, the sufficient evidence coming from animals (unrefined rapeseed oil)[9].

The mechanism is not contested. Rapeseed, corn and peanut oil heated above 260°C yield measurable genotoxic polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons; the lipid peroxidation product trans,trans-2,4-decadienal is described by IARC as one of the most abundant and most potent mutagens in cooking fumes, alongside 1,3-butadiene, benzene, acetaldehyde and acrolein. Mutagenic activity rises with temperature from 230 to 280°C, and oils high in unsaturated fatty acids produce more mutagenic emissions than hydrogenated oils[10].

Epidemiologically, a meta-analysis in never-smoking Chinese women pooled 13 studies, 3,596 cases and 6,082 controls: odds ratio for cooking fume exposure 1.74 (1.57 to 1.94), and 2.11 (1.54 to 2.89) without an extractor hood — case-control studies yield odds ratios, and reading that as "1.74 times the risk" overstates it[11]. Taiwan's own 1,302-pair study used cooking time-years as cumulative exposure and found a dose-response: odds ratios from lowest to highest group 1.63 (1.20 to 2.23), 1.67 (1.12 to 2.49), 2.14 (1.19 to 3.90) and 3.17 (1.34 to 7.68)[12].

Taiwan's largest prospective study saw neither of them

Those are all case-control studies: find the patients, then ask how many years they spent cooking. That design is most vulnerable to recall bias — people already ill think harder about past exposure than people who are not.

TALENT is a prospective cohort of 12,011 never-smokers or very light smokers aged 55 to 75 at 17 Taiwanese medical centres. The factors significantly associated with lung cancer risk in multivariable analysis were female sex, family history of lung cancer, and age over 60; secondhand smoke exposure, cumulative cooking exposure, cooking without ventilation equipment and previous chronic lung disease all failed to reach statistical significance, and stratifying by family history did not change that[13].

The two sides disagree and I will hide neither. Cooking fumes have clear chemical and animal evidence and a consistent dose-response in case-control studies; Taiwan's largest and least recall-biased prospective data did not reproduce it, and the strongest factor in that dataset was family history. Back on the absolute scale: the 2.5% to 5.3% belongs to secondhand smoke; there is no matching absolute risk estimate for cooking fumes, and no attributable fraction for cooking fumes in Taiwan that I can cite — worth saying in itself. That scale settles one thing: the overwhelming majority of people who cook for thirty years will not get lung cancer from it.

Thirdhand smoke: the residue is real, the human cancer evidence is not

Thirdhand smoke is the residue left on curtains, sofas, clothing and walls after the cigarette is out. It contains carcinogens, exposure biomarkers are detectable in people, animal models show damage. But a 2023 review says it directly: the harms of secondhand and thirdhand smoke are not equally well documented, and no epidemiological study currently demonstrates that thirdhand smoke causes cancer in humans[14]. So the phrasing is "worth reducing, but the human cancer evidence does not exist", not "thirdhand smoke causes cancer" — changing your jacket before picking up a grandchild is reasonable, calling it an imminent cancer threat is not.

How much does an extractor hood give back? About half

The 1,302-pair study measured the protective side too: long-term extractor hood use gave an adjusted odds ratio of 0.49 (0.32 to 0.76), roughly halving the risk, and over a 60% reduction in the highest cumulative exposure group (0.37, 0.16 to 0.83). Frequent stir-frying (1.53) and lard (1.92) were each independently associated with raised risk[12]. Halved is not zero: a hood overhead is not permission to take the oil to smoking point.

  • That 0.49 belongs to long-term use, not switching it on when you remember[12].

  • Shift the proportion of stir-frying down, towards steaming, boiling, braising and water-frying; frequent stir-frying is an independent risk factor[12].

  • Oil beginning to smoke means the temperature is too high; mutagenic activity climbs between 230 and 280°C[10].

For "run it a few more minutes afterwards" or "open the window this far" I found no quantitative research and will not invent numbers. Ventilation and indoor air quality are covered in "How much should the formaldehyde in a new home worry you?".

Wider LDCT eligibility does not mean everyone should go

Publicly funded low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) lung cancer screening began on 1 July 2022[15]. From 1 January 2025 eligibility widened into two groups: a family history group (parent, child or sibling diagnosed with lung cancer), men 45 to 74 and women 40 to 74; and a heavy smoking group, 50 to 74 with 20 pack-years or more, including current smokers and those who quit under 15 years ago included; once every 2 years[16]. By 30 June 2024, 114,445 people had been screened and 1,401 lung cancers found, 83.2% at stage 0 or 1[17].

Outside the criteria, paying for one is not "more screening, more peace of mind". An analysis of 57,898 women with lung cancer in the Taiwan Cancer Registry, 2004 to 2018, found that after LDCT was promoted early-stage (0 to I) incidence rose from 2.3 to 14.4 per 100,000 while late-stage (II to IV) barely moved (18.7 to 19.3, absolute difference 0.6, 95% confidence interval −0.5 to 1.7); the extra early cancers bought no reduction in late-stage disease, and the authors concluded almost all the additional cancers were overdiagnosis[2]. TALENT notes possible overdiagnosis too, particularly among its 61 adenocarcinomas in situ[13]. What overdiagnosis is, and why better survival does not mean screening worked, is the subject of "When screening finds too much".

Deal with the cigarette before the wok

First: if someone in the house still smokes, deal with the cigarette, not the person. Under the 2023 amendment to the Tobacco Hazards Prevention Act, Article 18 makes indoor workplaces shared by three or more people entirely smoke-free and Article 20 prohibits smoking indoors where a pregnant woman or a child under three is present[18] — the law has said it for you, so you need not be the awkward one. For someone who wants to stop, the formal entry point is the free quitline on 0800-636363, Monday to Saturday 9am to 9pm, voicemail outside those hours, and the official LINE account @tsh0800636363[19]. For subsidy amounts and cessation clinic reimbursement conditions I found no current version I can cite: ask the quitline or your nearest cessation clinic rather than calculating from figures circulating online.

Second, treat the hood as something switched on every day, and take the stir-frying down a notch. It is the one kitchen intervention with Taiwanese numbers behind it[12].

Third, pin down the family history. Whether a parent, child or sibling has had lung cancer decides whether you qualify for publicly funded LDCT[16], and it was the strongest factor in TALENT's multivariable analysis[13]. That one does not wait on a screening centre; one round of the table at new year gets the answer.

From the physician

I will not decide for you whether to stir-fry less often, but two numbers are worth taking away: 2.5% and 5.3%. The gap between them is real, and it is genuinely not wide enough to explain your mother's illness. What is worth the effort is the cigarette in the house, and getting the family history straight.
