WHAT NOBODY ASKS IN CLINIC

Some options close when treatment starts

Freezing does not guarantee a child (43% cumulative live birth), an injection is no substitute, and the subsidy covers only two groups.

Patient Education · What Nobody Asks In Clinic · 15 references
#Fertility#Getting Ready#Decisions

Ask before you decide. The assessment runs alongside your pre-treatment work-up rather than delaying it, and the honest figures — what freezing actually returns, what a GnRH agonist can and cannot do, who the subsidy reaches — are all here before you have to choose.

What makes this piece different from the others is that it expires. Fertility preservation has to be dealt with before anticancer treatment begins; once treatment has started there is much less that can be done, and some options disappear outright. So even if your head is full of stage and schedule, this one is worth getting out at your next clinic visit. Asking and then deciding against it is your right. Missing it because nobody mentioned it is just a miss.

Why "before it starts", and not "once treatment has settled down"

The wording of the 2018 ASCO fertility preservation guideline update, which reviewed 61 papers from 2013 to 2017, is fairly hard: clinicians should discuss the possibility of infertility "as early as possible" with patients of reproductive age, or with the parents of paediatric patients, and be prepared to discuss preservation options or refer to a reproductive specialist — and "the discussion should be documented". Sperm, oocyte and embryo cryopreservation are standard practice and already widely available[1].

The other reason not to wait gets said less often: the cancer itself may have moved first. Across 15 years at one Korean centre, 66 patients referred for sperm cryopreservation before chemotherapy already showed significant differences in sperm concentration and motility between cancer types, and the authors concluded that sperm quality may fall before chemotherapy starts[2]. The earlier the assessment, the more there is left to keep.

The opposite mistake needs guarding against too: this is not "go and freeze first, push the treatment back". A fertility preservation assessment runs in parallel with the other pre-treatment work-up, and the timing is set by oncology and reproductive medicine together; the treatment timetable is itself part of whether treatment works. One more thing to put here at the start: once your course has begun, a single ear or axillary reading of 38.0°C or above, shaking chills, sudden breathlessness or chest pain, or swelling and pain in one calf all mean the emergency department the same day — a separate matter from this piece's deadline, but equally not something to wait on.

"Chemotherapy makes you infertile anyway" — first find out which class your regimen is in

How much harm is done varies enormously by regimen, and the Hodgkin lymphoma data are cleanest. Alkylating regimens containing procarbazine or cyclophosphamide cause long-term azoospermia in 90 to 100% of men, and premature ovarian failure in 5 to 25% of women under 30; non-alkylating regimens of the ABVD type carry an infertility rate below 10%[3]. Another review pointing the same way adds detail: about a third of men develop temporary azoospermia, and for women the decisive variable is age — the risk of acute ovarian failure is much higher over 30[4].

So "chemotherapy is bound to make me infertile" does not hold, and neither does "chemotherapy probably won't affect me". The thing to ask is specific: is there an alkylating agent in my regimen, and at my age, roughly what level of expected impact does that put me at? If your field includes the pelvis, that is a different set of risks, covered in more detail in the rectal cancer guide's "Fertility questions have no next month"; ovarian transposition has a piece of its own in the research-update column.

For men the bar is far lower, and what usually blocks it is that nobody said anything

Freezing sperm involves no waiting time for ovarian stimulation and the procedure is simple. The Health Promotion Administration (國民健康署) subsidy caps at NT$8,000 per occasion, far below the NT$70,000 for a woman's oocyte retrieval cycle[5]. For male readers "too late" barely applies; the real obstacle is that nobody opens the subject.

A German centre's testicular cancer questionnaire (279 respondents, mean follow-up 5.7 years) gives a grounded set of numbers: 33% froze sperm before treatment, and of those only 11% later actually used it for assisted reproduction; of the 188 who did not freeze, only 2% (3 men) reported an unmet wish for a child[6]. Most of what gets stored is never used, and that is not a failure; not storing does not necessarily mean regret. The point was never "you ought to do it". It is that you have to know it exists before treatment starts.

Does freezing get you a child? Cumulative live birth 43%

This is the section I least want skipped. Thaw data from a US university centre show an overall cumulative live birth or ongoing pregnancy rate of 43% for planned oocyte cryopreservation; the cell above 70% has as its precondition having frozen young and having 20 or more mature oocytes at thaw, and increasing the number of freezing cycles does not independently raise the live birth rate[7]. That population includes non-medical egg freezing and is not cancer-specific, but the direction is clear.

What mainly decides the outcome is age at freezing. An analysis of 3,164 women and 4,577 oocyte freezing cycles in Melbourne, 2014 to 2023: the cancer group had the lowest mean age at freezing (31.3 years), below other medical indications (34.4) and non-medical indications (36.2); age at freezing significantly affected the cumulative live birth rate per thaw cycle, while differences between indications were not significant[8].

Freezing keeps a chance. It does not keep a child. Say that clearly first, so that the decisions built on top of it are not built on a misunderstanding.

Can an injection to protect the ovaries take the place of freezing?

No, and ASCO says so plainly. The guideline's position is that the evidence for GnRH agonists and other forms of ovarian suppression is conflicting, that they may be offered only where proven preservation methods are not feasible and to young women with breast cancer, and that they must not be used in place of proven fertility preservation methods[1].

They are not without effect. An individual patient data meta-analysis of 5 trials and 873 patients with early breast cancer: premature ovarian insufficiency 14.1% against 30.9% (adjusted odds ratio 0.38; an odds ratio divides one group's odds of the event happening against not happening by the other's, and is not a multiple of probability, interval 0.26–0.57), pregnancy after treatment 10.3% against 5.5%, and no significant difference between the groups in disease-free or overall survival[9]. The endpoints are ovarian function and number of pregnancies, not "fertility was preserved". Its reasonable place: it can be added, it cannot be swapped in for freezing.

Ovarian tissue freezing: who it particularly helps, and its one limitation

Its value is highest for people who must start treatment immediately and cannot fit in ovarian stimulation, and for prepubertal children. A meta-analysis of 23 studies shows haematological malignancies and breast cancer together accounting for about 66% of those undergoing ovarian tissue cryopreservation, gynaecological cancers only 7.5%; after autotransplantation, 78.1% recovered ovarian endocrine function, median graft survival 32 months[10].

Its limitation has to be written out as it stands. A 2026 safety review states that the largest concern with ovarian tissue cryopreservation and transplantation is the possibility of carrying surviving malignant cells back into the body with the transplanted tissue, so minimal residual disease detection and tumour cell purging in the frozen tissue are preconditions for clinical safety; and that long-term health follow-up data for children born after transplantation remain insufficient[11]. Whether it suits you is tied directly to which cancer you have and at what stage, and that is a judgement for reproductive medicine and oncology together.

One common expectation also needs dealing with: an AMH blood test will not tell you whether you can still have a child. A network meta-analysis in female childhood cancer survivors (8 studies, 1,303 people) regards AMH as a reliable way of screening ovarian reserve, but neither direct nor indirect comparison detected any difference between survivors and healthy controls in mean AMH or in the risk of premature ovarian insufficiency[12]. Ovarian reserve, fertility and the chance of a pregnancy are three different things.

Taiwan's subsidy began in September 2025, and covers only two groups

The Ministry of Health and Welfare press release of August 2025 is clear: the medical fertility preservation subsidy pilot programme (醫療性生育保存補助試辦方案) runs from 1 September 2025 (114年9月1日), and covers "nationals of this country aged 18 to 40 with breast cancer or a haematological cancer". A woman's oocyte retrieval cycle is subsidised up to NT$70,000 per cycle and a man's sperm retrieval and storage up to NT$8,000 per occasion, with actual spending reimbursed where it falls below the cap; at most 2 cycles in a lifetime; participating institutions limited to those that have passed cancer care quality accreditation and are assisted-reproduction institutions contracted with the Health Promotion Administration[13]. The finer eligibility tiers: breast cancer limited to stage 0 through stage 3; haematological cancers include leukaemia, lymphoma or multiple myeloma; a physician must judge that the treatment to follow may impair reproductive function; it cannot be combined with the IVF subsidy for the same cycle; the procedure must be performed within 2 months of qualifying, and the claim submitted within 6 months of the cycle ending[5].

Other cancer types are not in the programme at present, and I write that down so nobody makes a wasted journey. Most counties and cities have their own subsidies for medical egg and sperm freezing, with their own conditions and windows; check with the public health bureau where you are registered, or with your case manager (個管師)[5]. The IVF subsidy 3.0 is a different programme and pays for IVF cycles[14] — do not treat the two as the same thing. The preservation programme has only just started, and there are no local figures on access to cite yet.

There is also a piece of law to settle before freezing, because it governs how you can use what you froze. Article 2 of the Assisted Reproduction Act (人工生殖法) defines the "receiving couple" as the husband and wife receiving assisted reproduction; Article 11 sets the requirements for performing it; Article 21 provides that gametes stored beyond ten years shall be destroyed, with an extension possible on the provider's written consent[15]. The freezing itself happens at a licensed institution; using it later is a separate set of rules. How your marital status and future plans map onto those articles is a question for reproductive medicine and the hospital before you freeze, not something to find out afterwards.

Four questions you can ask at your next visit

First: at roughly what level is my regimen expected to affect fertility? Second: can the assessment run alongside my pre-treatment work-up, and will it delay the start of treatment? Third: do my cancer type and stage meet the Health Promotion Administration subsidy criteria, and is there a separate subsidy where I am registered? Fourth: if I decide against it, what options remain later. One last line: contraception during treatment still applies, and it does not conflict with wanting a child once treatment is over — the full version is in "No period is not contraception".

From the physician

Nothing on this page should push your treatment start date back; the schedule is part of whether treatment works. What it should do is get the conversation booked, in parallel, in the week you are diagnosed. Bring the name of your regimen to that appointment — whether it contains an alkylating agent is the question that sets everything else.
