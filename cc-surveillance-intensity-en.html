Is closer follow-up worth it?

The conclusion here is uncomfortable, and it is the point of the article rather than a footnote. By the end you will know the question to ask is not "can I have one more CT?" but "which month should my colonoscopy go in?"

Colon Cancer Guide · After Treatment · 12 references
#Follow-up#Imaging#Recurrence#NHI Coverage

What the trials compared was intensive against less intensive follow-up, not follow-up against none at all — and that distinction decides whether this article gets read correctly. On how often follow-up should happen, the guidelines themselves admit there is no international consensus.

"Is once every three months too little? Could I have a CT scan at every visit?" Whoever asks has usually already worked out how far ahead they can feel safe. Let me finish the uncomfortable half of the answer first: follow you up more closely and we do find more recurrences, and more people do go on to salvage surgery aimed at cure — but the improvement in overall survival is not clear.

What you get more of is operations, not years

The 2019 Cochrane review pooled 19 randomised trials and 13,216 people with non-metastatic colorectal cancer[1]. The hazard ratio for overall survival was 0.91 (95% confidence interval 0.80–1.04). A hazard ratio is the ratio between the rates at which something happens in two groups, so below 1 means the closely followed group died at a lower rate; but the confidence interval — the range the true value plausibly sits in — crosses 1, so the difference may be chance. In absolute numbers, about 24 fewer deaths per 1,000 people, with the true effect anywhere between 60 fewer and 9 more[1]. Cancer-specific survival: 0.93 (0.81–1.07). Recurrence-free survival: 1.05 (0.92–1.21) — closer follow-up did not produce fewer recurrences[1].

What went up in that review was surgery: salvage surgery with curative intent, hazard ratio 1.98 (1.53–2.56), 60 more operations per 1,000 people (the true effect between 33 and 96 more)[1]. The authors put it plainly: more salvage surgery was done and it did not convert into better survival, and intensive follow-up did not improve quality of life, anxiety or depression[1].

None of that may be read as "then I need not come back". These trials compared intensive with less intensive follow-up, not follow-up with none — 2.3% of the minimum-follow-up arm in FACS still had surgery with curative intent[2], and COLOFOL's low-frequency arm still had CT scans and CEA[3].

Three randomised trials, and the sentence each of them nailed down

FACS randomised 1,202 people after surgery to CEA only, CT only, both, or investigation only if symptoms appeared[2]. Surgery with curative intent: 2.3% with minimum follow-up, 6.7% with CEA alone, 8% with CT alone, 6.6% with both; against minimum follow-up the differences were 4.4% (95% CI 1.0%–7.9%), 5.7% (2.2%–9.5%) and 4.3% (1.0%–7.9%)[2]. Deaths, though: 18.2% across the three intensive arms combined against 15.9% with minimum follow-up, a difference of 2.3% (−2.6% to 7.1%), no statistical difference; the authors' own words: if any of these strategies carries a survival benefit, its size is likely to be small[2]. CEA and CT together also picked up nothing more than either alone[2].

COLOFOL randomised 2,509 people with stage II or III disease to CT plus CEA at 6, 12, 18, 24 and 36 months after surgery, or only at 12 and 36[3]. Five-year overall mortality was 13.0% against 14.1% (risk difference 1.1%, −1.6% to 3.8%, P=0.43), cancer-specific mortality 10.6% against 11.4%[3]. Even recurrence was slightly commoner in the high-frequency arm (21.6% against 19.4%, P=0.15) — no "test more, find more" signal at all[3].

GILDA randomised 1,228 people with Dukes B2–C disease, median follow-up 62 months[4]. Intensive follow-up brought the diagnosis of recurrence forward by an average of 5.9 months, and that was statistically significant; but the survival curves did not separate, and health-related quality of life scores were the same in the two arms[4]. "Knowing earlier" and "living longer" came apart in that trial, and that distinction is worth holding on to.

"No difference seen" is not "no difference there"

A microsimulation analysis recomputed FACS, GILDA and COLOFOL: under reasonable assumptions about how well removing metastases works, the mortality reduction those trials could have expected to see was under 5 percentage points and their statistical power below 10% — they never had the ability to detect a small benefit that would still matter clinically[5]. The same model estimated intensive extracolonic surveillance, against one CT at 12 months, adds 2.6 months of life expectancy (95% credible interval 0.5–5.1 months), and the authors judge a surveillance trial large enough to settle this no longer feasible[5]. A US observational study of 8,529 patients across 1,175 facilities found high-intensity imaging, against low-intensity, changed the time recurrence was detected by only −0.95 months, overall survival hazard ratio 1.01 (0.94–1.08)[6].

So whose schedule should you be following now?

First, who I am citing. I could not obtain the full text of the ESMO guideline on localised colon cancer this time — the publisher's side blocked it — so the interval figures below are not ESMO's. They come from NCCN Colon Cancer version 2.2026 (7 April 2026)[7] and ASCO's 2013 endorsement of the Cancer Care Ontario follow-up guideline[8]; for the ESMO document, all I can do is point out it is still the current version[9].

NCCN version 2.2026 for stage II and III: examination plus CEA every 3–6 months for two years, then every 6 months for three more; CT of chest, abdomen and pelvis every 6–12 months, for five years[7]. The ASCO-endorsed version: CEA every 3–6 months for five years, abdominal and chest CT annually for three years in most situations, and PET not recommended for surveillance outside a clinical trial, in as many words[8]. The two do not agree on intervals, and that is itself part of the answer: after comparing 21 member countries' guidelines, the European Society of Coloproctology wrote outright that follow-up frequency is highly contested and international consensus unlikely on current evidence[10]. For stage I this NCCN version asks only for colonoscopy a year after surgery, no routine imaging[7].

The colonoscopy box is the one you must not push back

NCCN version 2.2026 splits colonoscopy after surgery in two: if the whole colon was already examined at diagnosis, colonoscopy at one year; if the tumour blocked the scope so the whole colon was never assessed, at 3–6 months[7]. The ASCO-endorsed version also says anyone whose colonoscopy was incomplete before diagnosis should have it completed after adjuvant treatment finishes and before the one-year mark[8]; and the US Multi-Society Task Force wrote a dedicated 2016 recommendation for colonoscopy surveillance after resection[11]. Miss this box, and a lesion sitting in the proximal colon that nobody ever saw gets dragged out a year.

Colonoscopy after an operation and faecal immunochemical testing in the general population are two logics and do not substitute for each other — for screening policy, see "Your gene report decides more than your treatment". What CEA means, and why a normal value does not mean all is well, is in "Where T3N1 actually comes from". What can still be done once follow-up finds a recurrence belongs to "Metastatic disease: is cure still possible?". There are also trials using ctDNA to decide whether to give chemotherapy; see "What an undetectable ctDNA actually means".

What I can look up in Taiwan, and what I cannot

Taiwan's NHI fee schedule (medical service items, version effective 1 May 2025) lists 12021C carcinoembryonic antigen at 400 points, 27050C carcinoembryonic antigen at 400 points, 28017C colonoscopy at 2,363 points, and three CT items — 33070B (without contrast), 33071B (with contrast) and 33072B[12]. PET, item 26072B, is indicated for staging and suspected recurrence of colorectal cancer, but the same note states it must not be used for routine follow-up examinations, and requires that CT, MRI and the like have already failed to stage the disease and the reason it is necessary be documented in the record[12]. That lines up with ASCO's position of not using PET for routine surveillance[8].

What I could not find is any official schedule from the Ministry of Health and Welfare or the NHI Administration for how often someone is followed up after colon cancer surgery; the fee schedule governs the conditions under which a single examination is paid for, not a schedule[12]. So in your situation, whether each examination can be claimed, at what interval, and whether you will have to pay — confirm that with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.

The next twelve months: how many boxes belong on your calendar

  • Write the clinic dates in first, then ask at your next appointment: how many CEA tests and how many CT scans this year, and who reads those reports? The intervals come as ranges; you want to know which end you are on and why.

  • Work out which colonoscopy you are. Whether the scope got all the way round your colon at diagnosis decides between one year and 3–6 months[7]. If it did not, that box should be booked today.

  • If a new symptom turns up between examinations, do not wait for the next box. The schedule was designed for people without symptoms; a symptom is itself a reason to rearrange it.

  • Put exercise into the same calendar. If you have finished adjuvant chemotherapy there is now a prescription from a randomised trial you can follow; it is in "Exercise lowers recurrence. How much do you need?".

  • You do not need to add a PET scan yourself. Both the ASCO guideline and the NHI fee schedule note exclude it from routine follow-up[8][12].

From the physician

If the scope did not get all the way round your colon at diagnosis, the 3-to-6-month box matters more than anything else here. At your next appointment get this sentence out: how many CEA tests and how many CT scans do I have this year, and which month should my colonoscopy be in. Copy the answers into your calendar before you leave the room.
