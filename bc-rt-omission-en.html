Omitting radiotherapy: every condition has to hold

The evidence for omitting radiotherapy exists, but the group it applies to is narrow, and every trial assumes you will finish the anti-hormone tablets. This one lists the conditions and the costs one at a time.

Breast Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 11 references
#Decisions#Late Effects#Endocrine Therapy#Recurrence

In the 2025 interim analysis of EUROPA, the endocrine-therapy-alone group had a worse two-year global health status than the radiotherapy-alone group, and its recurrence endpoint has not yet been reported. The assumption that leaving out one treatment buys you quality of life is not supported by the data.

I wrote this one more slowly than the others, because it can do harm in both directions. Someone who needed radiotherapy, reads this and decides against it, and what she gets in exchange is a markedly higher rate of local recurrence; someone who meets every condition, is frightened into having it anyway, and carries a cost she did not need to carry. So let me say it before anything else: omitting radiotherapy is an option with evidence behind it, but what it applies to is a whole string of conditions, and if one of them is missing the case does not hold.

The conditions are not "older, and a small tumour"

CALGB 9343 enrolled women aged 70 or over with clinical stage I disease (T1N0M0) that was oestrogen-receptor-positive, all of whom had had a lumpectomy; 636 were randomised to tamoxifen plus radiotherapy or tamoxifen alone[1]. PRIME II enrolled women aged 65 or over with hormone-receptor-positive, node-negative disease, a largest tumour diameter of 3 cm or less, clear margins after breast-conserving surgery, and who were taking adjuvant endocrine therapy; 1,326 were randomised to whole-breast irradiation or no radiotherapy[2]; its five-year results were published earlier[3].

Lay those two strings out and count them: age, stage, tumour size, receptor status, nodes, margins, type of surgery, and then the one that most often goes missing — continuing to take the anti-hormone tablet. Everything in this article is about hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative, node-negative early breast cancer. HER2-positive and triple-negative breast cancer were not in these trials and cannot be read across.

What omission buys you is not "the same". It is local recurrence, put back on

PRIME II, at a median follow-up of 9.1 years: the ten-year cumulative incidence of local recurrence was 9.5% in the no-radiotherapy group (95% confidence interval 6.8–12.3; a confidence interval is the band within which the true figure can reasonably sit) and 0.9% in the radiotherapy group (0.1–1.7), an absolute difference of 8.6 percentage points[2]. CALGB 9343, at a median follow-up of 12.6 years: freedom from locoregional recurrence at ten years was 98% with radiotherapy and 90% with tamoxifen alone, a difference of about 8 percentage points[1].

At the survival end there is no difference: PRIME II gives ten-year overall survival of 80.8% against 80.7%, with no substantive difference in breast-cancer-specific survival or regional recurrence either[2]; CALGB 9343 gives ten-year overall survival of 67% against 66%, with no significant difference in time to distant metastasis or in breast-cancer-specific survival[1]. "Survival is the same" is not the same statement as "it makes no difference whether you have it". Those extra eight or nine percentage points are paid for with a second operation and a second round of treatment, and the follow-up only runs to nine or thirteen years. Whether that trade is worth making will differ from person to person, but it is a trade, not a free option.

LUMINA and IDEA get quoted a lot, and neither one has a control arm

LUMINA is a prospective single-arm cohort study: it enrolled women aged 55 or over, after breast-conserving surgery, with T1N0 disease (under 2 cm and node-negative), grade 1 or 2, of luminal A subtype (centrally read Ki67 no higher than 13.25%), all of them taking adjuvant endocrine therapy, and none of them received radiotherapy. 500 women were eligible and enrolled; the five-year local recurrence in the same breast was 2.3% (90% confidence interval 1.3–3.8), which met the threshold set in advance[4]. The evidence-based review panel of the Canadian Association of General Surgeons later pointed out that the design is rigorous, but that putting its process into routine practice has practical limits — central Ki67 reading is not something every hospital can do, and there is no one-size-fits-all way of running it[5]. IDEA is single-arm too: postmenopausal women aged 50 to 69, unifocal pT1N0 disease, margins of 2 mm or more, oestrogen- and progesterone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative, a 21-gene recurrence score of 18 or below, and patients had to agree to take endocrine therapy for at least five years to be enrolled at all. Among 200 women, five-year overall survival and breast-cancer-specific survival were both 100%, and five-year freedom from any recurrence was 99%[6].

Single-arm means nobody was randomised to the radiotherapy side. What these trials can say is "recurrence in this group of people was very low at five years". They cannot say "no radiotherapy is the same as radiotherapy". And do not set LUMINA's 2.3% at five years against PRIME II's 9.5% at ten: the years of follow-up, the design and the population are all different.

EUROPA turned the intuition upside down in 2025

A lot of people assume that what omitting radiotherapy buys is quality of life. EUROPA compares the two directly — radiotherapy alone against endocrine therapy alone. It enrolled women aged 70 or over with stage I, luminal A-like disease after breast-conserving surgery, randomised 1:1, 731 in all. What was published in 2025 is a preplanned interim analysis covering 207 women at a median follow-up of 23.9 months. The change from baseline in global health status at twenty-four months was −3.40 in the radiotherapy group and −9.79 in the endocrine therapy group, an adjusted between-group difference of 6.39 (0.14–12.65, p=0.045), with the direction favouring radiotherapy; treatment-related adverse events were 67% in the radiotherapy group and 85% in the endocrine group, and the commonest grade 3 to 4 event was arthralgia (7% in the endocrine group, 0 in the radiotherapy group)[7].

This result cannot be turned round and read as "radiotherapy is better than tablets". EUROPA's other co-primary endpoint — five-year recurrence in the same breast — is stated in the paper itself as not reported here, and the trial is still recruiting[7]. Two hundred and seven women, two years, and only the quality-of-life half of the data: there is one conclusion that will bear weight, and it is this — stop assuming that leaving out one treatment automatically buys you a better life.

Every one of these trials rests on the same assumption

CALGB 9343 gave tamoxifen in both arms[1]; every woman enrolled in PRIME II was on adjuvant endocrine therapy[2]; LUMINA required that endocrine therapy had been started[4]; IDEA went as far as writing "agrees to take it for at least five years" into its entry criteria[6]. Which is to say: the whole evidence base for omitting radiotherapy grows in the soil of "you will finish the tablets".

And the real-world figures are not good. In 8,769 women with stage I to III hormone-sensitive breast cancer followed through pharmacy dispensing records, by year 4.5 only 49% had taken the full course in the optimal way[8]. Every trial of omitting radiotherapy assumes you are one of that 49%. What the actual discontinuation rates are, when people stop and why, is in "The side effects that actually make people stop"; whether the tablets run for five years or ten is a separate decision, in "Five years of endocrine therapy, or ten?". Only one line belongs here: say it in clinic when you cannot go on taking them, but do not stop on your own at home — the thing you were planning to trade the radiotherapy for is precisely the act of finishing the tablets.

What the non-randomised data fill in, and what they cannot

A Swedish prospective cohort enrolled 601 women aged 65 or over with unifocal, non-lobular, grade 1 to 2, oestrogen-receptor-positive pT1N0 disease, treated with breast-conserving surgery and five years of endocrine therapy and no radiotherapy: median age 71, median tumour 11 mm, median follow-up 119 months, cumulative incidence of local recurrence 1.5% at five years and 5.5% at ten, ten-year overall survival 83.1%, and 3 of the 601 women (0.5%) died of breast cancer[9]. That is a useful sense of the order of magnitude, but it is not a randomised trial, and the women selected into it were the lowest-risk group to start with.

On guidelines I will say only what I am sure of: the ESMO 2024 early breast cancer guideline is this series' anchor document[10], but I did not obtain a recommendation on omitting radiotherapy that I could quote word for word, so I am not going to pretend I have one. What can be seen across the entry criteria of the trials themselves is a common shape: an age threshold at 65 or 70, hormone-receptor-positive disease, negative nodes, a small tumour, clear margins, and continuing endocrine therapy taken as a precondition throughout. Taiwan has no provision covering or not covering "omission of radiotherapy", because that is a treatment decision; but note that the bundled hypofractionation codes are in principle limited to claims from hospitals on the list accredited for quality in cancer care[11] — where this is done is subject to an institutional threshold in Taiwan, and that one is to confirm with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.

One sheet is enough: the tablet, the dates, the mammogram

If you are in the middle of discussing this with your doctor, three things can be done today. First, copy that string of conditions into a table and go through them one at a time against your own report, and see whether you meet all of them or fall short on one — if you fall short on one, the question to ask is "what does that one turn my risk into". Second, open a page and write down the name of your anti-hormone tablet, the date you started, and every occasion you feel you cannot keep taking it, with the reason; that page is the material for the rest of the conversation with your doctor. Third, put the mammogram follow-up dates in the diary now — omitting radiotherapy does not mean going without follow-up, and in the Swedish cohort follow-up was at least once a year for ten years[9]; the full follow-up schedule is in "Does follow-up need a yearly whole-body scan?".

And to finish, my own position, plainly: I am not going to write that radiotherapy can be skipped, and I am not going to write that everyone has to have it. This is a decision to sit down and talk through with your pathology report, your age, your receptor status, and an honest answer to the question of whether you are willing to take a tablet every day for the next ten years.

From the physician

The condition most easily missed of all of them is "keeps taking the anti-hormone tablet", and in the real world only about half of people finish the course. Take your pathology report to clinic and go through age, tumour size, receptor status, nodes and margins one at a time; if you fall short on one, ask what that turns your risk into.
