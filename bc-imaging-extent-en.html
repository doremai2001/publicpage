The size on the scan isn't the real size

A preoperative magnetic resonance scan sees more, but what happens after it has seen it? Two randomised trials, a meta-analysis, and one set of lobular carcinoma data pointing the other way.

Breast Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 10 references
#Imaging#Staging#Decisions#Surgery

The randomised trials say reoperations did not go down, and the largest observational study works the cost out as 11.3 percentage points more mastectomy; but in invasive lobular carcinoma the matched analysis reverses the whole direction — reoperation 1.9% against 12.6%, with no difference in the mastectomy rate. The same test, a different histological type, a different answer.

The ultrasound measures 1.5 cm, the magnetic resonance scan says it looks like 3 cm, and after the operation the pathology report says 1.8 cm. None of those three numbers is wrong; they are measuring different things. The real question in clinic is not "which machine is more accurate" but "will one more test change the operation I end up having". There are randomised trials on that, the answer is uncomfortable, and it does not all point one way.

Adding a preoperative magnetic resonance scan did not reduce reoperations

Britain's COMICE trial recruited 1,623 women at 45 centres with biopsy-proven primary breast cancer scheduled for wide local excision and randomised them to an additional magnetic resonance scan or to no further imaging. The primary endpoint was reoperation within six months, further mastectomy, or a mastectomy at the first operation that was pathologically avoidable. The result was 153 women (19%) in the magnetic resonance group and 156 (19%) in the control group, odds ratio 0.96, 95% confidence interval 0.75 to 1.24, P value 0.77[1]. An odds ratio is one group's ratio of happened-to-did-not-happen divided by the other's; a figure of 0.96 together with a confidence interval that crosses 1 means no difference could be seen between the two groups.

The Dutch MONET trial recruited 418 patients with non-palpable lesions and randomised them to an additional magnetic resonance scan before biopsy. The rate of breast-conserving surgery at the first operation was similar in the two groups (68% against 66%), but among those whose first operation was conserving surgery, re-excision for a positive margin ran at 34% in the magnetic resonance group against 12% in the control group, P value 0.008; the authors' concluding sentence is blunt: adding a magnetic resonance scan was paradoxically associated with a higher re-excision rate and should not be used routinely for preoperative assessment[2].

The extra things that were seen turned into mastectomies

A meta-analysis of 9 studies (2 randomised trials and 7 controlled cohorts) and 3,112 patients: mastectomy at the first operation 16.4% against 8.1% (odds ratio 2.22), overall mastectomy 25.5% against 18.2% (odds ratio 1.54), while re-excision after an initial conserving operation was 11.6% against 11.4%, with no difference visible[3].

The largest prospective observational study, MIPA, recruited 5,896 patients at 27 centres and works the exchange rate out more clearly: the mastectomy rate in the magnetic resonance group was 11.3 percentage points higher, and what that bought was 3.2 percentage points fewer reoperations among those who had conserving surgery (8.5% against 11.7%)[4]. Percentage points and percent are very different here: 11.7 minus 8.5 is 3.2 percentage points, and writing it as "3.2% fewer" sounds like a reduction of one part in thirty, which is another thing entirely. One more sentence has to go in: the MIPA authors themselves point out that the patients who had magnetic resonance scans were already younger, with denser breasts, larger tumours and a higher proportion of lobular carcinoma — clinicians were ordering the test for the people already more likely to end up with a mastectomy. That is indication bias; it is not the scan cutting the breast off.

Eight years later, there were no fewer recurrences

If cutting a bit more, and cutting more definitively, really helped, it ought to show up in the end. An individual patient data meta-analysis of 4 studies and 3,169 patients followed to eight years: local recurrence-free survival was 97% in the magnetic resonance group against 95% in the no-magnetic-resonance group, P value 0.87, hazard ratio in the multivariable model 0.88 (95% confidence interval 0.52 to 1.51); distant recurrence-free survival 89% against 93%, hazard ratio 1.18 (0.76 to 2.27)[5]. What did relate to local recurrence was age, margin status and tumour grade. What margins mean and the trade-offs of conserving surgery are in "Conservation or mastectomy: is survival the same?".

One set of numbers points the other way: invasive lobular carcinoma

Turning all of the above into "preoperative magnetic resonance imaging is useless" would go too far. The MIPA lobular analysis selected 547 women with unilateral invasive lobular carcinoma diagnosed on core-needle biopsy and matched them one to one on nine confounders, leaving 103 in each group: the reoperation rate was 1.9% in the magnetic resonance group against 12.6% in the no-magnetic-resonance group, P value 0.007, odds ratio for avoiding a reoperation 7.29 (95% confidence interval 1.60 to 33.21); while first-line mastectomy at 21.4% against 18.4% (P value 0.727) and overall mastectomy at 23.3% against 21.4% (P value 0.867) showed no difference in either[6].

This set of numbers runs opposite to the ones above: fewer reoperations, and no extra breasts removed. But it is an observational matched analysis, not a randomised trial, and the authors write in the paper that there is at present no randomised controlled trial of preoperative magnetic resonance imaging for invasive lobular carcinoma diagnosed on core-needle biopsy[6]. A confidence interval stretching from 1.60 to 33.21 also says the estimate itself is very imprecise. So it is a reason to ask your doctor why the scan is being ordered, not a settled conclusion.

Those 3.1% in the other breast cost three needles in four that were not cancer

ACRIN 6667 scanned 969 women with newly diagnosed unilateral breast cancer whose other breast was normal on both clinical examination and mammography, and detected in that other breast 30 cancers that were occult clinically and on imaging, that is 3.1%; sensitivity was 91% and specificity 88% (sensitivity being the proportion of those who do have cancer that the test picks up, specificity the proportion of those who do not that it calls negative). The cost was that 121 women (12.5%) went for a biopsy because of a positive magnetic resonance scan, of whom 30 had cancer — so of every four women needled because of it, three did not[7].

The same study has a result that often gets written up backwards: the number of additional cancers detected was not affected by breast density, menopausal status or the histological characteristics of the primary tumour[7]. So "my breasts are dense, the extent can't be seen clearly, a magnetic resonance scan must be added" has no support when it comes to assessing extent in someone already diagnosed. The evidence on dense breasts and magnetic resonance imaging grew up in screening — a different group of people, not yet diagnosed, answering a different question, and the two cannot be carried across to each other.

Tomosynthesis is a screening tool, and two things I could not find evidence for

Breast tomosynthesis is often described as "more three-dimensional, so it shows the extent better", but its evidence grew up in screening. In pooled data from two randomised trials in the MAITA consortium, the detection rate in the tomosynthesis arm at the first round was 50% higher than with digital mammography alone, the detection rate at the following round was 14% lower, and overall a 16% excess of cancers remained (incidence ratio 1.16, 95% confidence interval 1.04 to 1.30); and the increased detection at the first round did not hold for tumours of 20 mm or more, grade 3 tumours, HER2-positive or triple-negative cancers[8]. That is an answer to "which cancers get picked up by screening", not to "how big is this tumour".

On two other situations I have to be honest: a tumour next to a breast implant, and occult primary breast cancer (a node felt in the axilla with no lesion found in the breast). Both are situations in which a magnetic resonance scan is considered clinically, on the grounds that conventional imaging cannot see there in the first place. I could not find high-quality comparative evidence in the literature that I could cite either for or against, so these two are an evidence gap here, not a recommendation.

One more test costs days, and I could not find the reimbursement status

In a single-institution retrospective series of 577 patients, the 130 who had a preoperative magnetic resonance scan had their preoperative assessment delayed by 22.4 days (P value 0.011); after controlling for tumour size and stage, those who had the scan had an odds ratio for mastectomy of 1.80 (P value 0.024), while the positive margin rate (21.6% against 13.8%, P value 0.20) was no better[9]. These are observational data and they cannot be multiplied together with the waiting times in "What the first month after diagnosis looks like", but they are a reminder that booking one more test really does eat time.

On the Taiwan side, the NHI fee schedule does contain item 33145B, diagnostic mammography (restricted to specialists in diagnostic radiology, twice per person per year), item 19014C, breast ultrasound, and item 33125C, stereotactic breast biopsy[10]; but I could not find a breast-specific magnetic resonance item in the fee schedule, and I could not find a corresponding item name for tomosynthesis either. Whether these two have to be paid for privately, and whether they need prior review, is to be confirmed with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office — do not work out your own case from someone else's experience.

If this scan sees something, will it change your operation

This article is not telling you to refuse a test your doctor has arranged. There is usually a specific reason for ordering it, and you are entitled to know what that reason is. You can ask it in these words:

  • "If this magnetic resonance scan shows something extra, will it change your surgical plan? Or would you arrange a biopsy to confirm it first?"

  • "Is my histological type lobular or ductal? Does that change how you view this test?"

  • "If I have this extra test, how far back does my surgery date move?"

If the answer is "if we see something we would biopsy it first, we would not go straight to mastectomy", then the risk of the test has been fenced in. What has to be avoided is the other situation: a scan that has seen a lot of things turning an operation from conserving to mastectomy with no pathological confirmation and no second discussion.

From the physician

This article is not telling you to refuse a test your doctor has arranged; it is telling you to ask what the reason for it is. At the next visit, ask "if something extra shows up, would you confirm it with a biopsy first or change the operation straight away", and ask while you are there how far back this test moves your surgery date.
