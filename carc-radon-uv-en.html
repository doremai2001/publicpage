AT HOME

Radon is not Taiwan's problem. Sunlight is.

The Nuclear Safety Commission measured 293 homes at a mean of 19.33 Bq/m³ with not one above the WHO recommendation; the exposure nobody regulates for outdoor workers is the one they get every day.

Carcinogens at work and at home · At Home · 20 references
#At Home#Radiation & Sun#Lung#Prevention

Radon's Group 1 status is not in dispute. Whether Taiwan should be anxious about it is a separate question. This piece also separates radiation-contaminated buildings from radon, sets out how strong the sunscreen evidence actually is, and answers “will all these CT scans hurt me”.

Radon is not worth paying to measure in Taiwan, and I will say that first. That it causes lung cancer is not open for discussion; the evidence is hard. But Taiwanese homes have been sampled officially, and the numbers are low enough that the money is not well spent. This piece takes two natural carcinogens — the radon you cannot see and the sunlight you can — and says why the effort belongs with the second.

Whether radon causes cancer stopped being the question long ago; the question is how much

Radon is the inert gas uranium decays into in soil and rock: colourless, odourless, seeping indoors through cracks in the foundations. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) lists radon-222 and its progeny as Group 1; so, on the same list, are solar radiation, ultraviolet radiation, tanning devices and ionising radiation of all kinds[1]. The commonest misreading first: Group 1 says how certain we are that it causes cancer, not how dangerous it is — "Group 1 is not a danger ranking" and this series' entry page, "Carcinogens at work and at home", set that out in full.

13 European case-control studies across 9 countries pooled 7,148 lung cancer patients and 14,208 controls. For every 100 becquerels per cubic metre (Bq/m³) of household radon, lung cancer risk rose 8.4% (95% confidence interval 3.0% to 15.8%) — the bracket is the plausible range for the estimate, wholly above 0, ruling out "no increase". Corrected for measurement error and re-estimated on long-term true average concentration it is 16% (5% to 31%)[2]. Dose and risk are linear, with no safe threshold. 7 North American studies (3,662 cases, 4,966 controls) agree, odds ratio per 100 Bq/m³ 1.11 (1.00 to 1.28)[3] — an odds ratio compares two groups' happened-to-not-happened ratios and runs a little larger than the true risk multiple, hence the absolute numbers next.

"An 8.4% rise" is a relative increase, not a probability of cancer. The absolute figures from the same European analysis: for lifelong non-smokers, cumulative risk of death from lung cancer before 75 at 0, 100 and 400 Bq/m³ is 0.41%, 0.47% and 0.67%; for smokers, 10.1%, 11.6% and 16.0%[2]. The same relative risk landing on a smoker produces an absolute increase some twenty-odd times larger. Radon and smoking combined carry a risk far higher than either alone; the analysis explicitly rejects a model in which the two effects add, the direction being close to multiplicative[2], and WHO puts the smoker's risk of radon-induced lung cancer at about 25 times the non-smoker's[4]. For anyone who smokes, dealing with radon will never be as worthwhile as stopping. Tobacco itself is in "Why do mothers who never smoked get lung cancer?".

Taiwanese homes have already been measured for you

The Radiation Monitoring Center of the Nuclear Safety Commission's assessment of radon doses in Taiwanese homes combined 289 households measured by I-Shou University in 2015 to 2017 with 4 measured on complaint in 2019 to 2021, 293 in all. Mean indoor radon activity concentration was 19.33 Bq/m³, living rooms 2.5 to 63.3 and bedrooms 2.5 to 95.3, estimated annual radiation dose about 0.90 millisievert[5]. Three thresholds to compare against: WHO's recommended annual average of 100 Bq/m³, with 300 the level not to be exceeded where national circumstances make 100 unachievable[6], and the US Environmental Protection Agency's action level of 4 pCi/L (about 150 Bq/m³)[7]. Not one of the 293 exceeded 100.

The report explains why they are low: Taiwan's geology is unremarkable in thorium and uranium series content and is not a radon-prone area, and common building materials are mostly reinforced concrete, so there is no regional variation[5]. Radon in Taiwan currently sits as "officially measured, not regulated": the report states plainly that no domestic regulatory standard for indoor radon has been set[5], and radon is not among the 9 pollutants covered by the Indoor Air Quality Standards[8].

WHO estimates radon causes 3% to 14% of a country's lung cancers, the proportion varying with concentration and smoking prevalence[4]. That range cannot be carried straight over: Taiwan has no domestic study of household radon and lung cancer and no official attributable-case estimate, and I will not invent one.

Radiation-contaminated buildings and radon are two different things told as one

Taiwan's radioactively contaminated buildings came from radioactively anomalous material mixed into scrap steel during smelting and melted into reinforcing bar. What they produce is external gamma radiation, not a gas seeping up from below. In 2003 the Nuclear Safety Commission issued regulations on preventing and handling radioactively contaminated building incidents, and steel makers with smelting furnaces installed portal radiation monitoring; buildings suspected of contamination have fallen from the 2,930 announced to 29 as of August 2023[9]. Worry about your own building goes to the Nuclear Safety Commission, not to a radon detector.

While we are here, one frame worth dismantling. Radon is natural, sunlight is natural, and asbestos is a natural mineral. "Natural is safer" does not hold for carcinogens.

The one shining on you every day is the one nobody regulates

IARC Monograph 100D finds basal cell and squamous cell carcinoma "strongly associated" with solar radiation, the causal association with cutaneous melanoma established, and evidence of association for ocular melanoma. Tanning devices, rare in Taiwan, are in Group 1 alongside[10].

What matters here is outdoor work. Two 2011 systematic reviews and meta-analyses put the pooled odds ratio for occupational ultraviolet exposure and squamous cell carcinoma at 1.77 (1.40 to 2.22) from 18 studies[11], and for basal cell carcinoma at 1.43 (1.23 to 1.66) from 24 studies[12], the association stronger at lower latitudes in both. In plain terms, forty to eighty per cent higher, not several times higher. Neither figure has a matching individual absolute risk estimate I can cite; the magnitude available is the global attributable death count below, and on that scale the overwhelming majority of outdoor workers do not get cancer. The saving grace of these two is that they grow where you can see them — the thing to do is not to calculate a probability but to get a lesion that will not heal looked at.

The scale: WHO and the International Labour Organization estimated in 2023 that 1.6 billion people — 28% of the working-age population — were exposed to solar ultraviolet radiation working outdoors in 2019, and that 18,960 people in 183 countries died that year of non-melanoma skin cancer attributable to occupational sun exposure, about a third of all such deaths, up 88% since 2000[13]. In Taiwan, the 2023 cancer registry shows 138,051 new cancers, with skin cancer 8th among the ten commonest[14].

There is a blank in the regulations. The current High Temperature Work Heat Hazard Prevention Guidelines (third edition) of the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), Ministry of Labor deal with heat throughout and mention neither ultraviolet radiation nor skin cancer[15], and I could find no other dedicated guidance on ultraviolet exposure in outdoor work. For people on building sites, in farming and fishing, doing deliveries or maintaining roads, sun protection is at present their own affair.

Has sunscreen been proved to prevent cancer? Not as firmly as you think

There is exactly one randomised trial. Nambour, in Queensland, Australia, randomised 1,621 residents to daily SPF 15+ sunscreen from 1992 to 1996. The original report on the 4.5-year intervention found no significant difference between groups in the incidence of a first skin cancer; only in total tumour counts were squamous cell carcinomas significantly fewer in the sunscreen group[16]. The follow-up to 2006, about 15 years in all: 11 new melanomas in the sunscreen group against 22 in the control group. For all melanoma the hazard ratio was 0.50 (0.24 to 1.02, P=0.051), which did not reach statistical significance; only invasive melanoma, 0.27 (0.08 to 0.97, P=0.045), did[17]. A hazard ratio of 0.5 means events arrived at about half the control rate, not that the probability was halved.

So I will not say sunscreen is proven to prevent melanoma: 33 events across both arms, one site, one trial, SPF 15. The opposite misreading needs blocking too — the group applying sunscreen daily in Nambour showed no increase in skin cancer[16][17]. Sunscreen is also not the only tool. Hats, long sleeves, shade and avoiding the strongest hours are more practical and cheaper for someone outdoors eight hours a day.

"Doctor, will all these CT scans hurt me?"

A 2025 risk model in JAMA Internal Medicine projected that the 93,000,000 CT examinations performed in the United States in 2023 would produce about 103,000 radiation-induced cancers over those people's lifetimes (90% uncertainty interval 96,400 to 109,500)[18]. That is a model extrapolation, not cancers already counted. Actual cohort follow-up comes from EPI-CT, 876,771 people across 9 European countries: excess relative risk of haematological malignancy per 100 milligray of bone marrow dose 1.96 (1.10 to 3.12), which at roughly 8 milligray per CT today is about 1 to 2 extra cases per 10,000 children scanned over the following 12 years[19]. That magnitude deserves to be taken seriously and is not a reason to refuse a scan: for abdominal pain in the emergency department, suspected stroke, or cancer staging and follow-up, the benefit far outweighs it. Publicly funded LDCT delivers one fifth the radiation of an ordinary CT[20]; eligibility and trade-offs are in "Why do mothers who never smoked get lung cancer?" and "When screening finds too much".

What this piece asks you to change is not the house

On the Nuclear Safety Commission's measurements, radon in ordinary Taiwanese homes is low and there is no need to have it measured. But remember this is 293 households' living rooms and bedrooms[5], not a national census, and it does not cover basements, semi-basements or old properties kept shut year-round. Radon has no safe threshold, so what those spaces need is not a decision about paying for measurement but air moving through them. Indoor ventilation in full is in "How much should the formaldehyde in a new home worry you?"; radiation worries about a building go to the Nuclear Safety Commission.

  • For smokers, the most effective thing in this entire piece is stopping. Radon and smoking are close to multiplicative, and in the same room the smoker carries an absolute increase some twenty-odd times the non-smoker's[2].

  • For people who work outdoors: brim, long sleeves, shade, and the heaviest tasks scheduled away from the strongest sun. Sunscreen supplements that; it does not replace it.

  • An ulcer that will not heal, a patch that scabs and peels over and over, or a mole that clearly changes shape or colour within a few months: dermatology, and do not wait for it to settle by itself.

  • Before a CT, ask what this scan will change about what happens next; keep old imaging discs to avoid repeating the same region within a short interval. For a child, ask whether paediatric dose parameters are being used.

The two subjects here are exact opposites: radon is talked about too much in Taiwan, sunlight too little. If only one thing changes today, do not buy a radon detector — go and check whether the hat of the person in your family who works outdoors is still in the boot of the car.

From the physician

I do not worry about radon in Taiwanese homes. I worry about two other things: smokers who believe a change of environment offsets the cigarettes, and the person outside for eight hours a day whose changing mole nobody has looked at. The first goes to smoking cessation, the second to dermatology, and neither has to wait for the next news item.
