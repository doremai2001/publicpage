DURING TREATMENT

Exercises will not prevent trismus. Do them anyway.

An interincisal opening of 35 mm or less defines trismus, and prevalence peaks around 44% at six months. Against the usual advice, exercise improves it rather than preventing it.

Head & Neck Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 12 references
#Trismus#Exercise#Side Effects#Late Effects#Recurrence

Find a ruler before you read on and write down one number: the millimetres between your front teeth today. Everything here, including the part that contradicts what you were probably told, only becomes usable once you have that figure.

Restricted mouth opening is the after-effect of head and neck cancer treatment most likely to be waved past in half a sentence. It does not hurt, it does not bleed, it does not turn a lab value red — but it decides whether you can eat properly, whether a dentist can get instruments in, and whether I can examine you properly. One thing here contradicts the version you may have been told, and I am going to say it straight.

Measure one number first: 35 millimetres

The clinical definition of trismus is a maximal interincisal opening (MIO) of 35 mm or less. That cut-off was not picked out of the air. In data from 89 head and neck cancer patients, 35 mm was the line that best separated a problem from no problem: it almost never misclassifies a normal person (specificity 0.98), at the cost of missing some milder cases (sensitivity 0.71). So if you measure below 35 it is real; if you measure above 35 that is no reason to stop measuring. The same cut-off applies whether or not you have your own teeth[1].

How do you measure it? A plastic ruler, open as wide as you can, measuring between the incisal edges of the upper and lower front teeth. With no ruler to hand, index, middle and ring fingers stacked vertically between the front teeth come to roughly 35 mm — but that is only an estimate. A proper record means a ruler, a number and a date.

I want you to have that number from today. The reason comes later.

When is it tightest? Around the sixth month

A systematic review of 23 studies, 15 of them in the meta-analysis, covering 2,786 patients treated with surgery, radiotherapy and/or chemotherapy, used exactly this MIO ≤35 mm definition. The weighted prevalence runs: 17.3% already at baseline, before treatment (95% CI 10.8–26.5); a peak of 44.1% at 6 months (36.7–51.8); down to 32.1% at 12 months (27.2–37.4); and still around 32.6% between 3 and 10 years (22.0–45.3). In the individual studies, oral cavity and oropharyngeal primaries carried the higher risk[2].

Two things there are worth stopping on. First, nearly one in six patients cannot open properly before treatment has even started — a baseline measurement is not a formality, it is your starting point. Second, the curve peaks at six months and falls back, but it never returns to the pre-treatment level; long term it settles at about a third.

Technique has helped. A MASCC/ISOO systematic review of 22 studies published between 1990 and 2008 found a weighted prevalence of 25.4% after conventional radiotherapy against only 5% in the few IMRT studies available then — and the same review states flatly that "no clear guideline recommendations could be made for the prevention or management of trismus"[3]. I particularly want that sentence in print, because it is more honest than any confident promise.

"Start practising early and it won't tighten up" — I cannot tell you that

This is the heart of the article, and the part I am most afraid of seeing misquoted.

A meta-analysis by a Taiwanese group — Tri-Service General Hospital, Taipei Medical University and Cochrane Taiwan — pooled 13 randomised controlled trials and 733 patients, and produced two findings that point in opposite directions[4]:

  • As treatment: in patients who already have trismus, exercise therapy combined with a jaw-mobilising device improved mouth opening significantly in all six studies that measured MIO, with pooled improvements from 4.48 mm (95% CI 0.20–8.75) to 14.20 mm (10.73–17.67).

  • As prevention: across the seven studies using the development of trismus as the outcome, there was no significant difference against usual care — risk ratio 1.20 (95% CI 0.61–2.34). The authors' conclusion leaves no room: exercise therapy can improve mouth opening once trismus has developed, "but does not prevent trismus" in patients being treated for head and neck cancer.

An independent line of evidence points the same way. A Swedish randomised study of 66 patients put one group on prophylactic training with a TheraBite jaw-mobilising device and left the other without, measuring MIO before treatment, weekly during radiotherapy, and at 3, 6 and 12 months: no significant between-group difference at any time point, with both groups still inside the normal range of MIO variation at 12 months[5].

So if somebody tells you that practising hard now means you will not tighten up later, that claim runs past the evidence. I would rather you heard it from me first.

Then why do I still want you practising from the radiotherapy weeks on

Because the reason changed. The exercises did not.

What the authors of that Swedish study recommended, having got a negative result, was that patients "do not need to be burdened with an intense prophylactic training programme", but that MIO should be measured during radiotherapy and for up to 12 months afterwards, to identify the small group who genuinely need training[5]. In their data, a small subset of the control group had lost a mean of 17% of their MIO by week 6 — and those patients improved once they started using the training programme.

Put differently, the value of starting early lies in building the habit, keeping the tissues moving, and above all leaving a baseline and a serial record so a decline is caught early. Not in "practise and it will not happen".

How do you practise? Taiwanese medical centres teach it fairly consistently. Chi Mei recommends mouth-opening practice 3 to 4 times a day, 5 to 10 minutes each time, with regular measurement of the interincisal distance, to prevent muscle fibrosis[6]. Chang Gung teaches putting a clean finger inside the mouth and stretching the cheek outward in several directions, holding 5 to 10 seconds each way, plus biting on stacked tongue depressors to widen the opening progressively[7]. The rehabilitation department at Taipei Veterans General Hospital puts lateral and protrusive temporomandibular joint exercises and head-and-neck mobility work into a home programme done 2 to 3 times a day[8]; at Shuang Ho Hospital a speech-language pathologist leads exercises for the lips, cheeks, tongue, temporomandibular joint and mouth opening[9]. The US National Cancer Institute's professional summary likewise lists jaw opening and closing exercises as a prescription to reduce the risk of trismus[10].

Two safety points. Stretch gently and progressively; never force or lever the jaw open. Forcing causes pain, temporomandibular joint injury and mucosal tearing, and in an irradiated mandible a fracture or exposed bone, while uncommon, is a real risk. Devices and advanced programmes should be started under a rehabilitation physician, a dentist or a therapist — not bought online and escalated alone. When mucositis is severe, do not push through it; ask whether the exercises can be timed for after your painkillers have taken effect.

It has already tightened. Is there anything to be done? Yes

This is the reassuring part: exercise therapy with a jaw-mobilising device does work in people whose mouth opening is already restricted, with improvements in the meta-analysis running from 4.48 mm to 14.20 mm[4]. Fourteen millimetres is roughly the difference between "a spoon just about fits" and "I can eat normally".

The same authors caution that the training programmes varied a great deal between studies and that more high-quality trials are needed[4]. So there is no standard answer to which device, how long, or how hard — which is exactly where a therapist's individual adjustment earns its keep. I could not verify the National Health Insurance (健保) coverage status of these devices in Taiwan — ask your case manager (個管師), or the hospital's medical affairs or billing office, who can look up what is actually charged and what is covered.

An answer to something I am asked often: low-level laser (photobiomodulation) does have guideline-level evidence in oral mucositis, but for trismus the WALT position paper found only a single case report, and for radiation-induced fibrosis no clinical studies at all, only in vitro and animal data — both classified as expert opinion only[11]. I am not putting it on the list of options here.

A neck that swells and hardens is a different problem

Neck lymphoedema and fibrosis often turn up alongside restricted mouth opening, but the evidence base is thinner still. The first systematic review of treatment for head and neck lymphoedema covered 26 items and 1,018 subjects, of which 14 were cohort studies and 7 case reports, with only 2 randomised controlled trials. Manual lymph drainage had the most studies and the most participants. Its conclusion: "evidence for the efficacy of all types of lymphoedema therapy is limited by paucity of large randomized controlled trials"[12].

So the reasonable position: manual lymph drainage is the best-studied approach and it is sensible to try it with a trained therapist, but do not expect the swelling to disappear. Worth asking whether your hospital's rehabilitation department offers it.

Getting tighter is not always fibrosis — this is the section that matters most

Fibrotic trismus is usually slow, symmetrical on both sides, and changes over months. If what you are seeing does not look like that, stop and think about other causes.

  • Losing several millimetres of opening over a few weeks, or becoming suddenly asymmetric — that calls for repeat imaging, not more exercises. Restricted opening can be how a recurrence invading the pterygoid space announces itself.

  • New jaw locking, clicking, or severe temporomandibular joint pain.

  • Not being able to eat, or a dentist not being able to treat you, because of the restriction.

  • New asymmetric swelling, redness, warmth or fever in the neck — infection and lymphoedema are handled separately.

  • A new hard, fixed lump in the neck — that goes down the recurrence pathway, not the rehabilitation one.

  • Numbness or weakness in the shoulder or arm.

  • Exposed bone in the mouth, or a sore that will not heal.

Bring your ruler to your next visit

First, bring your MIO numbers. Date and millimetres; two or three time points is enough. If nobody measured before treatment, measuring today and calling it your baseline is not too late.

Second, ask: how often should my mouth opening be measured, and by whom? That negative randomised study recommended measurement during radiotherapy and for 12 months after treatment[5] — the best-grounded sentence available on this at present.

Third, ask: if the number starts falling, what is the next step and who do I get referred to? Settling the contact point for rehabilitation medicine or the speech-language pathologist now is far quicker than looking for someone once your mouth will not open.

From the physician

Measure your own millimetres and bring the number to clinic. If your opening drops by several millimetres over a few weeks, or becomes asymmetric, that calls for repeat imaging rather than more exercise, because tightening can be how a recurrence announces itself.
