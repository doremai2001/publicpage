FOLLOW-UP AND RECURRENCE

A PSA bounce is not a recurrence

After radiotherapy the PSA often climbs and falls back on its own. One blood test cannot tell that apart from a recurrence.

Prostate Cancer Guide · Follow-up and Recurrence · 18 references
#Prostate#Follow-up & PSA#Biomarkers#Recurrence#Treatment Pathway

Surgery and radiotherapy are followed by two different sets of rules, and most of the panic I see comes from mixing them up. Before you read your own number, you need to know which set applies to you — and what the interval between tests is supposed to be.

On the last day of radiotherapy, or the day you leave hospital after surgery, most men assume what follows is "drop in now and then". The true thing first: for years afterwards what moves you is one number on a blood form, not the ten minutes in clinic — and it is read by two different sets of rules after surgery and after radiotherapy. Half the panic I see begins there.

How often should the blood be taken, and who drew up that schedule

The NCCN patient guideline Advanced-Stage Prostate Cancer (the copy I obtained says Version 4.2024) gives: PSA every 6 to 12 months after surgery or radiotherapy, every 3 months at higher risk of recurrence; imaging held back until the PSA is persistently detectable or recurrence confirmed and the next treatment has to be decided[1].

Honestly: I could find no document from the Health Promotion Administration (國民健康署) or the National Health Insurance Administration (健保署) setting out a follow-up schedule after prostate cancer treatment. There is no official timetable to copy. Your interval depends on your risk group — reading it is covered in "The few lines on the report that change treatment" — and on whether you had surgery or radiotherapy. Ask your consultant to write it on the same sheet as the blood form.

One situation is not follow-up at all: a PSA that never fell to undetectable after surgery. The 2026 update of the EAU–EANM–ESTRO–ESUR–ISUP–SIOG prostate cancer guideline, Part I, makes this "persistent PSA" a subject of its own[2]. If that is you, get clear which of the two you are.

After surgery we want undetectable. After radiotherapy we do not.

The American Urological Association (AUA) update panel screened 13,800 citations and read 436 papers in full, and found the phrase "biochemical recurrence" alone had 53 definitions after radical prostatectomy and 99 after radiotherapy — 166 in all. The panel's post-surgical standard is a PSA ≥0.2 ng/mL, with a second draw confirming a value above 0.2[3].

That standard cannot be moved onto radiotherapy, for a physical reason: the prostate is still there, and an irradiated prostate still makes a little PSA. So we follow not zero but a low, stable nadir. The RTOG–ASTRO Phoenix consensus conference of January 2005 set post-radiotherapy failure at "a rise by 2 ng/mL or more above the nadir", dated on the day the threshold is crossed rather than backdated — backdating was the old 1996 definition's practice, and the Phoenix panel wrote that it "performed poorly in patients undergoing hormonal therapy"[4].

Both definitions carry the same caveat, and the AUA panel put it plainly: this is a reporting standard, "not intended to represent a threshold value for which to initiate treatment"[3]. Reading "nadir plus 2" as "I am safe until then" is a misreading; so is reading it as "the day I reach it, injections start".

When the PSA jumps after radiotherapy, the commonest reason is not recurrence

It is called a PSA bounce: the PSA falls to its nadir, then some time later climbs on its own for a while, then comes back down on its own. It is common after radiotherapy and brachytherapy, almost nobody is warned about it, and the man meeting it first usually finds the worst version online.

Reported proportions differ widely, because treatments and definitions do. A Japanese series of 352 men given ¹²⁵I seed brachytherapy, median follow-up 82 months: 117 (33%) bounced, 10 (2.8%) by 2 ng/mL or more — formally crossing the Phoenix line — and still coming back down by themselves[5]. A Korean series of 100, same treatment: bounce 45%, a rise of 2.0 or more in 7%; median onset 24 months; at the peak the PSA sat a median of only 0.44 ng/mL above the earlier nadir, falling back in a median of 4 months[6]. In 554 men given high-dose-rate brachytherapy, the three-year actuarial bounce rate was 41.3% at nadir plus 0.2 and 6.8% at nadir plus 2.0; the larger the dose per fraction, the more bounce (hazard ratio 1.786 — bounces sooner and in more men, P=.024)[7]. External beam radiotherapy is lower: 445 evaluable men, median follow-up 5.9 years, bounce 14.4%, median 31 months[8]. Among 305 low- and intermediate-risk men given five-fraction stereotactic body radiotherapy, 25% bounced, median 11 months[9].

So do not memorise one figure. Short courses with large fractions are becoming ordinary, so more men will meet a bounce, not fewer.

A bounce and a real failure cannot be told apart on one blood test

The sentence that matters most: a single blood test cannot tell you whether this is a bounce or a recurrence. On the page they look identical — both are "higher than last time". What separates them is time.

The Japanese series of 352 did that arithmetic: median onset 20 months after treatment for a bounce, 17.5 months for a bounce crossing 2 ng/mL, 51 months for a true biochemical failure; and a bounce that crossed the line took at most 12 months to fall again[5]. Roughly: a rise appearing a year or two out, not large, settling within months, is usually a bounce; one starting three or four years out and higher at every draw is more likely real.

While you wait, four things.

  • Get the doctor to fix the date of the next blood test there and then. "Let's look again sometime" is not a plan; a date is.

  • Copy every PSA onto one sheet, date beside value. I want the slope and the nadir, not the newest cell.

  • Do not demand hormone therapy because of one rise, and do not stop your medication because of one. The AUA panel says it up front: these are reporting standards, not treatment thresholds[3].

  • New back pain with leg weakness, numbness, or a change in bladder or bowel control is not in the "wait for the next blood test" category — it is dealt with the same day. Its own section is "Back pain plus a weak leg: do not wait".

Two things get said to comfort people, and both need their strength stated properly. First, "men who bounce do better": in 223 men free of recurrence four years after seed brachytherapy, seven-year freedom from biochemical recurrence was 100% in those who bounced and 95.7% in those who did not[10]. That is a single-institution after-the-fact grouping: take the reassurance, not the prognosis. The second matters more: the bounce is a phenomenon of radiotherapy and brachytherapy. There is no such thing after surgery. After radical prostatectomy a confirmed PSA ≥0.2 is recurrence[3], and comforting yourself with a bounce costs time — salvage radiotherapy's results depend heavily on the PSA at which it starts.

That nadir number is more useful than you would think

In 663 men treated without hormone therapy, median follow-up 61.3 months, the lowest PSA reached within 12 months is already talking. After seed brachytherapy, if it was ≤0.7 ng/mL, seven-year biochemical-failure-free survival was 99.1%, against 90.2% above 0.7; after external beam radiotherapy, if ≤1.0 ng/mL, 85.4%, against 67.1% above 1.0[11]. It is worth knowing what yours is.

If it really is a recurrence, what decides how serious it is is not that number

The European Association of Urology (EAU) systematic review collected 77 studies, of which 14 and 20,406 men asked whether biochemical recurrence changes survival. It does — but only in one group: after surgery, a short PSA doubling time and a high final Gleason score; after radiotherapy, a short interval from treatment to failure and a high biopsy Gleason score[12]. That review became the EAU biochemical recurrence risk groups[13].

Hold it against your report. After surgery, low risk needs a PSA doubling time over 12 months and pathological ISUP grade group under 4; after radiotherapy, over 18 months from treatment to biochemical recurrence and biopsy ISUP under 4[14]. A European validation in 1,125 men showed it independently predicts metastasis (hazard ratio 3.46) and prostate cancer death (5.12)[15]. North American validation, 940 men, ten-year cancer-specific mortality: after surgery 3.6% low risk, 12% high risk; after radiotherapy 18.4% low risk, 49.5% high risk[14].

Read the last two together: a "low-risk recurrence" after radiotherapy still carries a ten-year cancer death rate above a "high-risk recurrence" after surgery. Not that radiotherapy is worse — the two groups differ to begin with — but the reassurance a surgical patient hears cannot be handed to a radiotherapy patient. In the same study the concordance indices were only 0.62 and 0.69[14]: it separates directions, not individuals.

"Shall we do a PET scan while we're at it, just to be safe?"

I get this every week, and the answer depends on your PSA. In a prospective multicentre trial of 635 men with biochemical recurrence, ⁶⁸Ga-PSMA-11 PET found the disease in only 38% at a PSA under 0.5 ng/mL, and reached 97% only at 5.0 and above[16]. Scanning at a very low PSA gives a negative result carrying almost no information, and yet leaves people believing they have the all-clear.

From the same programme: in 382 men who had a PSMA PET, 150 intended conventional tests were cancelled, 52 of them bone scans or sodium fluoride PET[17]. That is the argument against "PSA up, book a bone scan": while the number is too small for anything to show, a scan buys no answer.

On cost in Taiwan, one thing: the National Health Insurance (健保) indications for PET do not include prostate cancer, and that document's line about prostate cancer being prone to false positives and negatives refers to fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET, not PSMA — different tracers[18]. Why the two must not be conflated, and how to ask the price of PSMA PET in Taiwan, is in "Two scans, answering different questions".

Three things to ask out loud after the next blood test

First: "What was my PSA nadir, and when was it measured?" That sets the ruler for every later rise. Second: "If the next one is higher, what is the plan — repeat the test, or arrange imaging? And what date is the next test?" Settling that while things are calm beats deciding in a panic. Third: "Given my treatment and risk group, how often should I be followed up, for how many years?" Then write it down.

From the physician

Your nadir, your treatment and the interval since it ended matter more than the latest figure. Ask for your interval to be written down, and keep every PSA on one sheet with its date. If a rise appears, the plan is a repeat test on a fixed date, not an immediate scan.
