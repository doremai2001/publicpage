THE FIRST MONTH

The first month: the boxes that actually have to be ticked

Five boxes whose order cannot be swapped — and NHI item 47079B lists finances, work, faith and family among what should be discussed.

Situation Guides · The First Month · 7 references
#Just diagnosed#The system#Getting Ready#Decisions

The sentence I hear most this month is "isn't this too slow". I have put in the evidence I could find: a meta-analysis across seventeen indications, a U-shaped curve that runs against intuition, and the places where no Taiwanese standard exists to quote. No safe number of days comes out of it.

If your cancer already has a series on this site, start there — the head and neck series' "What happens in the month after diagnosis", the rectal series' "Just diagnosed, and nobody has touched the tumour yet", the colon series' "What the first month after diagnosis looks like", the prostate series' "This first month: order matters more than speed", the breast series' "What the first month after diagnosis looks like", the lung series' "Those two weeks of waiting are not wasted". Those go into more detail than this one.

This one is for people whose cancer has not had its turn — liver, pancreas, oesophagus, stomach, ovary, lymphoma, the groups with nothing dedicated to them here. The good news: the first month overlaps across cancers far more than most people expect. In 2022 there were 130,293 new cancers in Taiwan, median age at onset 64, so more than half of those diagnosed were already past 64[1]. All hundred-odd thousand of them walk a first month built on the same frame.

What is moving this month is five boxes, not none

Get the diagnosis confirmed, the stage established, the general condition and other illnesses taken stock of, the case put to a team discussion — and only then does a treatment plan exist. The order is not interchangeable: staging waits on pathology and imaging, the plan waits on staging, and the Catastrophic Illness Certificate (重大傷病證明) waits until you hold a medical certificate of diagnosis (診斷證明書) carrying an ICD code.

From where you sit, those five boxes look like nothing being done. The tumour has been seen, the report says malignant, three weeks have gone, nobody has touched it. I understand the agitation. What these weeks decide is how the next six to twelve months run, and if the order goes wrong, every later step has to be done again.

"Waiting for the pathology report" is the one box nobody can skip

In Taiwan this is written into the statutory definition. Article 3, sub-paragraph 1 of the Cancer Control Act (癌症防治法) defines cancer as a malignant tumour "confirmed by pathological biopsy, or effectively presumed to be diagnosed by other examinations or tests, showing recurrence or metastasis clinically"[2]. Staging, the treatment plan and the Catastrophic Illness Certificate application all hang off this box.

So "when will the report be ready" is the sentence worth chasing this month, far more useful than "when can I have surgery". A biopsy sometimes needs extra special stains or molecular tests, costing a few days; those days buy one more piece of ground under the drug choice later. If the report is back and you want another opinion, or a second read of the pathology, how to get hold of your records, images and slides is in "Asking for a second opinion, out loud".

In Taiwan the treatment plan is a step with a name

The NHI fee schedule (支付標準) carries item 47079B, the cancer treatment plan consultation and planning fee (癌症治療計畫諮詢規劃費), effective 1 July 2023. Its payment rule is written out more fully than most patient leaflets: "this item includes consultation, assessment, planning, treatment recommendation, handling of emotions and guidance in respect of the patient's disease condition and physical status, the most suitable treatment modality, adherence to admission and outpatient follow-up, prognostic factors, psychological adjustment, finances, work, faith and family"; it is restricted to patients with a confirmed pathological diagnosis of cancer or of recurrence, and claimed once per treatment plan on completion of treatment[3].

I quote it not so you go and audit your bill, but because that list works as your checklist: finances, work, faith and family are all written into it. If those things have not been raised in clinic, it is not that they should not be; they have not been yet. How to ask about the money side is in "How to ask about a self-pay item" and "The cost that isn't on the receipt".

The meeting you will not be attending

Some cases go to a multidisciplinary team meeting (MDT) this month and some do not — where the treatment path is plain, discussing it case by case changes nothing. What proportion of cases in Taiwan reach a meeting, I could not find a statistic to cite. What the meeting does, what traces it leaves in the Taiwanese system, how far the evidence goes, and why "a meeting was held" is not a guarantee of quality, are in "Has my case been discussed by a team?". One sentence stays here: it is a step whose conclusion you may ask about, not a black box.

"Do these few weeks matter?"

No general answer exists, but there is material to lay out. A 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis admitted only high-validity studies controlling for the major prognostic factors — 34 studies, 17 indications, 1,272,681 patients, across bladder, breast, colon, rectal, lung, cervical and head and neck cancer. In 13 of the 17 indications, delay was associated with a statistically significant rise in mortality. The surgical estimates were consistent, at a mortality hazard ratio of 1.06 to 1.08 for every four weeks of delay — a hazard ratio compares the rate at which events happen in two groups, and 1 means the same rate. On the radiotherapy side there were only three estimates, of which adjuvant radiotherapy after breast-conserving surgery came out at 0.98 (confidence interval 0.88 to 1.09, not reaching significance); the authors also record plainly that five radiotherapy indications had no high-validity data at all[4].

The material pointing the other way is just as solid. Three independent Danish and UK datasets set the interval from a colorectal symptom first presenting in primary care to diagnosis against five-year mortality; all three came out U-shaped: the group with the shortest interval had the highest mortality — because the patients with the most alarming symptoms are diagnosed first, not because "slower is better". That is the authors' own explanation, and they add that the bias cannot account for the rising limb where the interval grows longer[5]. Methodologically the phenomenon has a formal name, the waiting time paradox, written up as a source of bias any analysis linking waiting time to clinical outcome has to deal with first[6]. Going through the systematic reviews published from 2010 to 2019, the same team found this literature incommensurable with itself: 29 reviews, 10 cancers, 33 different definitions of the waiting interval, and two reviews of diagnostic delay that reached opposite directions[7].

So the honest statement is this: the cost of delay exists, but the heterogeneity is large and it depends on the indication, and that set of numbers cannot be used as a promise to you personally, nor turned round as reassurance that waiting costs nothing. "Do these few weeks matter for me" is a question for your treating physician, because it differs a great deal between cancers. I am also not giving you a waiting time in days for Taiwan — I could not find any official time standard or quality indicator to cite.

Some situations are not in the "waiting for a slot" category

Remember this paragraph on its own, because "I am still waiting for tests anyway" gets read as "so put up with whatever happens". Heavy bleeding, difficulty breathing, a change in consciousness, pain you cannot control, fever with shivering, a sudden severe headache or sudden weakness in a limb — these are not in the "waiting for a slot" category. Go to hospital immediately. A scheduled wait and putting up with something in silence are completely different things.

The registry you never file, and four things you do

The one you do not file is the cancer registry. It is a statutory duty of the medical institution and you go through no procedure at all; the provisions and the timetable are in "Beyond the Catastrophic Illness Certificate: what else you can claim".

Four things you can act on. First, get a list out of them: which staging investigations are still missing, and what date they are expected to be complete. That is more useful than asking how much longer. Second, put every medicine, supplement and Chinese herbal preparation on one sheet of paper, take it to clinic and hand it to the doctor or the pharmacist; whether to change anything, and what to drop first, is their decision, so do not stop anything on your own. Third, where this hospital's staff, equipment or expertise cannot provide complete treatment, Article 73 of the Medical Care Act (醫療法) imposes the duty to "advise the patient to be referred", and the referral must include a written referral summary handed to you[2] — you can ask that out loud. Fourth, if a clinical trial is mentioned, that runs on a different logic, and is in "A trial is not treatment designed for you".

Two more that are about people rather than paperwork: whether to tell an elderly parent the truth, and how far, is in "Whether to tell him, and what the law says"; which extra layer of assessment matters when the patient is old and carries several other illnesses is in "Eighty years old: treat, or not?". The rules above were verified on 30 August 2026, and the NHI fee schedule cited is the 114.03.13 edition of the full table; each hospital's process and scheduling differ, so confirm with your case manager (個管師), the medical social work office (社工室) or the hospital billing office (醫務課).

From the physician

Those delay hazard ratios are population averages and vary hugely by indication; they will not estimate your own few weeks. One thing to do today: ask which staging investigations are still missing and what date they are expected. Heavy bleeding, difficulty breathing, a change in consciousness or fever with shivering are not in the waiting category — go to hospital.
