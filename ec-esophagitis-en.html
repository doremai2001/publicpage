DURING TREATMENT

Getting through radiation oesophagitis

Oesophagitis mostly arrives in weeks two and three, and the most painful days are exactly when you most want a break. Grading, pain relief, the evidence on preventive remedies — and when I will stop the treatment for you, and when you must not stop it yourself.

Oesophageal Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 17 references
#Side Effects#Swallowing#Nutrition#Daily Life

There is one section of this article I most want you to read to the end: pausing radiotherapy is a decision made after the doctor's assessment, with the missed sessions made up afterwards; you simply not coming in is an entirely different thing. The grading and pain relief that come before it are there to get you through without needing to stop.

Around the second or third week of radiotherapy, swallowing starts to hurt, as if there were a burn running from the throat down to the chest — almost every patient I treat with chemoradiotherapy for oesophageal cancer goes through this stretch. It is also the stretch in which most people ask me "can I rest for a few days?" Below I take it in time order: when it comes, what to do at each grade of pain, which of the things meant to prevent it have evidence and which do not; the last section is about when I will stop the treatment for you, and when you must not stop it yourself.

Pain from week two or three is on schedule, not a sign something went wrong

Timing first. Radiation oesophagitis most often appears two to three weeks after radiotherapy starts and can persist until four weeks after it ends — this figure comes from a review of the lung cancer literature and is extrapolated to oesophageal cancer, because the oesophageal cancer trials did not report this separately. The same review makes a specific point about oesophageal cancer: when systemic treatment (concurrent chemotherapy) is added, symptoms appear on average about a week earlier and the proportion unable to complete the planned treatment is higher[1]. So for someone on concurrent chemoradiotherapy, feeling unwell from week two is expected.

How bad does it get? The landmark oesophageal cancer trial RTOG 85-01 (published 1992, 121 randomised patients) put the numbers bluntly: in the concurrent chemoradiotherapy arm, severe side effects 44% and life-threatening side effects 20%, against 25% and 3% with radiotherapy alone[2]; in the long-term follow-up, toxicity that was actually fatal was 10% against 2%[3]. That is technique from more than thirty years ago, and today's fields and supportive care are different, but it explains why these five or six weeks cannot be treated as a course where "you just get zapped and go home".

"Can't eat enough, needs a tube or admission" — that is grade 3

When doctors say "grade so-and-so", it has a definition. The US National Cancer Institute's CTCAE version 5: grade 1 is no symptoms, seen only on investigation; grade 2 is symptomatic, with altered eating or swallowing and a need for oral nutritional supplements; grade 3 is "severely altered eating or swallowing, requiring tube feeding, total parenteral nutrition or hospitalisation"; grade 4 is life-threatening[4]. You can hold this against yourself: going from "switched to soft food" to "switched to liquids" is grade 2; "can't even get enough liquid down" is the doorway to grade 3.

How common is grade 3? The same lung cancer review puts it at about 10% to 20% with modern concurrent chemoradiotherapy and only 1% to 2% with radiotherapy alone[1] — once again, these are lung cancer numbers; in oesophageal cancer the field wraps the whole length of the tumour, so I use them as a direction, not as a precise probability. Who is more likely to get a bad case? Among 100 oesophageal cancer patients receiving radiotherapy in Tianjin, grade 2 or higher oesophagitis occurred in 41%; on univariate analysis it was associated with weight loss of more than 10%, low albumin and large tumour volume, and on multivariate analysis only the nutritional assessment score (PG-SGA ≥9) remained an independent factor[5]. People who are already thin and already eating too little when treatment starts get worse oesophagitis — which is why "Can't swallow, weight falling: a feeding tube first?" has to be discussed before treatment.

Pain relief and texture: swallow for as long as you can, but do not grit your teeth through it

The evidence for managing this stretch is almost all review-level clinical experience, not randomised trials, and I say so plainly. The rough order of steps is: a local-anaesthetic mouth rinse (a viscous preparation containing lidocaine), acid suppression, blending food to purée or liquid, adding an antifungal if candida is suspected; when pain interferes with eating, the review's own words are that "opioid analgesics such as morphine are frequently required", and when dysphagia is marked the drug has to be switched to a liquid form or given intravenously, subcutaneously or by patch[1]. The mistake I see most often in clinic is the patient saving the painkiller for "when I really can't take it any more" — when it hurts you cannot eat, when you cannot eat you lose more weight, and the thinner you are the worse the oesophagitis gets; that loop has to be cut early. I do not put doses in an article; that is a prescription that differs for every person.

On texture there is no literature to cite; this is my clinic experience: warm is better than hot, slippery is better than dry, small mouthfuls are better than large; put down anything sour, spicy, alcoholic or very salty (soups included) for these few weeks. The dietitian will give you a list of what you can swallow, and that list is worth more than any supplement.

The things people try in order to prevent it mostly did not work

Patients often bring me all sorts of things and ask whether they can take them in advance to prevent it. I will go through what has been tested. Sucralfate (a mucosal protectant) has two randomised trials: the 1997 US NCCTG double-blind trial of 97 patients, in which 40% of the sucralfate group stopped the drug within the first two weeks because of gastrointestinal side effects (4% in the placebo group) and the oesophagitis scores did not improve[6]; and a 2026 Memorial Sloan Kettering trial of 117 patients comparing prophylactic with therapeutic use, stopped early because there was no difference between the arms, with opioid use at the end of treatment 30% in both[7]. Manuka honey: NRG/RTOG 1012, a three-arm randomised trial of 163 lung cancer patients on chemoradiotherapy, found no difference in its primary endpoint of swallowing pain at four weeks[8]. Glutamine: a 60-patient lung cancer trial at Chung Shan Medical University in Taiwan saw grade 2 to 3 oesophagitis of 6.7% against 53.4%[9] — single-centre, small, open-label, lung cancer, dose only up to 30 Gy; I will not turn it into a recommendation. For herbal medicine there is a meta-analysis of 81 randomised trials and 7,283 patients with a relative risk for incidence of 0.71, but the authors themselves write that the "certainty of evidence is limited"[10]. To put it plainly: at present there is nothing I would actively tell you to go and buy to prevent oesophagitis.

When I will stop it for you, and when you must not stop it yourself

First, why you cannot stop on your own. Once the overall course of radiotherapy is stretched out, the tumour speeds up its regrowth in the gap: oesophageal cancer's own older data (1990s, radiotherapy alone) show local control falling by around two percentage points for each day of delay[11]; the evidence is firmest in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma[12]; and a modern retrospective series of concurrent chemoradiotherapy could not see an effect — but in that cohort the median delay was only four days[13]. How to read those three sets of data is written up in "Radiation dose: why higher is not better"; here I keep only the conclusion: not one of them says an interruption is safe. The way I read that modern retrospective series is this: it can only say "a brief pause arranged by the doctor, with the sessions made up afterwards, showed no visible harm"; it cannot say "stopping for a few days on your own is fine" — nobody in that cohort simply failed to turn up. In the Dutch ARTDECO trial, 94% completed radiotherapy[14]; this is something the great majority of people manage.

So when will I stop it? What follows is my clinical judgement; no guideline writes it as a rule: you cannot swallow even water, or you are clearly dehydrated (dizzy on standing, passing little dark urine); fever together with a sharp worsening of swallowing pain, where something other than candida has to be ruled out; grade 3 oesophagitis with nutrition no longer holding and the tube not yet in place; blood counts down to an unsafe range. This kind of stop is "a pause after the doctor's assessment, plus making up the sessions afterwards" — a different thing altogether from you not coming in.

When you must not stop on your own: if it only hurts and you can still swallow liquids, what needs to happen is the painkiller being stepped up and tube feeding or nutritional support being connected — not taking leave. Something at home, the New Year holiday, feeling exhausted — talk to the radiation oncology team about these first, and we will arrange it. Unable to swallow even water, fever, severe chest pain — that is not a wait-at-home situation, it is come back the same day; those situations are written up in "When to come back the same day".

Not just the oesophagus: cough and breathlessness need watching in these weeks too

The field passes through the lung, so radiation pneumonitis is another side effect to know about. A systematic review from the IMRT era (19 studies, 874 oesophageal cancer patients) plus a single-centre series of 73: grade 2 or higher radiation pneumonitis about 6.6% and 5.5%, no grade 4 or 5 reported, and the larger the volume of lung receiving 20 Gy or more, the more likely it is[15]. What I see mostly appears weeks to months after radiotherapy ends, not during the course. A new dry cough, getting breathless on the stairs, a low-grade fever — tell us; in people with pre-existing lung disease I pay particular attention.

What to write down every week for these five to six weeks

I ask every patient to keep a small notebook and bring it to each weekly visit. Page one is weight, measured once a week on the same day — ESPEN only says "regular assessment" without giving a frequency[16]; weekly is my habit. Page two is swallowing: what you could still swallow this week, and on which day one grade became impossible. Page three is pain: 0 to 10, recorded before and after the painkiller, and how many times a day you took it. Page four is water: how much went in over the day, and the colour of your urine. With these four pages open on the clinic desk, I know within thirty seconds whether to adjust the painkiller, connect tube feeding, or pause. The NHI "general tube feeding" item for tube-feed formula lists oesophageal cancer among its indications[17]; for the reimbursement details of painkillers and mucosal protectants I could not find a clause I could cite — ask the pharmacy department. You can start this notebook today; the first line of the first page is your weight before treatment begins.

From the physician

Do not save the painkiller for when you "really can't take it any more": when it hurts you cannot eat, and the less you eat the worse the oesophagitis gets. If you want time off or a few days' rest, ring the radiation oncology department first and let us assess and arrange it — do not just stay away. Unable to swallow even water, fever, severe chest pain: come back the same day.
