FOLLOW-UP AND BEYOND

The tablet you take for life, and how much

The 2025 edition deleted the TSH target numbers outright — this article is about why they were deleted, and who decides your number now that nobody is copying it out of a table.

Thyroid Cancer Guide · Follow-up and beyond · 14 references
#Endocrine Therapy#Bone Health#Side Effects#Follow-up

Most people assume taking a bit more means it comes back less. The randomised evidence for suppression is one trial, and what it says is that not suppressing was not worse; while on the cost side, most of the figures were never measured on thyroid cancer patients at all.

The question I hear most in clinic is not "am I going to be all right". It is "how long do I have to take this tablet". And then, almost always, a second one: "if I take a bit more, does it come back less?"

This article deals only with how much levothyroxine you end up on and what that amount rests on. Half the gland or all of it is in 〈Half the gland, or all of it〉; how the thyroglobulin they draw from your arm is read, and how long follow-up runs, is in 〈Follow-up is an ultrasound and one number〉.

Replacement and suppression are not the same thing

Nobody argues about taking thyroid hormone after the gland comes out. The body needs it. The argument is about which box you are aimed at. Replacement brings TSH back inside the normal reference range. Suppression means deliberately taking a little more, pushing that signal below normal, because differentiated thyroid cancer cells still listen to it. Every dispute lives in the second one.

The 2025 edition took the numbers out

The set of values you found online, the ones you can line yourself up against, is how the American Thyroid Association wrote it in 2015[1]. The 2025 edition deleted them outright. What is left in the table is four boxes of words: an excellent response and an indeterminate response keep TSH within the normal reference range; a biochemical incomplete response and a structural incomplete response go below it[2]. There is no figure to copy down.

The footnote to that table gives its own reason, word for word: "Data on optimal TSH target range are inconclusive and/or conflicting". The matching recommendation is a Conditional recommendation with low certainty evidence[2]. This does not sound like an answer. It is an honest one. The number your doctor gives you is not being copied out of a guideline any more. It is a judgement someone made for you.

What the target hangs on is not the box you started in

There is a turn here that is easy to miss. Those four boxes do not hang on the recurrence risk group you were put in on the day of surgery. They hang on your response to therapy[2]. That response category is assigned after a stretch of follow-up, and it moves. So the target was never meant to be set once and kept for life. The guideline adds a separate line: the risks against the benefits of TSH suppression, and the goal itself, should be re-evaluated over time — which the guideline does not grade, listing it as good practice[2].

And a sentence that was not there before

The new edition writes something no previous one did: "Long-term TSH suppression is not suggested for patients with low- or intermediate-risk disease who have no evidence of biochemical or structural recurrence." Conditional recommendation, low certainty evidence[2].

One thing I will report rather than tidy. That sentence uses the old three-tier wording, low-risk and intermediate-risk, while the recurrence risk stratification in the same document has been rebuilt into four tiers. The guideline does not say how the two map onto each other, and I am not going to translate it across on its behalf. If you want to hold that sentence against your own report, ask your doctor first whether it covers you.

The benefit side is this small

There is one randomised trial. A single-centre Japanese trial published in 2010 enrolled papillary thyroid carcinoma, 218 in the suppressed arm and 215 not suppressed, with disease-free survival as the endpoint[3]. It was a non-inferiority design, the result sat inside the margin, and disease-free survival did not differ (the confidence interval for the hazard ratio for recurrence ran 0.85 to 1.27). It used the AMES classification of the day rather than today's stratification, and most of those patients had neither a total thyroidectomy nor radioiodine (I-131). So I will not write that suppression has been proved useless. A non-inferiority trial cannot prove that. It can say one thing only: in these people, not suppressing was not worse.

The rest is observational and pooled. A 2002 meta-analysis supports suppression: across studies running from 1934 to 2001, 2,880 of 4,174 patients with papillary or follicular thyroid carcinoma were suppressed, and the relative risk for disease progression, recurrence and death combined was 0.73 (confidence interval 0.60 to 0.88) — but the span of years is enormous and neither surgery nor radioiodine was standardised across it[4]. A 2024 meta-analysis took only adults with intermediate- and high-risk differentiated thyroid cancer (the older three-tier wording again) followed for five years: the pooled progression-free and disease-free endpoint gave a hazard ratio of 0.75 (95% confidence interval 0.48 to 1.17), disease-specific and overall survival 0.69 (0.31 to 1.52), neither significant; cardiac and skeletal adverse events came out at 1.82 (1.30 to 2.55), significantly higher in the suppressed arm[5].

On the cost side, every number belongs to somebody

I am going to labour this section, because not labouring it misleads. Many of the figures for the harms of suppression were not measured on thyroid cancer patients at all. The guideline concedes as much itself.

Bone first. A cross-sectional study of 179 women on long-term levothyroxine after thyroidectomy, median age 59, 178 of them postmenopausal, sorted them into three groups by how far their TSH ran (the study's own groupings, not anybody's target). Radiological vertebral fractures were present in 44.6% of the most suppressed group, 24.0% of the middle one and 4.3% of the least; and inside that most suppressed group, women with normal bone density were fracturing no less often than the rest[6].

Step one ring further out and these are no longer cancer patients. In individual participant data from 13 prospective cohorts and 70,298 people from the general population, subclinical hyperthyroidism carried a hazard ratio of 1.36 for hip fracture (95% confidence interval 1.13 to 1.64); in the lowest TSH band, hip fracture was 1.61 (1.21 to 2.15, 47 events among 510 people) and clinical vertebral fracture 3.57 (1.88 to 6.78) — that last one standing on 8 events among 162 people[7]. Evidence pointing the other way belongs here too. Among US veterans, 10,370 thyroid cancer patients against 10,370 matched controls, a mostly male population, osteoporosis ran 7.3% against 5.3% (odds ratio 1.33, 95% confidence interval 1.18 to 1.49) — but fractures were not more common, and the odds ratio between TSH and fracture was 1.01 (0.96 to 1.07)[8]. Losing bone density and actually breaking something are not one event.

The atrial fibrillation figure people quote most comes from 2,007 community-dwelling adults aged 60 and over followed for ten years — not thyroid cancer patients, and their low TSH was not caused by a tablet. Ten-year cumulative incidence was 28% in the lowest TSH band against 11% in those with normal values, adjusted relative risk 3.1 (95% confidence interval 1.7 to 5.5); the mildly low band showed no difference at all[9]. Cancer patients have their own data: Korean national records, 113,791 people with differentiated thyroid cancer against 455,188 age- and sex-matched controls, hazard ratio 2.07 for new atrial fibrillation (95% confidence interval 1.98 to 2.17)[10]. But that compares having this disease against not having it, not being suppressed against not being suppressed — in the same paper, people who had only a lobectomy came out at 1.93 (1.72 to 2.15). You cannot book the whole of 2.07 to suppression.

Cardiovascular death comes with two warnings pointing opposite ways. One: 524 patients with differentiated thyroid cancer against 1,572 matched controls from the same region, adjusted hazard ratio for cardiovascular death 3.35 (95% confidence interval 1.66 to 6.74); within the patient group, each tenfold fall in the geometric mean TSH gave an adjusted hazard ratio of 3.08 (1.32 to 7.21)[11]. Two: 705,307 US veterans on thyroid hormone, median age 67, 88.7% male, the overwhelming majority not thyroid cancer patients. At the suppressed end, adjusted hazard ratio for cardiovascular death 1.39 (95% confidence interval 1.32 to 1.47); at the other end, where people are taking too little and TSH climbs above normal, 2.67 (2.55 to 2.80)[12]. Cutting your own dose because you are frightened of taking too much is the thing this paragraph exists to stop.

One cohort where both sides were counted on the same people

One study put benefit and cost on the same patients, which is why it is worth more than the rest. 771 patients with low- and intermediate-risk differentiated thyroid cancer (the three-tier stratification of the time), all of whom had a total thyroidectomy, median follow-up 6.5 years: structural recurrence in 43 (5.6%), postoperative osteoporosis in 29 (3.9%, women only), postoperative atrial fibrillation in 17 (2.3%). Split at the median TSH into a suppressed and a non-suppressed group, recurrence was identical (hazard ratio 1.02, confidence interval 0.54 to 1.91); atrial fibrillation and osteoporosis taken together came out at 2.1 (1.001 to 4.3); osteoporosis in women, 3.5 (1.2 to 10.2)[13]. In this box of patients, pushing harder bought nothing, and the cost was measurable.

Why people who had half the gland out so often end up on the tablet anyway

Plenty of people are told a lobectomy means no tablets, and are taking one a few months later. Nobody lied to them. Where the target is set decides it. The guideline lays the two proportions of patients who avoid thyroid hormone side by side under the two different goals, and the gap is wide; those two proportions and what sits behind them are in 〈Half the gland, or all of it〉[2].

What it looks like in practice: among 115 patients with low-risk differentiated thyroid cancer who had a therapeutic lobectomy and no radioiodine, 97 (84%) had a postoperative TSH above the threshold that study used; 66 of them (68%) started levothyroxine, at a median of 74 days after surgery, and 77% had the dose adjusted at least once[14]. That was an era still working to the older, lower target. So "do I need the tablet" is really the question "where has my target been set".

How to ask about the dose

Get these four sentences out at your next appointment:

  • "Which response category am I in right now? Can that change?"
  • "Is the goal you set for me inside the normal range, or below it? Why that side?"
  • "If it is below normal, how long are we planning to keep it there? When do we look at that again?"
  • "If I get palpitations or cannot sleep, should I cut the dose, or come in for a blood test first?"

One last thing. The way this tablet most often goes wrong is not people taking too much. It is people feeling unwell, stopping or halving it themselves, and then not coming back for months. In that 705,307-person dataset, the cost at the too-little end was larger than the cost at the too-much end. If you feel bad, come and say so. Do not adjust it on your own.

From the physician

Read these out at your next appointment: "Which response category am I in?" and "Is the goal you set for me inside the normal range or below it, and why that side?" And the one that matters most — if you feel unwell, come and say so before you cut the dose or stop. In the data, the cost at the too-little end is larger than at the too-much end.
