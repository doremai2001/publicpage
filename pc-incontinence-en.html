SIDE EFFECTS AND LIVING WITH THEM

Why continence rates never agree

Three continence figures from the same men, the same day. None is false — and none means anything without its definition.

Prostate Cancer Guide · Side Effects and Living With Them · 16 references
#Prostate#Urinary Function#Side Effects#Quality of Life#NHI Coverage

Before you compare two percentages, ask which ruler each one used. That single question changes what the rest of the conversation is worth.

"Will I leak?" is the second question I am asked most often in prostate clinic, behind "do I actually need treatment". I cannot answer with one percentage, because every "continence rate" you find measures a different thing. So: definitions first, numbers second.

The same men, the same day, three answers

The five-year patient-reported results from PACE-B are what I keep on hand: the same men after radiotherapy, same questionnaire, same time point, asked "do you leak?" under three definitions: counting no leakage at all (leak-free), it was 64% (164/258) in the stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) group and 69% (172/249) in the conventional and moderately hypofractionated group; counting needing no pad of any kind (pad-free), 91% (233/257) and 90% (225/250); and asking whether leakage was a moderate or big problem, 6% (15/250) and 4% (9/244)[1].

64%, 91%, 6% — none of the three is false. They answer three different questions: has a drop ever escaped, do you need to wear something, does it interfere with your life. When two articles quote figures that will not reconcile, usually neither made anything up — neither said which ruler it used. Most men care about the last two: do I pad up for work, will I risk a day out.

On the surgical side, one judgement call halves the number

The systematic review pooling 51 studies of robot-assisted radical prostatectomy runs into the same thing. Defined as using no pad at all, incontinence at 12 months ran from 4% to 31%, mean 16%; same literature, same 12 months, defined as no pad, or one safety pad, it becomes 8% to 11%, mean 9%[2]. One decision — does a safety pad count as incontinence — halves the figure.

ProtecT is the only dataset that put three strategies in one randomised design, kept one definition throughout and followed to twelve years — leaking enough to need a pad. Among 1,643 randomised men, years 7 to 12: 18% to 24% of the surgery group needed pads, 9% to 11% of the active monitoring group, and 3% to 8% of the radiotherapy group[3]. Its six-year conclusion: surgery had the largest negative effect on urinary control and, despite partial recovery, stayed worse than the other two throughout, while radiotherapy had very little effect[4]. The full comparison is in "Surgery or radiotherapy: different bills".

"Radiotherapy makes you incontinent too" is a misreading

The urinary problem after radiotherapy is not the same object. After surgery it is stress leakage — it escapes when you cough, lift, stand up. After radiotherapy it is irritative: urgency, frequency, nocturia. ProtecT's twelve-year nocturia figures (up twice or more a night) make the point: 48% after radiotherapy, 34% after surgery — on this item surgery did better[3]. Where that irritation goes over time is in "That 13.7% is being read wrong".

One year, five years, twelve years — where the gap ends up

Does the gap close? Partly. In the PCOS cohort of 1,655 men the odds ratio for incontinence, surgery against radiotherapy, was 6.22 (1.92 to 20.29) at year 2, 5.10 (2.29 to 11.36) at year 5, no longer significantly different at year 15[5]. An odds ratio is not "so many times the probability" — it is the ratio of two "with against without" odds, and when the event is not rare it looks worse than the real difference in risk. Half of that closing is not surgery improving but the other two declining with age, and at year 12 ProtecT's gap in pad use is still there[3]. So I will not say "it settles down after a year". Most of the improvement happens inside the first year, then slows, and some men never get back to where they started.

I still teach the pelvic floor exercises, and I still will not say they stop the leaking

MAPS is the cleanest randomised trial here. British, men still leaking six weeks after surgery, one group given four one-to-one therapist sessions inside three months, the other education and advice. In the prostatectomy arm of 391 men, the proportion still leaking at 12 months was 76% (148/196) with the intervention and 77% (151/195) in the control group, an absolute risk difference of −1.9% (95% confidence interval −10 to 6): no difference[6]. The 2023 Cochrane update, 25 randomised trials and 3,079 men, is as blunt: "despite a total of 25 trials, the value of conservative management for urinary incontinence after prostate surgery remains uncertain"[7].

The other side goes in too. A 2023 meta-analysis of 11 randomised trials, with regaining continence as the endpoint, put the odds ratio for pelvic floor training at 2.71 (1.86 to 3.94) at one month and 1.21 (1.11 to 1.31) at twelve months[8]. What stands up: it may bring the first six months forward a little, the gap at a year is small, and the largest trial did not see even the early effect.

So why teach it? It costs nothing, risks nothing, and is one of the few things a man can do himself. In clinic I say: find the muscle first — imagine holding in wind, or stop the stream halfway to locate it (once; do not make a habit of it). Keep the abdomen, thighs and buttocks relaxed so only the pelvic floor works; let go when you cannot hold it, rest as long as you contracted, and stop before anything trembles. It turns on finding the right muscle, so have a therapist confirm it once — plenty of men practise by feel, and practise the wrong thing.

Who leaks more — some of it is fixed, some of it is a question to ask before surgery

Preoperative membranous urethral length is a fairly reliable predictor. A meta-analysis of 30 studies and 11,239 men split men at the median: the longer membranous urethra was associated with better continence at 12 months, pooled relative risk 1.30 (95% confidence interval 1.18 to 1.44), study medians running between 10.4 and 17.3 millimetres. The authors say explicitly that the association held whichever definition of continence was used[9]. So this one does not move with the definition, and it is measurable on your MRI before surgery.

The treatment chapter of the EAU prostate cancer guideline records that bilateral nerve-sparing improves recovery of urinary control at 12 months (relative risk 1.08), and, on a self-reported questionnaire definition of leaking, 21.3% still incontinent 12 months after robot-assisted radical prostatectomy and 20.2% after open retropubic prostatectomy[10]. Those two are not on the "no pad" ruler, so they cannot be subtracted from the 9% above. Together they say: nerve-sparing helps, and it is not "then you will not leak".

One more sentence I say every time: the robot will not stop you leaking. The Brisbane trial in Australia randomised 326 men and found no significant difference in the EPIC urinary domain, robot-assisted against open, at 6, 12 or 24 months (91.33 against 90.86 at 24)[11]. The rest is in "Is the robot worth what it costs you".

Still leaking after a year: two routes in Taiwan, and both are covered

MASTER is the only large randomised trial here: 380 British men with urodynamically proven stress incontinence after prostate surgery, randomised to a sling or an artificial urinary sphincter. At 12 months the sling was non-inferior (absolute risk difference −0.034, 95% confidence interval −0.117 to 0.048)[12]. It held at 24 months, with one further number you should have: reoperation within two years, 20 men (11%) in the sling group and 4 (2%) in the sphincter group. The authors write that "almost all other outcomes indicated better results for men receiving the artificial urinary sphincter", and that most men were satisfied despite not being completely dry[13]. Non-inferior means much the same, not better.

In Taiwan, artificial urinary sphincter implantation is National Health Insurance (健保) code 78038B at 12,352 points[14]; the sphincter itself is special material FUS0100411SB at 162,052 points a set and the male urethral sling FSP72STMS142 at 100,000 points a set[15]. What decides whether you can use them is the coverage rule. For the sphincter (D113-1) the first condition is "incontinence persisting for nine to twelve months after trauma or surgery", the rest being failure of conservative treatment, no urinary tract obstruction, no infection, normal bladder function, no vesicoureteric reflux, no residual urine, and a patient willing and able to operate it — with prior notification. For the male sling (D113-3) the text reads "for the treatment of stress urinary incontinence occurring after prostate cancer surgery in men, excluding cases that are T3 or have distant metastases", and the sixth of its seven conditions is "having first received six months of non-invasive treatment for stress urinary incontinence, with documentation in the medical record", with bladder capacity above 250 mL, residual urine below 50 mL, maximum flow 15 mL or more, and no bladder neck or urethral stricture[16].

What that means in practice: "wait a year, try conservative treatment first" is not only clinical advice in Taiwan, it is a condition of coverage, and it has to be in your notes. If you are heading for a sling under National Health Insurance, get the behavioural therapy, bladder training, biofeedback or medication into the record at every visit from now on, rather than reconstructing it in month twelve. The text I quote comes from the special materials coverage rules in the Executive Yuan Gazette; that file dates from 2016 and the wording may have been revised, so have your case manager (個管師) or the hospital billing office (醫務課) check the current version.

Three sentences: one for before surgery, two for afterwards

The first: "that percentage you just quoted — which definition, completely dry or not needing a pad?" That one sentence makes the rest of the conversation useful.

The second, if you are still deciding about surgery: "what is my membranous urethral length, and can the nerves be spared?" Part of that is fixed by the imaging and the operative plan before you go in.

The third, if you are already leaking: "is my conservative treatment documented, and when should I be referred to discuss a sling or an artificial sphincter?" The coverage rule says nine to twelve months of persistence, so month nine after surgery is exactly the right time to ask.

From the physician

What matters to you is probably not “have I ever leaked a drop” but “do I pad up for work”. Say which one you mean, and ask for the figure under that definition. If you are already leaking, get the conservative treatment written into your notes now — under National Health Insurance it is a coverage condition, not just advice.
