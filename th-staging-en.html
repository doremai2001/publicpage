DIAGNOSIS AND STAGING

Why the stage depends on your age

Stage is built for death, and the guideline says outright that it does not adequately predict recurrence; the reverse sentence it never wrote. The two labels still do not translate into each other.

Thyroid Cancer Guide · Diagnosis and staging · 8 references
#Staging#Evidence#Decisions#Recurrence

People handed "stage II" go and look up how long that stage lives. But in the AJCC eighth edition, stage II under 55 means distant metastases by definition; and among reports all saying eighth edition stage I, layering on risk group and age spreads ten-year disease-specific survival from one hundred per cent down to eighty-nine.

People handed a report usually ask what stage they are, then look up how long that stage lives. Thyroid cancer is the worst cancer to look up that way, because its staging table contains something no other cancer's does — your age.

This article explains two things: why 55 appears in a staging table at all, and why your report carries two grouping labels that are not the same kind of label. Histological type and subtype are in 〈Papillary, follicular, NIFTP: reading the report〉; what the risk group changes about your management is in 〈Whether to have radioiodine〉 and 〈The tablet you take for life, and how much〉. Treatment is not in this one.

Staging answers one question, and it is death

That is not my metaphor, it is the guideline's own sentence. The 2025 American Thyroid Association guideline for differentiated thyroid cancer says outright that AJCC and UICC staging is designed to predict disease-specific survival and therefore does not predict the overall risk of structural persistence or recurrence[1]. The 2015 edition put it even more plainly: because the AJCC/TNM mortality-risk staging system does not adequately predict recurrence risk in differentiated thyroid cancer, the 2009 edition of these guidelines proposed a separate recurrence risk stratification[2].

The two systems are given separate justifications inside the same document. The 2015 edition's recommendation 47 asks for staging in every patient with differentiated thyroid cancer, on the stated grounds of its usefulness in predicting disease mortality and the requirements of cancer registries (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence); recommendation 48A asks for recurrence risk stratification in patients who have had a thyroidectomy, on the stated grounds of its usefulness in predicting risk of disease recurrence or persistence (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence)[2]. The 2025 edition's counterpart is recommendation 28A, likewise a strong recommendation with moderate certainty evidence, and its stated purpose runs longer than the usual paraphrase: to determine the risk of structural disease persistence or recurrence, locoregionally and/or distantly, and/or survival[1]. "And/or survival" tends to fall off in the retelling. It is in the text.

So the asymmetry between the two systems holds in one direction only. Stage is built for death, and the guideline says outright that it is not enough to predict recurrence — which is exactly why a separate recurrence stratification was made. The recurrence system's main job is structural recurrence, and the guideline's own recommendation extends it to survival as well. The tidy symmetrical version — one ruler for death, one for recurrence — is a sentence the guideline never wrote, so I will not write it either.

The line at 55, and the 45 that came before it

The AJCC eighth edition took effect in 2018 and raised the staging age cutoff from 45 to 55[1]. The 2015 edition's staging table was still the seventh edition, with the cutoff written as 45[2]; the 2025 edition is the first differentiated thyroid cancer guideline from this association built on the eighth[1]. Hold data from before 2018 against a report written now and it will not line up. So every stage number in this article carries its edition.

The eighth edition rule most often misread is this one: all patients younger than 55 have stage I disease unless they have distant metastases, in which case their disease is stage II[3]. Now read it backwards — if someone under 55 is given "stage II", that means, by definition, that distant metastases are already present. "Only stage II" does not exist here.

Above 55 the arrangement is different: tumour confined to the thyroid with no metastases is stage I; add lymph node metastases, or a tumour larger than 4 cm, or gross invasion of the strap muscles, and it is stage II; gross invasion of subcutaneous soft tissue, larynx, trachea, oesophagus or recurrent laryngeal nerve is stage III; invasion of prevertebral fascia or encasement of the carotid artery is stage IVA; distant metastases are stage IVB[3]. Two further eighth edition changes: extrathyroidal extension seen only under the microscope no longer counts toward the definition of T3, and lymph node metastases no longer push a patient automatically into stage III[1].

Stage II in the seventh edition and in the eighth are not the same people

A meta-analysis of 6 studies and 10,850 patients with differentiated thyroid cancer, median follow-up 55 to 148 months, sorted the same people twice, once by each edition[4]. The whole distribution shifted forward: stage I from 60% to 81%, stage II from 5% to 13%, stage III from 21% to 2%, stage IV from 10% to 3%.

Ten-year disease-specific survival came out like this: seventh edition stage II 98.1% (95% confidence interval 96.6–99.6) against eighth edition stage II 88.6% (82.7–94.6); seventh edition stage III 96.8% (94.1–99.64) against eighth edition stage III 70.5% (59.1–83.9)[4].

Almost ten percentage points fell away, and not because the patients got worse — because the people packed into the box labelled "stage II" were swapped out. The eighth edition moved a great many patients forward out of stages III and IV (the guideline's own figure is that 20% to 30% of patients are downstaged relative to the seventh edition), and what stayed behind in stages II and III was the more serious end[1].

The other system: recurrence risk, and it changed editions too

The 2015 edition's recurrence risk stratification had three tiers: low, intermediate, high. The 2025 edition has four, and defines them straight off the recurrence probability: low (under 10%), low-intermediate (10% to 15%), intermediate-high (16% to 30%), high (over 30%)[1]. The guideline states its reason for splitting: not every criterion inside the old intermediate box predicted the same recurrence risk. The later articles use these four names the same way.

I have to be honest about something: I did not obtain the full entry criteria for each box of the 2025 four-tier system — in the guideline it is a figure, and the extractable text gives only the four percentage bands. The recurrence rates attached to each box are numbers the guideline took from other people's papers; I have not gone back to those papers, so I am not printing them. To show what puts a person in which box, all I can cite is the 2015 table: low risk requires no aggressive subtype, no vascular invasion, no clinical lymph node metastases; intermediate includes microscopic invasion into perithyroidal soft tissue, aggressive histology, papillary carcinoma with vascular invasion, and clinically apparent lymph node metastases; high includes gross extrathyroidal extension, incomplete resection, distant metastases, involved nodes 3 cm or larger, and follicular carcinoma with extensive vascular invasion[2].

What you see when the two systems are laid over each other

A registry of 4,881 adults under 55 with differentiated thyroid cancer, first treated between 1980 and 2016, median follow-up 6.6 years, 122 (2.5%) dead of the disease. Cross AJCC eighth edition stage with the 2015 three-tier recurrence risk and with age, and 10-year disease-specific survival looks like this — note that this table's endpoint is survival: it uses recurrence risk to sharpen the survival estimate[5]: stage I plus low recurrence risk, 100%; stage I plus intermediate, 98%; stage I plus high risk, under 45, 95%; stage I plus high risk, 45 to 55, 89%; stage II plus high risk, under 45, 78%; stage II plus high risk, 45 to 55, 61%.

Two reports both reading "eighth edition, stage I", and the range is 100% down to 89%. Both reading "eighth edition, stage II", and it is 78% down to 61%. The authors' own conclusion: the eighth edition reclassified up to a third of patients into the younger prognostic stage groups, widening the spectrum of disease inside those lower stages; those optimistic estimates may not reflect the prognosis of an individual patient carrying high-risk features[5].

The eighth edition sorts recurrence better too, but that is a different endpoint

A registry-based study of 3,248 patients disease-free after treatment, with recurrence as the endpoint, had 110 (3%) recur at a median of 27 months. Sorted by the seventh edition, the hazard ratio for stage II against stage I was 0.91 (95% confidence interval 0.39–2.11) — that is, essentially no difference; switch to the eighth edition and stage II's hazard ratio becomes 4.06 (2.38–6.93)[6].

That is good news, but please do not stir it into the same sentence as the survival figures above. That endpoint was death. This endpoint is recurrence.

Staging systems never explained very much

The 2015 edition contains a rarely quoted line: the staging systems designed to predict death from thyroid cancer account for only 5% to 30% of the uncertainty, and the recurrence risk systems for 19% to 34%[2]. Even with stage and risk group both settled, most of the uncertainty is still outside.

So I do not deliver a stage as a conclusion. It is a starting point.

Between the two axes I draw no arrow

Your report will carry two labels: a stage and a recurrence risk group. They do not translate into each other — "stage I" is not "low risk", and "intermediate-high risk" is not "stage III". A stage label is not a recurrence probability, and a risk tier is not a probability of dying. They are sorted by different rules, and they are read together, not swapped for each other.

The "98% five-year survival for thyroid cancer" you see everywhere usually traces to American statistics — the 2017 US Preventive Services Task Force document cites 98.1%[7]. That is all histological types, all stages and all ages together. It can describe a country. It cannot describe your box.

As for stage-specific numbers for Taiwan, I have to be straight: in the national cancer registry report I obtained, thyroid cancer has no stage distribution and no survival by stage[8]. No such line item exists. So "how long does stage such-and-such live in Taiwan" is something I cannot give you, and I will not substitute foreign numbers for it.

Ask about the two labels separately

Take the report and read four sentences. First: "Which AJCC edition was my stage assigned under?" Second: "Besides the stage, which recurrence risk box am I in, and is that the three-tier or the four-tier system?" Third: "Which feature put me in that box — size, invasion, or nodes?" Fourth: "What does that risk group change about what happens next?" And if you are under 55 and your report says stage II, there is a fifth you must ask: "Is that because there are distant metastases? Where are they?"

From the physician

Copy the two labels off your report onto two separate lines: one for the stage and the AJCC edition it used, one for the recurrence risk group and whether it is the three-tier or four-tier system. Kept apart, they stop you reading a stage as a recurrence probability, or a risk tier as a probability of dying.
