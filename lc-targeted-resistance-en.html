HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

Preparing for the day the targeted drug stops working

Resistance does not mean the drug is useless, and it does not mean switching today. What to ask on the day the report comes back.

Lung Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 15 references
#Lung#Targeted Therapy#Recurrence#Biomarkers#Decisions

Nothing in this piece is a reason to change what you are taking. The most dangerous thing in it is not the cancer's next move; it is a decision made at home, in the days before anyone else knows about it.

While a targeted drug is working, clinic is a quiet place. Quiet enough that many people hear the word "resistance" for the first time on the day a report comes back, and the instinctive reaction that day is usually one of two things: this drug has stopped working, and — should I stop taking it. Neither is right, and the second one can cause harm.

The most dangerous thing goes first

A 2011 dataset of 61 patients followed people who had been asked to stop their targeted drug in order to enter a trial. 14 of them (23%, 95% confidence interval 14–35) had an explosive flare of their disease after stopping — defined as hospitalisation or death from disease progression — at a median of 8 days after stopping, range 3 to 21 days. Risk was higher in people with pleural or central nervous system disease, and it had no relationship to the resistance genotype, meaning there is no way to predict in advance who it happens to[1]. That data is old and the drugs were first-generation, but what it means has not changed.

So treat these as hard rules: do not stop the drug on your own, do not reduce the dose on your own, do not change once daily into every other day on your own, and do not add supplements or Chinese medicine on your own. Not because those things are necessarily bad, but because drugs interact with one another, and your team needs to know everything that is going on inside you. If you want to add something, say so first, so that it becomes something somebody knows about. If a side effect is more than you can carry, the answer is the same: say it, and let the person adjusting things be your doctor.

What "resistance" actually means

A prospective study biopsied the same patients twice, before treatment and after progression, and obtained 52 paired next-generation sequencing (NGS) results. The commonest changes at progression were MET amplification 17%, CDKN2A/2B deletion 15%, MTAP deletion 13% and EGFR C797S mutation 13% — not one mechanism accounted for as much as a fifth. What the investigators emphasise is that resistance is often more than one thing at once, and that different lesions in the same person may differ[2].

Another randomised trial looked at the same question with blood-based sequencing: MET amplification 13.1%, secondary EGFR mutations 7.6%. One result in that analysis is worth remembering: patients in whom no known resistance mechanism was found in blood at progression had longer progression-free survival on the next line of treatment (7.4 against 4.6 months, hazard ratio 0.63)[3]. A hazard ratio compares how fast progression accrues over the same stretch of time; it is not somebody's probability multiplied by 0.63, and the two month-figures in front of it are what to carry away. The 17% and the 13.1% do not contradict each other — one comes from tissue and one from blood, and they are measuring different things to begin with.

Progression on a scan is the start of a series of questions: whether to re-biopsy, whether to treat one particular lesion locally, whether to change systemic treatment, whether there is a trial. It is not a single sentence reading "time to switch drugs", and still less "this drug is useless now".

There is one kind of resistance a blood test cannot see

Cancer cells do not only change their genes; they change their form. In a paired-tissue study of 62 patients who progressed after first-line third-generation targeted therapy, 15% had a change in histological type, mostly to squamous cell carcinoma; these patients had shorter time on treatment and worse survival[4]. Harder still is transformation to small cell carcinoma. In 67 cases from eight hospitals, the median time to transformation was 17.8 months; after transformation there was often still a response to platinum-based chemotherapy and taxanes, but of the 17 patients given immunotherapy not one responded; median survival from the moment of transformation was 10.9 months, and brain metastases were common afterwards[5]. Small cell lung cancer runs on completely different treatment logic, set out in "Small cell lung cancer: speed is its only rule".

This section matters because a change of histological type is entirely invisible to a blood test. It is not a question of blood being less sensitive; it cannot be seen in principle.

Re-biopsy or blood test, and what a negative report cannot rule out

The advantages of blood testing are clearest at first diagnosis: the report is quicker and there is no second needle, and the full figures for that stage are in "Those two weeks of waiting are not wasted"[6]. After progression the question is different, because the weaknesses are magnified at this point in time. Against tissue testing as the standard, the sensitivity of blood for detecting T790M is 70% — three in ten are missed — and the authors of that study put their conclusion bluntly: given the 30% false-negative rate of plasma genotyping, patients with a negative plasma result still require a tissue biopsy for confirmation[7]. It runs the other way too: a variant detected in blood does not necessarily come from the tumour, and clonal haematopoiesis arising from white cells produces false positives[8].

So what a negative liquid biopsy report can say is "we did not detect it this time". What it cannot rule out is: a resistance mutation present but undetected, a change confined to one lesion, and the whole business of histological transformation above.

Nor am I going to write "you must have a re-biopsy". The prospective study above enrolled 154 patients and ended up with usable paired tissue in only 52 — getting the specimen is itself the hard part[2]. Where the lesion sits, how big it is, your clotting and your lung function all bear on whether it can be done and whether it is worth doing. This is a clinical judgement, and the question to ask is: with my lesions, can a biopsy actually reach one, and what is the risk?

ALK behaves differently, and the difference is useful

In 103 specimens re-biopsied after progression: among people who progressed on a first-generation ALK inhibitor, only 20% had an ALK resistance mutation; among those who progressed after a second-generation drug, 56% did. And in cell-line experiments, cells carrying an ALK resistance mutation responded to newer drugs while those without one did not[9]. EGFR data cannot be transferred onto ALK or ROS1, and the reverse is equally true.

What the next line looks like, and the place where I have to put the brakes on myself

A randomised trial after progression on a third-generation targeted drug enrolled 657 patients: platinum-based chemotherapy alone gave median progression-free survival of 4.2 months and a response rate of 36%; the two arms adding a newer antibody gave 8.3 and 6.3 months with response rates of 63% and 64%. The price was a marked increase in haematological toxicity, and the trial amended its dosing sequence partway through[10]. Progress with new drugs along the MET route is covered more fully on this site in "When the targeted drug stops working, which road do you take?", so I will not repeat it.

The next passage is where my own specialty most easily crosses a line. A randomised trial took patients with progression at only a few sites and randomised them to add or not add stereotactic radiotherapy; in the lung cancer group (59 patients), median progression-free survival was 10.0 against 2.2 months (hazard ratio 0.41). But the same paper states that 51 of those 59 patients (86%) had no targetable driver mutation[11]. Which means that someone taking a targeted drug with only one or two sites progressing is precisely the group this trial did not enrol. Quoting that number at you as evidence that adding radiotherapy will buy you time would be me selling something I should not sell. It is worth discussing. It has to be discussed as something not yet demonstrated.

In Taiwan, whether to switch was never yours alone to decide

In the National Health Insurance (健保) drug reimbursement rules (藥品給付規定), third-generation EGFR targeted therapy is restricted to first-line use in locally invasive or metastatic lung adenocarcinoma with an Exon 19 deletion or Exon 21 L858R mutation, or second-line use after failure of a first- or second-generation targeted drug with a T790M mutation. Prior authorisation is required before use, each course is capped at three months, and a fresh application must be made every three months, with imaging and other clinical data attached to show there has been no progression; each prescription is capped at 4 weeks, with a full response assessment every 8 to 12 weeks; if the disease progresses or recurs, or unacceptable toxicity develops, no further application may be made. First-line use and the other EGFR targeted drugs may only be used one at a time and may not be swapped except for intolerance[12]. Another third-generation drug was brought under the same first-line indication and the same review mechanism from 1 August 2026 (115 年 8 月 1 日)[13]. The ALK drugs are structured similarly. Verified 2026-08-30.

Translated into plain language: whether to continue and whether to switch is, in Taiwan, a formal process that has to be re-applied for with imaging every three months. It is not something that can be completed at home, and it is not something you can work out by skipping a few days.

On testing: next-generation sequencing came under NHI coverage on 1 May 2024 (113 年 5 月 1 日), with non-small cell lung cancer inside the covered scope, priced as several different panels, and covered once per person per cancer type for life[14]. The insurer's own question-and-answer document states that patients "with disease progression after first-line targeted therapy" are not bound by the precondition of a prior negative single-gene EGFR test[15]. That document is the August 2024 (113 年 8 月) version and flags its own forthcoming revision, so your own eligibility and co-payment should be confirmed with your case manager (個管師) or the hospital billing office (醫務課). As for the reimbursement status of several newer drugs, I could not find it in the current rules — that is "I could not find it", not "I found that it is not covered". Out-of-pocket costs and trial opportunities are also things your case manager can look up for you.

On the day the progression report arrives, take these into the room

First, is this progression happening everywhere, or at only one or two sites? Second, in my situation, should this be a tissue biopsy or a blood test, and on what reasoning? Third, if the blood report comes back negative, what happens next? Fourth, is there a clinical trial that fits me? Until those questions have answers, the drug goes on being taken and the clinic visits go on happening. New headache, double vision or unsteadiness is handled separately, and that is in "Brain metastases are not the end".

From the physician

Do not stop, reduce or reschedule the drug yourself, and say what supplement or Chinese medicine you are considering before you start it. Progression on a scan opens a set of questions — tissue or blood, one site or many, which trial — and in Taiwan those run through a reimbursement application made afresh every three months. Keep taking the drug and keep the appointments while they are answered.
