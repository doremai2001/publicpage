PITUITARY ADENOMA

Where endonasal surgery and radiotherapy fit

The randomised evidence propping up the endoscope comes to fewer than seventy people across two trials; and on the radiation side, what steadies on the scan within months often takes several more years to show up in the biochemistry.

The radiation half of this is the part I deliver myself, which is why every comparison here comes with its primary source, including the ones that do not flatter my side of it — finishing the radiotherapy is not the end, because hypopituitarism accumulates year on year and reaches 45% by fifteen years.

When something has to be done about a pituitary adenoma, there are only two roads: take it out through the nose, or irradiate it. Each road has its own strengths and its own bill, and in Taiwan's clauses there is an explicit order between them as well. Three things have to be compared: how much can be removed, whether the hormone comes down, and what the price is.

My position first: stereotactic radiosurgery and fractionated radiotherapy are the part I deliver myself; the other side of every comparison in this topic is my neurosurgical colleagues' operations, my neuro-otology colleagues' hearing assessments, and my endocrinology colleagues' drugs. That is why every comparison here comes with its primary source, so you can take it to neurosurgery, to neuro-otology and to endocrinology for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

Endoscopic against microscopic: this is the actual shape of the evidence

Endoscopic endonasal transsphenoidal surgery is already what most centres do, but the evidence holding it up is worth looking at properly. There are only two published prospective randomised trials. One had 33 people and compared pure endoscopic surgery with endoscope-assisted microscopic surgery over a mean follow-up of 6.3 years; in the microscopic arm, 7 of 15 people (46.7%) turned out on endoscopic inspection to have residual tumour the microscope had not seen, but length of stay, complication rate (16.7% against 20.0%) and nasal symptom scores showed no significant difference[1]. The other had 36 people, single-blind randomised, and was terminated early because accrual was too slow; all 3 cases of new hypopituitarism were in the microscopic arm, and quality of life did not differ between the arms[2]. Sixty-nine people between them, and one of the two never finished.

What actually holds this up is observational data: a meta-analysis of 56 comparative studies and 26,295 people found a higher gross total resection rate with the endoscope, relative risk 1.08 (95% confidence interval 1.02 to 1.15) — a relative risk of 1.08 means the probability is 1.08 times that in the comparison group, not 8 percentage points more taken out, and a confidence interval is the range the true value plausibly sits in; operating time did not differ, intraoperative blood loss was about 44 mL more with the endoscope, and cerebrospinal fluid leak, epistaxis, meningitis, diabetes insipidus and postoperative visual change were comparable[3]. But another meta-analysis, this one specific to Cushing's disease (97 papers, 6,695 people), gives the opposite answer on one of those same complications: cerebrospinal fluid leak was distinctly commoner with the endoscope (12.9% against 4.0%), while transient diabetes insipidus was less common (11.3% against 21.7%)[4]. Two meta-analyses reach opposite conclusions on the same complication — I am leaving both on the table and smoothing neither of them over.

"The operation sorted it" — but "sorted" means something different for every type

In that Cushing's disease meta-analysis, across 6,695 people overall remission was 80% and recurrence about 10%, with the two techniques broadly comparable overall; the difference only shows up on stratifying by size — for microadenomas the results were comparable, while for macroadenomas the endoscope had the higher remission rate (76.3% against 59.9%) and the lower recurrence (1.5% against 17.0%). The authors add a sentence of their own: these are all cohort data, and "confounding cannot be excluded"[4].

Acromegaly has different denominators. A prospective longitudinal cohort of 659 people (85% macroadenomas, 35% invasive, median follow-up 51 months), judged against the strict 2010 consensus criteria (both growth hormone and IGF-1 have to be pushed below the threshold), gave immediate postoperative remission of 37% and long-term remission of 69%, with endoscopic surgery and absence of cavernous sinus invasion the independent predictors of long-term remission[5]. Data on 282 people from three Dutch centres add another way of saying it: every additional millimetre of maximum tumour diameter lowers the chance of both early and long-term biochemical remission a little further[6]. When a prolactinoma does reach the point of surgery, the figures the consensus quotes are immediate postoperative biochemical remission of 90% to 100% for well-circumscribed microadenomas and 66.7% to 81.0% for Knosp grade 0 to 1 macroadenomas[7]; when that point is reached is in "Prolactinoma: the drug comes first, not the knife".

What you have to watch after the operation is the water

Diabetes insipidus is mostly temporary. In a series of 881 transsphenoidal operations, among the 857 who did not have diabetes insipidus beforehand, 18.3% developed it immediately after surgery, 12.4% needed medication for it at some point during the admission, and only 2% had permanent diabetes insipidus genuinely requiring long-term treatment. The strongest risk factor was a visible intraoperative cerebrospinal fluid leak — and, counter-intuitively, transient diabetes insipidus was commoner after microadenomas than after macroadenomas (21.6% against 14.3%)[8].

The other thing happens after you have gone home. The window for delayed hyponatraemia is the third to the fourteenth day after surgery. A single-centre randomised trial of 300 people split them into free fluid intake, moderate restriction and strict restriction: hyponatraemia overall ran at 31.9%, 28.2% and 21.0%; severe hyponatraemia at 7.4%, 5.1% and 0%; readmission at 6.4% against 1.6%[9]. Suddenly sleeping all the time once you are home, headache, nausea, speech that sounds odd — that is the time window in which to think of it. The emergency side of this is in "Pituitary apoplexy, and not enough hormone".

Radiation holds the tumour; the hormone comes down far more slowly

Start with non-functioning adenomas. International multicentre data on 869 people (median imaging follow-up 3.7 years) give tumour control after single-session stereotactic radiosurgery of 95.5% at five years and 88.8% at ten[10].

Tumours that secrete are another matter. International multicentre data on 371 people with acromegaly: actuarial rates at ten years — an actuarial rate is calculated from how long each person was actually followed, not from a simple headcount — of 69% for a first endocrine remission and 59% for durable remission, and the average time to reaching a durable remission was 38 months after the radiosurgery[11]. That three-year-odd gap has to be known in advance: the tumour steadies on imaging within a few months, but getting the biochemistry down inside the target range often takes several more years, and during that period the drugs do not stop straight away. Data on 46 people who had radiosurgery upfront without previous surgery say the same thing: five-year endocrine remission of 81% in Cushing's disease against only 28% in acromegaly, the authors concluding that this road can be considered for people with Cushing's disease who will not or cannot have surgery, and that its role in acromegaly is limited[12]. The fractionated radiotherapy data point the same way: among 116 people, ten-year control was 96% for non-secreting tumours and only 62% for secreting ones (clinical plus biochemical control)[13]. These figures come from different studies, different populations and different eras, so using them as a single-session-against-fractionated comparison is wrong; that question belongs to "One session, or fractionated".

The long-term price that matters most is the one that keeps accumulating

Hypopituitarism after radiotherapy is not a one-off risk. In multicentre data from 17 institutions and 1,023 people (410 non-functioning, 262 Cushing's disease, 251 acromegaly, median follow-up 51 months), 248 developed a new pituitary hormone deficiency, with actuarial incidence of 7.8% at one year, 16.2% at three, 22.4% at five and 31.3% at ten, and a median time to onset of 39 months; the axes lost most often were thyroid at 35.6%, gonadal at 24.3% and cortisol at 21.6%[14]. Single-centre data on 241 people with longer follow-up run the curve out to the end: 20% at five years, 39% at ten and 45% at fifteen, with a median time to onset of 44.1 months[15]. The Pituitary Society's 2021 update to its acromegaly guidelines puts two things in one sentence: long-term follow-up after radiosurgery and fractionated radiotherapy shows that "approximately half achieve and maintain biochemical control", while among people whose pituitary function was normal to begin with "up to one-third develop hypopituitarism"[16]. That is why finishing the radiotherapy is not the end of it, but a switch to blood tests at regular intervals for the rest of your life.

On the optic pathway: in a series of 218 radiosurgery treatments, 4 people (1.9%) developed radiation-induced optic neuropathy, at a median of 48 months after treatment; risk was 1.1% where the dose to the optic nerve was under 12 Gy, and distinctly higher in people who had had external beam radiotherapy previously or concurrently[17]. The population in that dataset was a mixture — 122 meningiomas, 89 pituitary adenomas, 7 craniopharyngiomas; thresholds of this kind are the background logic we work with when we draw a plan, not a table to hold your own plan up against. For protons, the most recent data I can cite here is a single-centre retrospective series of 22 people (five-year local control 100%); the paper itself calls this preliminary evidence with no control group, and it cannot be used to say protons are better than photons — that question is in "Protons: precise. Then what?"[18]. Whether irradiation grows a second tumour is in "Can radiotherapy grow a second tumour".

Taiwan's clause puts radiotherapy after the operation

In the current NHI fee schedule (the version effective 115.04.01) there are two codes for stereotactic radiosurgery: 37028B, three-dimensional X-knife stereotactic radiosurgery, at 82,000 points, and 37029B, image-guided intensity-modulated X-ray stereotactic radiosurgery, at 153,229 points. Both indication lists name pituitary tumour outright — but immediately afterwards comes "and one of the following conditions must be met": A is having had a craniotomy with residual or recurrent tumour, B is where a craniotomy would carry a risk of neurological injury or be dangerous, C is severe cardiopulmonary disease or other medical illness making invasive surgery or general anaesthesia unsuitable, and the list runs on to F. Both codes also state that "every case requires a prior case-by-case application to the insurer"[19] (the clauses are in Chinese; the wording here is my translation of them). The clause makes radiotherapy's official position quite explicit: after surgery, or where surgery cannot be done.

On the surgical side there is only one item in the whole table: 83057B, "resection of pituitary tumour via the sphenoid sinus", at 30,571 points, effective from 1 March 2022[20]. What has to be read carefully is this: the surgical item itself does not distinguish endoscopic from microscopic; what it distinguishes is whether the instruments used during the operation are billed separately — brain endoscope 28037B at 2,000 points and precision operating microscope 56019B at 2,000 points, each its own code. How your particular operation will be claimed, and whether there is a self-pay difference, is a question for the medical affairs office. Not every hospital offers these treatments; to have one, ask your own doctor where to be referred.

The two specialties will not give you the same answer, and that is the reason to hear both

Ask surgery: for a tumour of my size, with or without cavernous sinus invasion, what gross total resection rate and what biochemical remission rate do you expect, and which definition of remission are you using? Ask radiation oncology: where do the doses to my optic nerve and pituitary stalk fall, how long before the hormone could come down inside the target range, and what happens with the drugs in the meantime? The question to put to both sides is the same one: five and ten years from now, how is my pituitary function going to be followed, and who is responsible for following it? Write the answers on the same sheet of paper, and the difference between the two roads becomes visible.

From the physician

To choose between the two roads, get three numbers pinned down first: the expected gross total resection rate, which definition of remission is being used, and who is responsible for following your pituitary function five and ten years from now. Surgery and radiation oncology will not give you the same answers — and the difference is itself information, not somebody trying it on. Taiwan's clause is explicit too: every case of radiosurgery requires a prior case-by-case application.
