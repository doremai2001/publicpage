WHAT THE EVIDENCE SAYS

Older, or not strong enough: the course can be shortened

Shortening the course presupposes still treating, and there is a randomised trial on that question. What decides the length of the course is performance status and neurological function, not the year on your birth certificate.

Glioblastoma Guide · What the Evidence Says · 12 references
#Short Course#Decisions#Chemotherapy#Evidence

A short course is not a discounted treatment: in that randomised trial, half as many people in the three-week arm needed their steroid dose increased as in the six-week arm. This article lays five trials out in order, and says plainly which sentence only holds in glioblastoma in people aged 60 and over.

"He's in his seventies — does he really have to come in for six weeks?" I hear that in clinic most weeks, and usually a family member asks it first. The course can be shortened; that much is true. But something has to be said before we get to shortening, because if the order is reversed a lot of people go straight to "then let's not treat at all" — and there is a randomised trial that argues directly against that conclusion.

Whether to treat at all comes first, and that question has a randomised trial

In the New England Journal of Medicine in 2007, the French ANOCEF group randomised newly diagnosed patients over 70 who were still in reasonable condition (KPS 70 or above) either to supportive care alone or to supportive care plus 50 Gy of radiotherapy. Ten centres, 85 people randomised, 81 with glioblastoma analysed, median age 73. The trial was stopped at the first interim analysis because the effect crossed the pre-specified boundary: median survival 29.1 weeks in the radiotherapy arm against 16.9 weeks with supportive care alone, hazard ratio 0.47 (95% confidence interval 0.29 to 0.76), P=0.002[1]. A hazard ratio divides the speed at which deaths happened in the two groups, and 0.47 means deaths in the radiotherapy arm happened at less than half that speed — not "the probability becomes 0.47 times as big".

Two more sentences from the same paper I read out every time: no serious adverse events attributable to radiotherapy occurred, and the two arms showed no significant difference in quality of life or cognitive assessments over time[1]. Which is to say that the arm having radiotherapy was not trading quality of life for time. The 2020 Cochrane review pooled four randomised trials and 397 people and points the same way: death happened 2.01 times faster in the group given supportive care alone than in those given post-operative radiotherapy (95% confidence interval 1.58 to 2.55 — the bracket is the plausible range for the estimate, not your personal probability), on moderate-certainty evidence; and none of those trials recorded toxicity clearly attributable to the radiation[2]. How survival numbers should be read is in "That number, and how I am going to tell you about it".

Three weeks does not lose to six

The Canadian randomised trial in JCO in 2004 enrolled 100 post-operative glioblastoma patients aged 60 and over and compared 60 Gy in 30 fractions over six weeks with 40 Gy in 15 fractions over three weeks: median survival 5.1 against 5.6 months, P=0.57, six-month survival 44.7% against 41.7%[3]. No difference could be seen.

But the number in that paper that most deserves saying out loud is not survival, it is the steroids: among those who completed radiotherapy, the proportion needing an increase in their post-operative steroid dose was 49% in the standard six-week arm and 23% in the short three-week arm[3]. Half as many. A short course is not a discounted treatment; it genuinely is easier on the body, and using less steroid is a real difference for an older patient (those side effects are in "Steroids: the drug that saves you, and has to come down"). The words the authors chose for this regimen were that it "seems to be a reasonable treatment option", not that it is better[3].

The strength of the evidence has to be reported separately. Cochrane 2020 put hypofractionated against conventional fractionation across five trials and 943 people, hazard ratio 0.95, but rated the certainty of that evidence "very low"; restricted to the subgroup that matters here — glioblastoma in people aged 60 and over, two trials and 293 people — the hazard ratio is 1.16 (0.92 to 1.46) and the certainty of the evidence is "high"[2]. So the correct sentence is "in glioblastoma in people aged 60 and over, no difference in survival can be seen between the long and the short course, and the evidence for that one statement is high-grade". It cannot be stated as a general rule.

One week is shorter still, and the guideline left a reservation on it

The randomised trial run by the International Atomic Energy Agency in 2015 compared 25 Gy in 5 fractions over one week with 40 Gy in 15 fractions over three weeks. Look at who could enter: three kinds of patient were taken — aged 50 and over with poorer performance status (KPS 50 to 70), aged 65 and over with poorer performance status, and aged 65 and over with good performance status. In 98 people, median survival was 7.9 against 6.4 months, P=0.988, progression-free survival 4.2 months in both, and no difference in quality of life[4]. It is the only trial so far that has written "not old, but not strong enough" into its eligibility criteria.

Two limits belong with it. First, it had only 98 patients and a median follow-up of 6.3 months, and the authors' own wording is that the short regimen "can be recommended as a treatment option" because it takes less time[4]. Second, the EANO 2021 guideline attaches a specific reservation when it reaches this regimen: shortening further to 5 fractions of 5 Gy appears not to sacrifice survival, but if systemic treatment improves and older patients live longer, it may well cause neurocognitive adverse events[5]. The other side of that is in "Memory, concentration, and changes in who he is".

Drug only, no radiotherapy: an option under particular conditions

The Nordic trial put three regimens into the same randomisation: 342 people aged over 60, assigned to temozolomide alone, 34 Gy in 10 fractions over two weeks, or 60 Gy in 30 fractions over six weeks. Against standard six-week radiotherapy, the drug-only arm lived longer (8.3 against 6.0 months, hazard ratio 0.70, P=0.01); hypofractionated radiotherapy gave 7.5 months, the same direction, but it did not cross the line for statistical significance (P=0.24). Between the two short regimens themselves — drug only against hypofractionated radiotherapy, 242 people — no difference could be seen[6]. In the group over 70, both short regimens beat standard six-week radiotherapy, and what the authors themselves wrote is that standard radiotherapy was associated with poor outcomes, particularly in those older than 70[6]. What I want you to notice is what those results have in common: of the three regimens, the longest was not the best.

Another trial, NOA-08, took people aged 65 and over with KPS 60 or above and compared temozolomide alone against 60 Gy of radiotherapy alone. It was a non-inferiority design — that is, it set out to show one arm is not worse than the other by more than a pre-set margin, which is not the same as showing it is better. Median survival was 8.6 against 9.6 months, hazard ratio 1.09, non-inferiority P=0.033[7]. Read the direction carefully: 1.09 leans towards radiotherapy, and what this trial demonstrated is "the drug is not worse than radiotherapy beyond the pre-set boundary", not "the drug is better". The toxicity column matters too: lymphopenia in 46 against 1 patient, thromboembolic events in 24 against 8[7]. Brain tumour patients are already a high-risk group for deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism; which symptoms to recognise and when to come back the same day are in "When to come back the same day: three tiers".

The paper that lines all the regimens up is the 2020 Cochrane network meta-analysis (12 randomised trials, about 1,818 people). The ranking is chemoradiotherapy > temozolomide alone > radiotherapy alone > supportive care alone; chemoradiotherapy against short-course radiotherapy gives a hazard ratio of 0.67 on high-certainty evidence, while temozolomide alone against chemoradiotherapy gives 1.42 on low-certainty evidence[8]. The same paper gives numbers on the toxicity side: temozolomide alone against 60 Gy of radiotherapy carries a relative risk of grade 3 or worse thromboembolic events of 2.74 (1.26 to 5.94)[8] — relative risk divides the rate at which the event occurred in one group by the rate in the other, so 2.74 is nearly three times as often in the drug-only arm. So "older people can just take the drug and skip radiotherapy" is not a general rule. It is an option for people who are MGMT-methylated and not suited to chemoradiotherapy — and what that line decides and does not decide is in "That MGMT line: what it decides and what it does not".

What most people are advised today is the short course plus the drug

CCTG CE.6, in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2017, is the axis of this cell: 562 people aged 65 and over, median age 73, both arms given exactly the same radiotherapy (40 Gy in 15 fractions) and differing only in whether concurrent and adjuvant temozolomide was added. Median survival 9.3 against 7.6 months, hazard ratio 0.67, P<0.001; progression-free survival 5.3 against 3.9 months[9]. The abstract also states that quality of life was comparable between the arms[9].

I am not going to open up its MGMT stratification here: the benefit of adding the drug differs a great deal between the methylated and the unmethylated groups, and the P value in the unmethylated cell just failed to cross the line[9] — why that cell is the most dangerous passage in this whole topic is in "That MGMT line: what it decides and what it does not".

What decides is performance status, not the year on your birth certificate

"Over what age should the course be shortened" is a question with a fault in it. The 1993 analysis that graded 1,578 patients with malignant glioma does have 50 years as its first split point; but within the branch aged 50 and over, the most important variable was performance status, and after that only normal against abnormal mental status produced any meaningful further division[10]. In the simplified version rebuilt later on glioblastoma, age is one of four variables in the final model — how much that set of tools explains and how much it does not is in "That number, and how I am going to tell you about it". Age was never the single one.

EANO's wording is likewise a list, not a threshold: people with unfavourable prognostic factors (defined by age and/or performance status) may have hypofractionated radiotherapy, for instance 40 Gy in 15 fractions, whose activity is similar to 60 Gy in 30; and the general statement is that performance status, neurological function, age and an individual risk-benefit assessment all go into the decision[5]. There is evidence in the other direction too: the five-year analysis of the EORTC-NCIC trial states that the benefit of combined treatment was seen in all clinical prognostic subgroups, including patients aged 60 to 70[11]. So nobody switches automatically to a short course on turning 65.

One last thing that has to be said honestly: people who really are in poor condition, in bed most of the day, were excluded from almost every randomised trial. Cochrane's own words are that patients with this poor prognosis are usually excluded from randomised trials by their performance status, and that no randomised trial has ever compared comfort-oriented care against active radiotherapy or chemotherapy[2]. At that point the discussion is no longer about the length of a course; "Eighty years old: treat, or not?" and "The last few weeks: what actually happens" on this site cover it more fully than I can here. On the NHI side: I have read clause 9.25 word for word and there is no age condition anywhere in it[12] — what it contains is the disease name, the concurrent and adjuvant stretches, and prior approval every three months. The clause not saying something does not mean an application will be approved; whether it goes through is a separate matter, and one for your case manager.

So I would ask you to swap age for capability. There is really only one question, and it is short enough to get out in the room: given how much I can do for myself in a day right now, are you recommending three weeks or six, and is the reason my performance status or my age?

From the physician

Age is one of four prognostic variables; what really decides the length of the course is how much you can do for yourself in a day. So the question to ask is: given that, are you recommending three weeks or six, and is the reason performance status or age? If someone is already in bed most of the time, that is a different discussion — read "Eighty years old: treat, or not?" on this site before having it.
