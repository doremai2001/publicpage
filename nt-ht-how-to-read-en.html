HYPERTHERMIA: GETTING ORIENTED

Which ruler to read this set with

The evidence for hyperthermia is not absent. It is old, modest in size, and its endpoint is usually whether the tumour shrank — not how long anyone lived.

#Hyperthermia#Evidence#Decisions

Before anything else this article says where I stand, more plainly than the fixed disclosure does. Then it gives you five questions. Carry them into the cancer-site articles and you will find that some very attractive numbers are not answering what you are actually asking.

Warning 1 (what this displaces): Most of the treatments in this topic are paid out of pocket, and most sit outside standard treatment. Before you decide, you need to know that what they displace is usually not time but three things of real value: standard treatment you have not yet used up, money you will need for later lines, and your eligibility for clinical trials (many trials exclude people who have recently received other experimental treatments). And there is one more, harder to see — physical reserve. The strength burnt through in a course that does not work may no longer be there when a genuinely useful option appears. So the question is not whether this is worth trying — it is what you give up by doing it.

This article will not tell you to do it or not to do it. What it will do is stop a few attractive numbers from carrying you away in every article after it.

Warning 2 (finding the right doctor): Do not make this kind of decision alone, and do not make it on the word of the person offering the treatment. The doctor you are looking for is one willing to tell you "this is not right for you" — if they never once say who this is NOT for, that is not a consultation, it is a sale.

My position first: proton therapy and hyperthermia are both technologies within my own field, and the hospital I work at has the equipment for them. So I draw every line in this topic tighter, not looser.

The fixed disclosure at the top of this topic needs saying more plainly here: my hospital has hyperthermia equipment, I deliver hyperthermia myself, and I am running a retrospective study of it. I know what that means — the author of the set you are reading is the seller. So the way I handle it here is this: every number is written against its study design and its endpoint, every result favourable to me carries its limitation in the same breath, and the two sentences least favourable to me sit in the middle of this article.

The conclusion first: the problem here is not an absence of evidence

Hyperthermia is unlike the other four technologies in this topic. For protons, carbon ions, FLASH and boron neutron capture, the problem is that randomised comparisons are scarce or have not reported. Hyperthermia has randomised controlled trials, and they point the same way. The problem lies elsewhere: most of those trials were run thirty to forty years ago, none of them is large, the control arm was the radiotherapy of the year they recruited, and — this is the crucial one — a substantial proportion of sessions never actually reached the target.

Three trials, one direction

Breast: five randomised trials, pooled because each recruited too slowly. Among 306 randomised patients, the complete response rate was 41% with radiotherapy alone and 59% with hyperthermia added, an odds ratio after stratification of 2.3[1]. Pelvis: the Dutch randomised trial enrolled 358 patients (bladder, cervix, rectum), with complete response 39% against 55%[2]. Head and neck: a meta-analysis of six two-arm studies, 451 cases, complete response 39.6% against 62.5%, odds ratio 2.92[3]. And the two-arm part of a separate meta-analysis in locally recurrent breast cancer: 38.1% against 60.2%, odds ratio 2.64[4].

Four different cancer sites, different decades, different teams, and in each the complete response rate moves from around four in ten to around six in ten. That consistency is the most solid thing this field has, and I am not going to understate it.

But look carefully at what the endpoint is

Every one of those numbers is a complete response rate — the proportion in whom no tumour could be seen on imaging or by examination. It is not a survival rate. Tumour shrinkage and living longer are related, but they are not the same thing, and trials across many cancers have produced improved local response with no matching improvement in survival. The cervical cancer article, the thirteenth, is the clearest example of exactly that.

While we are here: an odds ratio is not a multiple of a probability. An odds ratio of 2.64 does not mean “more than twice as effective”; it is a statistical ratio. Where there are absolute numbers, look at the absolute numbers — “four in ten becomes six in ten” is the part worth remembering.

The two most honest sentences come from the trials that support hyperthermia

In the ESHO breast randomised trial, the authors wrote in their abstract that among patients who were heated, 84% reached the target temperature at least once, but that the variation in temperature was large[5]. The melanoma randomised trial is blunter still: because of equipment difficulties, only 14% of treatments reached the prescribed thermal dose[6].

Neither sentence comes from an opponent. They were recorded by the people who ran the trials. What they mean is that the positive results above were produced in conditions where most sessions did not reach dose. You can read that as good news (the mechanism is strong enough to work at a discount) or as bad news (we do not actually know how good it is when done properly, and we do not know whether the place you are going does it properly). I agree with both readings — but anyone who gives you only the first has left out the half that decides the outcome. It is also why the ninth article is entirely about thermometry.

The control arm was that year's radiotherapy, not today's standard

The control arm of the 1990 Dutch trial was the radiotherapy of 1990. Since then, image guidance, intensity modulation, brachytherapy, concurrent chemotherapy and systemic therapy have all changed. “It beat the radiotherapy of its day” is not “adding it today still wins by as much”. Each of the six cancer-site articles answers that question, because it is the step most easily skipped and the one that most changes the conclusion[7].

So why is it in this topic at all

This topic has an inclusion threshold: a treatment has to hold a formal status in at least one jurisdiction — a registered clinical trial, a drug or device authorisation, or a named-patient or compassionate route. Hyperthermia clears that line: it holds legitimate medical device status and has decades of clinical use behind it. But clearing the threshold only means it can be discussed; it does not mean it suits you. The sixth question in every article that follows is “when is this reasonable and when is it not”, and that is the section actually worth reading.

One reading I would rather you did not take

Seeing “four in ten becomes six in ten”, it is easy to conclude “then adding it can hardly hurt”. That inference fails in two places. First, those figures are randomised comparisons in particular cancers and particular situations; they do not transfer to any cancer you like — the sixteenth article lists several sites where it has been tried and the evidence will not carry a conclusion. Second, hyperthermia is not free: it costs time, money and physical reserve, it is uncomfortable while it happens, and there is a group of people for whom it is not suitable (metal implants, no normal response to pain, poor circulation in the region to be heated, inability to hold one position for an hour) — all in the tenth article. Treating it as “no loss in adding it” is counting the cost as zero.

Carry these five questions into every article that follows

  • Is this randomised, retrospective, or single-arm? A single-arm study cannot answer what adding it bought.

  • What is the endpoint? Tumour shrinkage, freedom from local recurrence, or living longer?

  • How many people, which year, which country? A hundred patients thirty years ago are not a hundred patients now.

  • Was the control arm of that era the treatment I would be offered today?

  • Which kind of machine did the trial use, was temperature measured, and how often was target reached? If it is not stated, it was not verified.

The last question matters particularly in Taiwan, because the class of machine available near you is not necessarily the class used in the trials above — I handle that separately in the eighth article. One sentence you can say out loud at your next consultation: “the kind you would be giving me — is it the kind those trials used?”

Facts checked: September 2026.

From the physician

The second of the five questions matters most: what is the endpoint? Tumour shrinkage and living longer are different things, and almost all the evidence in this set sits with the first. Next time you hear “it improves the effect by fifteen per cent”, ask what that fifteen per cent is fifteen per cent of.
