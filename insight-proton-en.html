Is proton therapy actually better?

Two phase 3 oropharyngeal trials in The Lancet reached different conclusions — clinician-scored toxicity fell, patient-reported outcomes did not move.

Insights · Radiation Therapy · 5 references

I get asked this almost every week in clinic. The gap on the dose map is real and obvious; the question is whether it becomes a difference you can feel. Two large trials answered that this year, they disagreed, and the reason why is worth knowing.

Radiotherapy has always carried a built-in cost: the beam has to travel through the body to reach the tumour, and everything along the way absorbs dose too. Protons behave differently. They release little energy on the way in, then dump most of it near the end of their range in a steep Bragg peak, after which the dose drops to nothing. There is no exit beam through the far side, which photons always deliver. A 2026 technical review in Frontiers in Oncology puts numbers on the gap: in paediatric craniospinal irradiation, mean oesophageal dose fell from 9.06 Gy with photons to 4.73 Gy with protons, and in left-sided breast cancer mean heart dose fell from 5.6 Gy to 2.6 Gy[1]. The difference on the dose map is not in doubt. Whether patients feel it is a separate question.

Two phase 3 randomised trials in oropharyngeal cancer appeared in The Lancet in 2026, and they did not land in the same place. The US multicentre trial randomised 440 patients to intensity-modulated proton therapy (IMPT) or photon IMRT: 3-year progression-free survival was 82.5% versus 83.0%, meeting non-inferiority, while grade 3 dysphagia occurred in 34% versus 49%, grade 3 xerostomia in 33% versus 45%, and feeding-tube dependence at 60 days in 26.8% versus 40.2%[2]. The UK TORPEdO trial made patient-reported outcomes its primary endpoint instead. Among 205 patients randomised 2:1, one-year quality-of-life scores were 78.3 versus 77.1 and swallowing scores 79.5 versus 79.7, with no difference between arms, and severe weight loss was actually more common with protons, 18.2% versus 5.7%[3]. Same cancer, same comparison: clinician-graded toxicity came down, the patients' own questionnaires did not move.

The two results do not contradict each other; they measure different things. Clinician-graded acute toxicity and a patient's own sense of function a year later were never the same endpoint, and modern IMRT has turned out better than it looked when these trials were designed. Five-year overall survival did favour protons in the US trial (90.9% versus 81.0%), but that was a secondary endpoint in an open-label trial and needs confirmation before anyone treats it as proof that protons extend life. In Taiwan the news this year is reimbursement: from 1 January 2026 the National Health Insurance covers proton therapy for childhood cancers in patients under 19 (central nervous system tumours, ocular tumours, osteosarcoma and others), with roughly 100 children expected to qualify each year[4]. Adults still pay out of pocket; the island's fourth proton centre opened at China Medical University Hospital in July 2024[5]. If you are weighing it up, bring your cancer type, stage and treatment field to your oncologist and ask which organ the spared dose actually comes off. And do not delay standard treatment while waiting for a proton slot.

From the physician

If you are weighing up proton therapy, bring your cancer type, stage and treatment volume to clinic and we will look at one specific thing together: which organ receives the dose you would be sparing, and how much that organ matters to your daily life. That is a calculation we can actually do. One thing to say up front — do not delay standard treatment in order to wait for protons.
