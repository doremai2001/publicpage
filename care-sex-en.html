WHAT NOBODY ASKS IN CLINIC

Sex during treatment: who raises it?

Whether you can, what residue really means for a partner, and why brachytherapy seeds do not end intimacy — with the evidence's gaps named.

Patient Education · What Nobody Asks In Clinic · 18 references
#Sex & Intimacy#Quality of Life#Daily Life

You will probably have to open this one yourself. Here is what the evidence can answer — when to pause, how common the changes are, what seeds and drug residue actually mean — and, just as important, where it answers nothing at all.

Almost nobody raises this in clinic, and for years I did not raise it enough myself. In the British CaSHOS cross-sectional survey of people treated for cancer within the last decade, 61% had never discussed sexual health with their cancer team at any point[1]. There is no citable Taiwanese figure. What there is, is a 2023 consensus paper by fifteen specialists in urology, radiation oncology and related fields, which opens by admitting that many countries have guidelines for sexual dysfunction after prostate cancer surgery and that "Taiwan has not yet developed such a clinical practice guideline"[2]. Nobody asked you. That is not because the question does not matter.

"Am I allowed to, during treatment?" — usually yes, and it turns on a few specific conditions

The first recommendation of the 2018 ASCO guideline on sexual problems in people with cancer (adapted from Cancer Care Ontario 2016) is that a member of the team should open the conversation about sexual health and dysfunction[3]. This was never meant to be yours to start. In practice it usually is.

Given normal blood counts, no bleeding, no active infection, and wounds and mucosa intact, treatment itself is not a contraindication to sex. The list to avoid is short: the days after chemotherapy when counts are lowest; a bleeding tendency, or pre-existing haemorrhoids or anal fissure; mucosa that is breaking down; a central venous catheter or an unhealed wound; and the period when the pelvis and perineum are being irradiated.

I could not find any society-level guideline — ASCO, ESMO, NCCN and ONS all searched — that gives a white cell or platelet number below which sex should be avoided. So this section gives principles, not a number. If you are in one of those situations, ask in clinic first.

One set of things cannot wait until the next day: fever or shaking chills after sex, severe perineal or anal pain, bleeding that will not stop, burning on urination with fever. On cytotoxic chemotherapy, any measured fever is an emergency — do not take an antipyretic and watch overnight. The full temperature threshold is in "The fever that cannot wait until morning".

And one thing has to be said plainly: "you can have sex now" and "you will not get pregnant now" are completely different statements. Which contraception to use, how long to continue it after treatment ends, and why men have to use it too, are all in "No period is not contraception".

Desire drops, the body does not respond — how common is that

Far commoner than most people assume. A meta-analysis of 36 studies and 9,749 breast cancer survivors (including Chinese-language databases) puts the pooled prevalence of female sexual dysfunction at 70% (95% CI 64–76%; a confidence interval is the range in which the pooled estimate plausibly sits), and sexual distress at 57% (29–86%)[4]. An interval running from 29 to 86% is absurdly wide — the studies disagree badly — so read it as an order of magnitude, not as your own odds. Gynaecological cancer survivors are higher still: of 200 sexually active gynaecological cancer survivors at one Indian hospital, 189 (94.5%) scored in the dysfunction range, and in multivariable analysis age was the only independent predictor[5].

For men it varies by modality. A single-centre retrospective series of 429 men under fifty with locally advanced rectal cancer: with concurrent chemoradiotherapy the five-year cumulative incidence of erectile dysfunction was 25% (20–30%), against 3.9% in those who had no radiotherapy[6]. A prospective multicentre study in metastatic renal cell carcinoma (357 men): 77.8% had erectile dysfunction of some degree and 62% had no sexual activity at all, with performance status, haemoglobin and symptom score as the independent predictors — not age[7]. Hormonal therapy is the clearest single route to loss of desire: one review reports that androgen deprivation therapy raises the risk of reduced desire five- to six-fold and of erectile dysfunction three-fold[8].

Body image belongs in the same account: a meta-analysis of 92 studies and 27,039 people found the impact of a stoma on body image to be moderate (effect size −0.467)[9]. These numbers all say one thing: changes in desire and in how the body feels are part of treatment, not a sign that what you feel for your partner has changed.

Can chemotherapy drugs reach a partner through sex

I have been asked this privately many times, and the evidence is thinner than most people assume. There are two sets of measurements. A Japanese home study followed 3 outpatients on chemotherapy and their family members: all 35 and 16 urine samples had drug detectable in them, and 8 of 12 wipe samples taken in the patients' homes had cyclophosphamide at 0.03 to 7.34 ng/cm²[10] — note that this is 3 people. The other is 38 germ cell tumour survivors after cisplatin, in whom platinum in semen was higher than in blood (p=0.03) and did not correlate significantly with time since the last dose; the authors label it a preliminary finding themselves[11].

The residue is real. But read the next sentence to the end: no study to date has measured how much a partner is exposed to through sex, and no study has taken a partner's health outcome as an endpoint. What we hold are concentrations. What we do not hold is any answer to whether that contact makes a partner ill.

So where does the widely repeated "use a condom for 48 hours after treatment" come from? A waste-handling document. Cancer Care Ontario's Safe handling of hazardous drugs classifies incontinence pads soiled by a patient who received a hazardous drug within the past 48 hours (or longer depending on the drug) as hazardous waste, and strongly recommends double gloves and a gown for staff handling patients' body fluids and contaminated linen[12]. That document is about waste classification for healthcare workers and home carers. It is not a trial of sex, and it writes "or longer depending on the drug" itself. The honest version: using a condom for some days after treatment is a reasonable precaution, the number of days depends on the drug, and you should ask your team — but 48 hours is not a safety threshold with a trial behind it.

People with radioactive seeds in them — can they be close to anyone

Yes, and this one has been quantified. ICRP Publication 98 records that more than 50,000 prostate cancer patients worldwide receive permanent iodine-125 or palladium-103 seed implants each year, that there has been no reported incident of harm to staff or family members, and that in the great majority of situations the dose to a companion or carer is far below the recommended limit of 1 mSv per year. The one uncommon situation calling for particular precautions is a partner who is pregnant at the time of implantation; expulsion of a seed through urine, semen or the gastrointestinal tract is rare, but patients should be given clear instructions on how to handle it[13]. "Once you have seeds in you there can be no sex life and no picking up children" is the opposite of what the report concludes.

Taiwan has its own predictive model for external dose rate around patients implanted with iodine-125, built from water phantom measurements plus Monte Carlo simulation, with measurement and simulation differing by less than 5%[14] — so we do have a firm local handle on how much the people around a patient receive. The hospital gives you a written sheet at discharge, including what to do if a seed comes out. Follow that sheet.

Vaginal dryness and stenosis are not a psychological problem, and they do not resolve if left alone

With external beam radiotherapy plus brachytherapy for pelvic cancers, the equivalent dose in the tumour region lands around 60 to 90 Gy; the surrounding vaginal tissue is irradiated, fibroses and contracts, and in severe cases can close completely. Standard management is a dilator used by the patient herself. A 2026 review is clear: dilators are "effective when used consistently", but patients are often unwilling to use them because of pain or even injury, and the field lacks standardised assessment criteria[15]. I am not going to write this up as a simple piece of homework. It hurts, you cannot get through it, nobody ever showed you how — all three need saying out loud, and all three can be adjusted.

ASCO recommends starting with a lubricant plus a vaginal moisturiser, and considering options such as low-dose vaginal oestrogen when that is not enough[3]. A consensus from a 16-member expert panel of the North American Menopause Society and the International Society for the Study of Women's Sexual Health notes that genitourinary syndrome of menopause is commoner in breast cancer survivors and is frequently underdiagnosed and undertreated[16]; a meta-analysis of 8 observational studies saw no association between vaginal oestrogen use in breast cancer survivors and an increase in recurrence risk[17]. That is all observational data, so the only statement I can make is that it is an option to weigh up together with gynaecology and oncology. Not "safe". Not "absolutely not". On the male side there are drugs that help, and that is a urology clinic; which items the National Health Insurance (健保) covers is for your case manager (個管師) or the hospital billing office to confirm, because I could not find a citable formal provision.

This can be taught, and Taiwan has done it

A gynaecological oncology ward at a medical centre in northern Taiwan ran a randomised study in which 63 cervical cancer patients were given a single session of sexual health education: sexual knowledge (β=3.794) and self-efficacy both improved significantly[18]. If one session produces a measurable difference, what most people are short of is information.

Three sentences to get out at your next visit. Given my treatment and my blood counts, is there a period I should avoid? For the drug I am on, how many days do you advise using a condom for residue in body fluids? If it hurts, or my partner has concerns, is there anyone in the hospital you can refer us to? None is easy to say. But if you do not ask, those ten minutes in clinic will run through in exactly the order they always do.

From the physician

Nothing here is a rule for your body. Blood counts, wound healing, the fields you were treated in and the drug you are on all change the answer, and no guideline sets a number. Take one of the three closing questions to your next appointment — and if fever or bleeding follows sex, treat it as an emergency, not as embarrassment.
