THE LOW-RISK BOX: HOW AGGRESSIVE TO BE

Watching instead of operating, on conditions

"Let's just watch it" and active surveillance are not the same thing: the second has entry and exit conditions written down, a fixed ultrasound interval, and requirements the team doing it has to meet.

Thyroid Cancer Guide · The low-risk box: how aggressive to be · 16 references
#Active Surveillance#Decisions#Evidence#Imaging

You take it to mean the doctor decided it isn't serious, so it can be left alone. It is a plan with a timetable — and the commonest reason people leave that plan is not the tumour changing, it is the person not being able to hold on. That reason is already written into the exit conditions.

The report says papillary carcinoma, and the size is a fraction of a centimetre. I say: this one does not have to be operated on yet, we can watch it. Most people stall. Watch it — meaning leave it alone?

No. Active surveillance has entry conditions that are written down, exit conditions that are written down, a set imaging interval, and requirements for the team running it. This article covers those four, plus one usually skipped: the people who leave surveillance mostly leave for reasons that have nothing to do with the tumour. How much gland comes out is in 〈Half the gland, or all of it〉; the blood numbers afterwards are in 〈Follow-up is an ultrasound and one number〉.

This is written down, not one surgeon's personal style

Recommendation 11A of the 2025 American Thyroid Association guideline puts active surveillance down as an option that may be offered, restricted to cT1aN0M0 papillary carcinoma — tumour one centimetre or under, no suspicious neck nodes clinically, no distant spread. The grade is a conditional recommendation on low certainty evidence[1]. In plain words: you may do this, but the research holding it up is weak.

More than one society says so. The Korean Thyroid Association published a whole guideline in 2025 on active surveillance alone[2]; the Korean Society of Thyroid Radiology issued a 2024 consensus governing how the ultrasound is done and the tumour measured[3]; the Japan Association of Endocrine Surgery has one from 2021, which states of itself that grading of evidence was not suitable here, so it carries no grades[4].

The other side has to be said too. In the systematic review the American society commissioned for this edition, seven studies compared active surveillance with immediate surgery and a further seven were uncontrolled treatment series; the quality rating was fair for one and poor for the rest (the source does not say whether that rating covers only the seven comparative studies or all fourteen), and the conclusion was that methodological limitations preclude strong conclusions[5]. Active surveillance has not been proven. It has been permitted. Those are two different things.

The ceiling for getting in is still one centimetre

The Korean Thyroid Association writes the entry definition most completely, and four conditions have to hold at once: the needle report is Bethesda category V or VI and the nodule is one centimetre or under; no clinical evidence of nodal or distant metastasis; no imaging sign of gross invasion into strap muscle, trachea or recurrent laryngeal nerve; and the subtype is not one of the aggressive ones — tall cell, columnar cell, hobnail, solid, diffuse sclerosing. The same document says that under the age of 19, surgery is recommended regardless[2]. The Japanese consensus adds seven situations that go straight to surgery, and two of them I look at especially hard: a tumour sitting against the trachea that may already be into it, and a tumour lying along the course of the recurrent laryngeal nerve[4].

That you must have cytology first is not a position every country holds. The 2021 Korean imaging consensus does not recommend biopsy for certain highly suspicious nodules under a centimetre, and the reasoning turns on where the nodule sits; that passage belongs to 〈A scan found a nodule. Now what?〉. "You may skip the needle" is not the same sentence as "you may decide this yourself". Everything below still has to hold.

What about over a centimetre? The data are thin. At one Japanese centre only 61 people with T1b disease chose surveillance[6]; one American academic centre widened the ceiling to two centimetres, with 112 people in the surveillance arm and mean follow-up 37.1 months[7]; the New York cohort took intrathyroidal tumours up to 1.5 centimetres, 291 people[8]. Few centres, few people, short follow-up. I will not tell you 1.5 or 2 centimetres can be watched. The ceiling that stands up is one centimetre.

What gets watched is the ultrasound, not your blood

All three documents give the same interval: neck ultrasound every 6 months for the first 1 to 2 years, then once a year if nothing has progressed[1][2][4].

Here is the part that runs against instinct. During surveillance, thyroglobulin — a protein only thyroid cells make, which can be measured in blood — is not checked, and neither are its antibodies. Recommendation 13 of the 2025 edition says exactly that, listed as a good practice statement, which the guideline does not grade[1]. The reason is plain: the whole gland is still there, so the number has no baseline. How it is read depends entirely on what operation you had and whether you had radioiodine — that belongs to 〈Follow-up is an ultrasound and one number〉.

What makes you stop and operate

Recommendation 14 of the 2025 edition lists the exit conditions in one string, again as a good practice statement with no evidence grade: growth of the primary tumour by 3 millimetres or more, new biopsy-proven nodal metastasis, distant metastasis, evidence of extrathyroidal extension, posterior growth — and then patient anxiety, inability to attend follow-up, and the patient saying they want surgery[1].

Look at that second half. It puts the state of the person and the state of the tumour inside one recommendation, not a footnote. The fourth Korean exit condition is "the patient elects to undergo surgery", at the highest grade; the same guideline requires growth to be met on two consecutive examinations at least 6 months apart before it counts, because ultrasound measurement carries its own error[2][3]. The Japanese size threshold is 13 millimetres[4]; the Korean guideline sets the same figure[2], so it is not a Japanese peculiarity.

Why does "posterior growth" get a line of its own? Because behind the thyroid sit the trachea and the recurrent laryngeal nerve. The Japanese consensus records this: among papillary microcarcinomas under 7 millimetres, not one showed invasion of trachea or nerve at operation[4]. Position sometimes matters more than size.

"It hardly ever grows" does not hold if you are young

Start with the largest series. Kuma Hospital in Japan, 30 years, 3,222 people with low-risk papillary microcarcinoma under surveillance: growth of 3 millimetres or more in 3.8%, 4.7% at 10 years and 6.6% at 20; new nodal metastasis in 0.8%, 1.0% at 10 years and 1.6% at 20; not one death from thyroid cancer[9]. But that is every age averaged together: a Japanese series of 1,235 people put the 10-year progression rate at 22.5% under 40, 4.9% at 40 to 59, and 2.5% at 60 and over[4][10]. The 291 in New York run the same way: 3.8% grew by 3 millimetres or more, 12.1% cumulative at 5 years, and no regional or distant metastasis appeared[8].

"Progression" in that set of numbers means the tumour reaching 12 millimetres or new nodal metastasis. It does not mean death. A Korean prospective cohort of 706 points the same way: composite progression 5.3% at 2 years and 14.2% at 5; for those diagnosed under 30 the odds ratio was 2.86 (95% confidence interval 1.10 to 7.45) — an odds ratio is the chance of something happening in one group divided by the chance in another, and this interval does not cross 1[11].

Kuma also used 1,211 patients aged 20 to 79 to estimate something else: watched all the way to 85, the lifetime probability of progression came out at 60.3% for people in their twenties, 37.1% in their thirties, 27.3% in their forties, 14.9% in their fifties, 9.9% in their sixties, 3.5% in their seventies[12]. The Japanese consensus, citing the same paper, lists a different and lower set[4]; the two do not reconcile, and what I have quoted is the original abstract. So active surveillance at 70 and active surveillance at 25 are decisions of completely different weight.

The place doing this has conditions on it too

This is the condition most often skipped, and it decides whether those good-looking numbers reproduce anywhere else. The 2016 framework later guidelines borrowed sorts the requirements into three columns: ideal is an experienced multidisciplinary team, high-quality neck ultrasonography, prospective data collection, and a tracking or reminder programme; inappropriate is reliable neck ultrasonography not being available, or little experience with thyroid cancer management. The patient side has an entry too: someone unlikely to keep to the follow-up plan is inappropriate[13].

The Japanese consensus uses a heavier word: a multidisciplinary team including ultrasound specialists highly experienced in imaging the neck is mandatory[4]. The Korean radiology society requires a high-quality neck ultrasound by an expert before surveillance begins, and the ideal / appropriate / inappropriate category written into the report[3]. The 2025 edition is the bluntest of the three — if the follow-up is not actually carried out, the safety claims made for this approach do not hold[1]. So "I have decided not to be operated on" does not work on paper: it takes your willingness and a team that can deliver.

Operating later has a price, and it lands in the temporary column

Watching for years and then operating — same as operating at the start? Not quite. The Korean Thyroid Association pooled six studies of its own: delayed surgery carried more temporary hypoparathyroidism, odds ratio 1.705 (95% confidence interval 1.188 to 2.448), and more temporary vocal cord palsy, 1.519 (1.038 to 2.222). For permanent hypoparathyroidism, permanent vocal cord palsy and recurrence, all three intervals crossed 1 and no difference showed[2].

One paper is more cautious still. A 2025 meta-analysis of 21 studies, 9,397 people: disease progression during surveillance 14.53% (95% confidence interval 9.59 to 21.43), and no thyroid cancer death in either arm; but it concluded that overall complication and recurrence rates were higher in the delayed surgery group, and that active surveillance should be undertaken with caution[14]. So: no visible rise in the permanent column, a possible rise in the temporary column, and one meta-analysis in which even recurrence came out higher.

What actually takes people out of surveillance is not the tumour

The 2025 edition puts it flatly: the proportion going on to surgery varies between centres, and is driven more by patient choice than by signs of progression[1]. In a Japanese series of 2,288 people, 162 (7.1%) converted to surgery more than 12 months in: disease progression 57, patient preference 43, physician preference 31, other thyroid or parathyroid disease 24, other 7[15]. In a Korean multicentre cohort of 370, 58 (15.7%) had delayed surgery, and the leading reason was anxiety, at 37.9%, with tumour growth 32.8% and nodal metastasis 8.6%[16]. This is not failure. Anxiety bad enough to affect your life is already written into the exit conditions. I do not treat holding out against surgery as the goal.

One more thing, said straight. All three guidelines say nobody knows how long surveillance should run, or at what age it stops. The Japanese document concludes that it is recommended for life; the American one says the length of necessary follow-up remains unknown[4][1]. What you accept is not only the absence of an operation. It is a timetable with no end written on it.

Five things to settle before you take this road

You can read these out. "Is mine cT1a? Is it against the trachea, or growing backwards?" "If I go into surveillance, how many months until the first ultrasound? Who performs it? Do you have a system that reminds people to come back?" "At how many millimetres will you tell me to operate?" "At my age, which band does the lifetime probability of progression fall in?" And the one that matters most: "If I start losing sleep over this, can I say at any point that I want it out?"

From the physician

You can read these out at your next appointment: "Is mine cT1a? Is it against the trachea, or growing backwards?" "If I go into surveillance, how many months until the first ultrasound, and who performs it?" "At how many millimetres will you tell me to operate?" Then add one more: "If I start losing sleep over this, can I say at any point that I want it out?"
