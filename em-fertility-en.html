Keeping the uterus to have a child: what it takes

The entry conditions have to hold in full, and the guideline admits that not one of its recommendations is Level I evidence; complete response about 79.7%, recurrence 35.3%, live birth 20.5%.

Endometrial Cancer Guide · Special situations · 18 references
#Fertility#Endocrine Therapy#Decisions#Evidence

Two kinds of reader will be uncomfortable here: the one hoping to hear "the drug is enough" will run into a recurrence rate of a third, and the one who has been told "just have it taken out" will find that the guideline does keep a road open. I have attached the grade of evidence to every entry condition, so you can take it to gynaecological oncology and go through them one by one.

"I haven't had children yet — does the uterus have to come out?" When that question gets asked there is usually a biopsy report on the desk, collected that morning. Early endometrial cancer does have another road, but it is not taking a drug instead of having an operation. Under very strict conditions it trades a higher recurrence risk for a window in which to have a child; and the guideline says that once childbearing is complete, removing the uterus is still recommended. Women who meet the conditions genuinely have a chance; women who force the road get delay instead.

Forty-eight recommendations, and not one of them rests on the highest level of evidence

The main guideline here is not the general European endometrial carcinoma guideline. The 2025 ESGO, ESTRO and ESP text hands this question over itself, stating that fertility-sparing treatment is covered by the guideline ESGO produced with the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology and the European Society for Gynaecological Endoscopy[1] — the 2023 document[2]. That document declares its own evidential weight: of its 48 recommendations not one is Level I evidence, only 16 reach Level II, and in its own words "66% of the recommendations are supported only by observational data, professional experience and consensus". It also states that to date there are no randomised controlled trials comparing the different types of medical treatment[2]. Every condition below stands on that foundation.

The entry conditions are a list that must all hold, not a few boxes ticked

The brackets are the grades the guideline gives itself[2].

  • Endometrioid carcinoma, "Grade 1, Stage IA without myometrial invasion and without risk factors" (V, A); for grade 2 the evidence is "limited" and it can only be discussed case by case (IV, C). That IA is the older FIGO version — see "The staging changed; your treatment does not change with it".

  • Grading must use the three-tier system, grades 1, 2 and 3; the binary system should not be used for these patients (III, A).

  • The pathology needs a second opinion from an experienced histopathologist (III, A); hysteroscopic-guided biopsy is preferred over blind biopsy (III, A).

  • Myometrial invasion is assessed with MRI, or with transvaginal ultrasound by a specialised radiologist or sonographer, using standardised high-quality protocols (III, A); CT should not be used for this (III, A).

  • MRI or CT to exclude pelvic and para-aortic lymph nodes and distant metastases (II, B); adnexal involvement ruled out by pelvic MRI or transvaginal ultrasound (II, B).

  • For copy number high (p53 abnormal) tumours the guideline says plainly that conservative therapy would be inappropriate (IV, D); mismatch repair immunohistochemistry is mandatory, to identify those at high risk of Lynch syndrome (III, A); the full ProMisE classifier "is encouraged, although available data do not allow clinical applicability" (IV, B). Where those names come from is the whole of "Two kinds of endometrial cancer, now four molecular groups".

  • Three Lynch sentences, all (II, A): establish any concurrent or metachronous cancer; tell the patient her risk of persistence and recurrence is higher than other patients'; decide case by case whether to go ahead. See "Lynch syndrome: what the test means for you and your family".

  • Joint care with at least gynaecological oncology, fertility medicine, pathology and radiology (V, A); reproductive potential assessed and fertility specialists consulted before treatment starts (V, B).

  • Weight control during treatment is "highly recommended", the reason given being that it increases the chance of response (II, A). See "Weight, diabetes and this cancer".

One further clause is the easiest to misread: if an early and focal myometrial invasion of 1 to 2 mm is suspected from the resection material, a fertility-sparing approach "may be discussed on a case-by-case basis" (IV, C). None of those three qualifiers can be dropped — it does not say that a uterus with invasion can be kept. Age is not on the list; the guideline only says that with increasing age the chance of a live birth with a woman's own gametes is lower (II, A)[2].

How the drug is given, and how often tissue has to be taken

The most effective combination in the guideline is hysteroscopic tumour resection followed by oral progestins and/or a levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system, with both complete response and live birth rates higher than other options (II, B). Doses, copied out: megestrol acetate 160 to 320 mg per day, or medroxyprogesterone acetate 400 to 600 mg per day (III, B); the intrauterine system is levonorgestrel 52 mg (III, B). Gonadotropin-releasing hormone analogues are not recommended as first-line treatment (II, B)[2]. That "and/or" is not settled, and two meta-analyses point opposite ways, so both go in. The one built around hysteroscopic resection pooled 21 studies (407 women with endometrial cancer, 444 with atypical hyperplasia); in its subgroup comparison, resection followed by oral progestins gave higher figures than resection followed by the intrauterine system — complete response 91.8% versus 82.5%, live birth 28.2% versus 16.3%[3]. The one built around the intrauterine system pooled 25 studies and 821 women and found no difference in complete response or recurrence between the intrauterine system plus a GnRH analogue, plus oral progestins, or after hysteroscopic resection[4]. Both pool retrospective studies and their populations differ; this question has no answer yet.

The density of follow-up is what this plan costs: complete response within 6 to 12 months of treatment (III, B), and no later than 15 months (IV, C); no response at all at 6 months, multidisciplinary counselling (IV, B); pelvic examination and ultrasound every 3 months (IV, B); endometrial histological assessment by hysteroscopy every 3 to 6 months (IV, B); and two consecutive biopsies at least 3 months apart both showing complete response before it counts (IV, C). After that, try to conceive as soon as possible (V, B); the guideline says assisted reproduction can be considered to shorten the wait, with no higher recurrence risk observed (III, B)[2].

Eight in ten respond, a third of them relapse, one in five take a baby home

The largest meta-analysis pooled 46 studies and 861 women with clinical stage IA, well-differentiated endometrioid disease: complete response 79.7%; among those who reached complete response, 35.3% relapsed during follow-up; pregnancy rate 26.7% (95% confidence interval 21.3 to 32.3; 826 women), live birth rate 20.5% (15.7 to 25.8; 650 women)[5]. A confidence interval is the range in which the estimate plausibly sits. The three numbers have to be read together; drop one and it becomes a different story. Their denominators differ and must not be multiplied through. The authors' own conclusion is that "only one-fifth of them are estimated to achieve live birth"; in studies restricted to women aged 35 or under the live birth rate was 30.7%[5].

Only two prospective datasets exist, and their numbers look worse. A Japanese 16-centre phase II study enrolled 28 women under 40 with presumed stage IA endometrial cancer and 17 with atypical hyperplasia, on medroxyprogesterone acetate 600 mg per day plus low-dose aspirin for 26 weeks: complete response 55% in endometrial cancer, 82% in atypical hyperplasia; recurrence 14/30 (47%), between 7 and 36 months[6]. The Australian randomised feMMe trial put all 165 participants on the intrauterine system; pathological complete response at 6 months was 82% in atypical hyperplasia, 43% in endometrial cancer[7] — but its registered eligibility reads "BMI over 30, wishing to retain fertility or at high risk of surgical complications because of co-morbidities or obesity", and the mean age was 53 with a mean BMI of 48[8], so this is not a pure fertility-sparing population. In its long-term follow-up (52 women, median further follow-up 18.9 months), 21 (40%) ended up having a hysterectomy, agreement between the last biopsy and the hysterectomy specimen was only 62%, and of the 19 who had not reached complete response, 10 (53%) had no further sampling[9]. That last one is what the real world looks like: follow-up breaks off.

The two things most easily missed: the ovaries, and that grade on the report

In a cohort of 102 women aged 24 to 45 who had a hysterectomy for endometrial cancer, 26 (25%) had a coexisting epithelial ovarian tumour; of those 26, 12 (46%) had been graded 1 on the pre-operative biopsy, 4 (15%) had normal-looking adnexa on pre-operative imaging, and 4 had ovaries that looked benign to the eye during surgery[10]. That is why the guideline requires adnexal involvement to be ruled out; whether the ovaries come out once childbearing is complete is left to case-by-case discussion (III, B)[2].

How reliable is that pre-operative grade 1? A surgical cohort of 623 compared pre-operative biopsy with the final specimen: overall agreement 68.7%, upgrading 24.9%, and among those called grade 1 before surgery only 63.5% agreed[11]. That cohort had a median age of 64 and is the whole surgical population, not a fertility-sparing one, so extrapolating needs a discount — but it is the reason behind the second-opinion recommendation. Two further datasets should be stated plainly. For conservative treatment in women who do have myometrial invasion, all that can be cited is 3 retrospective studies and 39 women, recurrence 37%, the authors writing that this "cannot be considered a standard of care to date"[12]. On whether molecular classification can select patients, a meta-analysis of 9 studies and 295 women gave complete response 95% for POLEmut (n=23), 82% for NSMP (n=212), 62% for MMRd (n=46) and 41% for p53abn (n=14, progression 38%) — but the authors' qualifier is "if confirmed in larger cohorts", so at this stage all that can be said is that it is being tested[13]. Another analysis of 6 studies and 337 women gave a pooled relative risk of recurrence or progression of 1.1 (0.99 to 1.23), but the authors state the studies carried a "high risk of bias" and the result was driven by one single study[14] — only enough to say the quality is not sufficient to conclude anything.

On "after you have had your children", the guideline leaves no room to blur

Plainly first, then the original wording. Removal of the uterus is recommended once childbearing is complete (II, A): "Completion surgery is recommended after completing childbearing", the reason given in the main text being "due to a high recurrence rate". If treatment does not respond, if conception does not happen, or if there is recurrence or progression, surgery is likewise recommended (II, A). Whether the ovaries come out at the same time is judged case by case (III, B); there is no general rule. For a woman who does not want surgery straight after delivery and is not planning a second pregnancy immediately, the guideline recommends maintenance with the intrauterine system (III, B)[2] — that is a postponement, not a cancellation.

In the Taiwanese clauses, I could not find this use

Three clauses, translated from the originals. The licensed indication for megestrol tablets is "palliative therapy for advanced (such as recurrent, metastatic or inoperable) endometrial carcinoma and breast carcinoma", and for the high-dose medroxyprogesterone formulations "adjuvant and palliative therapy for inoperable, recurrent or metastatic endometrial carcinoma, and breast carcinoma in postmenopausal women"[15]; the licensed indication for the levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system is "contraception, heavy menstrual bleeding, prevention of endometrial hyperplasia caused by oestrogen replacement therapy"[15], while clause 5.3.5 of the NHI drug reimbursement regulations reads "restricted to women with heavy menstrual bleeding for three consecutive months or more causing severe anaemia (haemoglobin ≤10 g/dL), and it may not be used again within five years of each use"[16]. Item 1 of clause 5.5.1, on GnRH analogues, reads "this class of drug is restricted, within the indications recorded on the product licence, to cases of prostate cancer, central precocious puberty, endometriosis, and breast cancer in premenopausal (or perimenopausal) women"[17] — endometrial cancer is not among those four indications.

All I can say is this: that is what the clauses say, and I could not find this use in them. That is not the same as "so you will have to pay for it yourself", and not the same as "it is not covered" — ask the medical affairs office or your case manager. There is one more direct friction. The guideline asks for hysteroscopic sampling every 3 to 6 months; the note to item 28022C, "hysteroscopy", in the NHI fee schedule reads: "1. Where the hysteroscopy is normal, once per year per institution. 2. Where the hysteroscopy is abnormal and medication or observation is adopted, follow-up is limited to one occasion, at an interval of at least three months. 3. Where hysteroscopic surgery has been performed, follow-up may be carried out within three months of surgery, and if normal, is limited to one occasion"[18]. The two sets of rules are not talking about the same thing. I am not going to reconcile them for you; this is one to settle in clinic.

To take this road, first find out which condition you are stuck on

What you can do today is get three things together. The pathology report: histological type, grade, whether myometrial invasion is mentioned, and whether a second pathologist has read it — if not, that is something you can ask for. The imaging report: whether myometrial invasion and the adnexa were judged on MRI or transvaginal ultrasound, and whether the person reading it does gynaecological imaging; if all you have is a CT, the guideline says outright it cannot be used. And a fertility clinic appointment — which belongs before treatment starts, not after a response.

Three questions to take into the room: which conditions do I meet, and which am I stuck on? If there is no response at 6 months, what is the next step? When do we talk about the surgery after childbearing? For options that close the moment treatment starts, see "Some options close when treatment starts"; cervical cancer runs on a different framework, see "Young patients: fertility and the ovaries".

From the physician

This road trades a higher recurrence risk for a window of time in which to have a child; it is not a drug taken instead of an operation. The entry conditions have to hold in full — one missing and it does not hold — and once childbearing is complete, what the guideline still says is to remove the uterus.
