WHO IS TREATED, AND GETTING READY

Bladder and bowel: copying the same state each day

Full or empty is decided by your treatment plan; holding more is not better. The volume you hold in is itself the least reproducible thing there is, and the requirement to empty the rectum is being loosened.

300 to 500 mL is the range pointed to by those two retrospective datasets in cervical cancer: below it, the bladder itself takes more dose; above it, the rectum does. The thing runs in both directions from beginning to end, which is why I am not going to tell you to hold as much as you can. I give this one sourced range and invent no other millilitres.

A patient apologised to me at the treatment room door: "I'm sorry, doctor, I couldn't hold it today." That apology made me want to explain the whole thing again from the beginning. Your plan was calculated on one particular state your body was in on the day of the planning scan, and whether your bladder is full or empty is decided by that plan. You have one job: copy that state, once a day. Some people's plans need it full, some need it empty, and both are right.

My position first: pelvic radiotherapy is what I do every day, so every good word about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it. Implants such as the rectal spacer are not something my department provides; I write about them as an option you can go and ask about, and for cost and for who performs them, please ask that department and the medical affairs office.

The subject is not holding on. It is copying the state your plan was built on

"A full bladder" has a very old rationale on the gynaecological and rectal side: a planning study of 29 patients with cervical or endometrial cancer had the same people scanned for two complete sets of plans, one "all empty" and one "all full", and the full bladder brought the integral dose to the bladder itself down (D50 falling from a median of 94% to 87%), with the paper's concluding sentence stating that a full bladder is the precondition for that fall in integral dose[1].

But when what is being treated is the whole bladder — bladder-preserving radiotherapy for muscle-invasive bladder cancer — many hospitals use an empty-bladder protocol. An audit of 40 patients treated to 55 Gy in 20 fractions compared the two protocols: in the empty-bladder group there was no significant difference in bladder volume between the planning CT and the cone-beam image on the day of treatment (p=0.11), whereas in the full-bladder group there was (p less than 0.05)[2]. The experts have not settled it among themselves either: in a consensus statement from two French learned societies, consensus was reached on 30 of 34 items, and one of the 4 that failed was exactly how to set target margins for empty-bladder and full-bladder protocols[3]. So "holding your urine is the standard preparation for pelvic radiotherapy" is wrong. Whatever your plan needs, that is what you copy.

How far a glass of water can push the uterus

What a full bladder does is push the small bowel up and out of the field: a planning study of rectal cancer IMRT in 11 healthy volunteers calculated that for every additional 100 mL of bladder volume, bowel V15 fell significantly by 16%[4]. The gynaecological study above measured that changes in bladder and rectal filling displaced the body of the uterus in the cranio-caudal direction by a median of 7 mm (95% confidence interval 3 to 15 mm — the range in which the true value plausibly sits)[1]; and a 13-patient cervical cancer study is blunter still: the mean change in the three-dimensional position of the uterine fundus was 26.1±10.8 mm[5]. These are geometric and dosimetric numbers. They are not rates of side effects.

The variable hardest to reproduce is precisely the volume you held in

The prospective MESORECT study put 48 patients with locally advanced rectal cancer through both a full-bladder and an empty-bladder protocol, with 7 cone-beam images each: under the full-bladder protocol, mean bladder volume was 243 cm³ with a standard deviation of 164; under the empty-bladder instruction, mean 73 cm³ with a standard deviation of 58[6]. The volume you work so hard to hold in is itself the least stable thing in the whole workflow.

Prostate SBRT adds another cut: 85 men treated with 40 Gy in 5 fractions, with a full bladder and an empty rectum required, had a mean bladder volume at the fifth fraction 19.0% lower than at simulation — and the bigger the volume someone held on the simulation day, the more it dropped over the course (correlation coefficient −0.69)[7]. External-beam radiotherapy for cervical cancer (43 patients) measured the bladder taking in a further 30 mL during a single treatment session[8]. This is why the images have to be matched every day; that half of it is in "Daily imaging: the underrated half".

Holding too much has its own price: the 300 to 500 mL cell

"The fuller the better" is wrong, and there are clinical-endpoint data saying so. A single-centre retrospective study of 142 women having postoperative radiotherapy for cervical cancer divided them into three groups by bladder volume on the simulation day, with a median follow-up of 48 months: the group at or below 300 mL had the highest bladder V30 and V40; the group above 500 mL had the highest doses to the bladder and to the rectum at 2 cm³; and the group between 300 and 500 mL had the significantly lowest incidence of radiation cystitis (p=0.0396)[9]. A second single-centre retrospective study, of 71 women having VMAT after surgery for cervical cancer with a median follow-up of 33 months, points the same way: the group below 300 mL had the most late radiation cystitis, and the group at or above 500 mL had the most late radiation proctitis[10].

The labels have to travel with the numbers: both are single-centre retrospective cohorts, the population is women having postoperative radiotherapy for cervical cancer, and the grouping is by bladder volume on the simulation day rather than by how much anyone drank, so it cannot be extrapolated to other cancers. The direction, though, is clear: too little and too much each carry a different price. So I am not going to give you an instruction of "the more the better", and outside that one range I am not going to invent millilitres or drinking times of my own — those are each hospital's clinical protocol, so follow the one your hospital gave you.

The rule about emptying out properly is being loosened

The rule has a definite source. A 2005 study of 127 men with prostate cancer (78 Gy of three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy, with no daily image guidance) found that men whose rectal cross-sectional area on the planning CT was greater than 11.2 cm² had significantly higher biochemical failure, with a multivariable hazard ratio of 3.89 (1.58 to 9.56), and the paper's concluding sentence said that the rectum should be empty at simulation[11].

The same question has since been asked twice again, this time under daily image guidance. A 40-patient study in 2023 could find no correlation between rectal volume and intrafraction motion, and its conclusion reads that the findings support relaxing strict rectal diameter tolerances and do not support a need for rectal preparation in contemporary image-guided prostate radiotherapy[12]. A larger study in 2026 (255 patients plus 40, with 96 using a micro-enema and 159 having no preparation) found that the enema only brought the proportion with a rectum of 90 cm³ or more down from 28% to 18%, that a large rectum had no effect on intrafraction motion, and concluded that rectal preparation is not universally required in contemporary image-guided radiotherapy[13]. The evidence for the emptying methods themselves is weak too: a systematic review of 18 papers across 5 categories of strategy concluded that there is no robust evidence on which to recommend any one of them[14]; and the only randomised trial, of an anti-flatulence agent in 30 patients, produced no significant reduction in either rectal or gas volume[15].

The population label on this section is prostate cancer plus daily three-dimensional image guidance, and it cannot be stretched into "no pelvic cancer needs any bowel preparation". Whatever your hospital asks you to do, do it. But if today is simply a day when you cannot empty properly, when you are bloated — that is not your failure. The dataset that ties rectal state to treatment outcome has as its premise 78 Gy of three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy, and no daily image matching; under today's practice of matching images every day, that link has been loosened. Writing only "you must empty out properly" leaves the people who cannot blaming themselves for the whole course.

If you cannot hold it, or cannot empty, saying so beats getting through it

If you get on the couch and feel you are about to leak, if you are holding on so hard you are shaking, if your lower abdomen hurts — put your hand up and say so; do not endure it until the treatment is over. During those minutes of enduring without moving, your body is in fact constantly making small adjustments, which is precisely what the workflow least wants. Not emptying properly, bloating, constipation or diarrhoea: say it before the next treatment, not on your next clinic day — you see the treatment team almost every day. That the later part of the course gets harder also has a source: the cervical cancer study observed the bladder becoming less tolerant[8]. I am not going to give you a number for how long you can hold it. That number does not exist.

Holding less later on is normal, and being adjusted is not you getting it wrong

That bladder volume shrinks systematically over a course is a measured fact: in the bladder cancer audit it happened under both protocols (p less than 0.05)[2], and in prostate SBRT the mean was 19.0% lower by the fifth fraction[7]. How the team catches it: bladder cancer workflows prepare small, medium and large plans in advance and pick one on the day after looking at the image[2]; online adaptive radiotherapy is more direct still — a study of 8 bladder cancer patients brought the planning target volume down from a mean of 416.5 cm³ to 296.8 cm³[16] — and these are geometric and dosimetric endpoints, not side-effect endpoints.

When the team looks at the images and goes back and changes your preparation, that is the data telling us to change it. It is not you having done something wrong.

One further dataset is more direct still. In the same prostate stereotactic body radiotherapy cohort (85 patients, 510 CT sets), the investigators compared changes in bladder volume, trigone displacement and rectal volume against the urinary and rectal toxicity that followed — and none of the three showed any significant correlation with the incidence of toxicity; the authors' conclusion reads that these results "cast doubt on the need for excessively strict bladder filling and rectal emptying protocols" in image-guided prostate stereotactic body radiotherapy[7]. The labels have to be carried in full: prostate cancer, stereotactic body radiotherapy, single-centre retrospective, and it cannot be extrapolated to a five- or six-week pelvic field. But the direction is unmistakable: preparation done imperfectly and whether you get side effects are not the same line.

From tomorrow morning, three things are enough to watch

One sentence on cost: the full table of the NHI fee schedule contains no separate code for image-guided radiotherapy, and no dedicated item for a pre-treatment bladder ultrasound or for patient education[17] — that is a statement of fact, and it cannot be run backwards into "so it is free" or into "so you have to pay for it"; if you are told about any charge, confirm it with the medical affairs office. Then the three things. One: put the preparation you have been given into your phone — full or empty, when to start — and follow it, neither adding to it nor discounting it. Two: keep one line a day — could you hold it, did you empty properly, were you bloated — and read it to the radiographer before the next treatment. Three: on any day you cannot do it, say so that day.

From the physician

If you cannot hold it, or cannot empty properly, say so that day rather than enduring until the treatment is over — during those minutes of holding still, the body is in fact moving the whole time. Later in the course the bladder holds less; that has been measured and it is normal. When the team changes your preparation on the strength of the images, that is the data telling us to change it, not you having done something wrong.
