HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

Which side effects go away, and which stay for life

Which cisplatin effects recover and which do not, why the targeted-therapy rash is no reason to stop, the reporting rules for immunotherapy, and the NHI one-or-the-other limit.

Head & Neck Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 15 references
#Chemotherapy#Targeted Therapy#Immunotherapy#Side Effects#NHI Coverage

Patients tend to line the three drug classes up as a ladder, newest at the top. The part of this page to read twice is not the comparison, it is the list of symptoms that have to be said out loud before the next dose, because that is the only point at which most of them are still simple to deal with.

Head and neck cancer uses three classes of drug: conventional chemotherapy (mostly cisplatin), targeted therapy (cetuximab), and immune checkpoint inhibitors. Patients tend to line them up as if later meant newer and newer meant better. It is a misunderstanding. The evidence behind each is different, the situations they apply to are different, and their side effects differ in kind — some go away, some do not. That last point matters more than which one sounds most advanced.

Cisplatin: which side effects go away, and which stay

Cisplatin's side effects come in two piles. The ones that recover: nausea and vomiting, falling white cells and platelets, rising kidney indices — most settle once the drug stops or with supportive care. The ones that largely do not: hearing loss and peripheral neuropathy. That asymmetry is the most important thing in this article.

The grading table we use in the notes (CTCAE) is specific about both. Hearing impairment grade 1 is "subjective change in hearing in the absence of documented hearing loss"; grade 2, hearing loss not yet needing a hearing aid; grade 3, hearing loss where a hearing aid or intervention is indicated and self-care is limited. Tinnitus is graded separately. Peripheral sensory neuropathy grade 2 is "moderate symptoms; limiting instrumental daily activities", grade 3 "severe symptoms; limiting self care"[1]. Note how grade 1 is written — it counts "something feels off to me" as real.

So commit this to memory: ringing in the ears, sounds becoming muffled, numbness in fingers or toes — say it before the next dose, not after. A dose can only be adjusted before it is given. The NRG Oncology consensus paper writes the timing of chemotherapy during radiation, hydration, and the monitoring and grading of ototoxicity into a defined process[2]. Audiometry is not an extra; it is part of that process.

Weekly or three-weekly? Two randomised trials gave different answers

I am asked this often, and the honest answer is that there is no settled conclusion.

Japan's JCOG1008 enrolled 261 postoperative high-risk patients, comparing weekly 40 mg/m² with three-weekly 100 mg/m². Weekly met non-inferiority for overall survival (HR 0.69). A hazard ratio is a ratio of the risk of death; 0.69 means this group's risk was about seven-tenths of the other group's — an average across a population, not a number worked out for you. Toxicity was lower too, though label the direction: grade 3 or higher neutropenia 49% three-weekly versus 35% weekly; infection 12% versus 7%, again higher three-weekly; kidney and hearing impairment also less frequent weekly[3]. But in the same report, treatment-related deaths were 0 in the three-weekly arm and 2 in the weekly arm[3]. So "weekly is safer" cannot be said flatly.

India's Noronha trial enrolled 300 patients at a weekly dose of 30 mg/m², and pointed the other way: 2-year locoregional control 58.5% weekly versus 73.1% three-weekly, an absolute difference of 14.6%, p=0.014. The authors concluded three-weekly should remain preferred in the adjuvant setting; acute grade 3 or higher toxicity was 71.6% weekly versus 84.6% three-weekly[4].

The difference most often raised between the trials is the weekly dose itself (40 versus 30 mg/m²). A pooled analysis of 59 prospective trials and 5,582 patients found similar survival for high- and low-dose regimens in the postoperative and conventionally fractionated definitive settings, with high-dose cisplatin significantly better only when combined with altered-fractionation radiotherapy[5]. Which schedule your doctor picks usually turns on kidney function, hearing, age and performance status, not on which paper is newer.

One more set of symptoms counts as an emergency during chemotherapy, and they do not wait for the next clinic appointment: fever above 38°C — particularly in the week or two after a dose, when the white cell count is at its lowest; shaking chills; a whole day unable to eat or drink with a clearly reduced urine output; vomiting so persistent you cannot keep tablets down; large areas of bruising or gum bleeding that will not stop. For any of these, contact your treatment team the same day or go straight to the emergency department, and make your first sentence to the emergency doctor that you are on chemotherapy and which day your last dose was.

The targeted therapy rash is not an allergy, and a worse rash goes with longer survival

Cetuximab's commonest side effect is an acneiform rash. A meta-analysis put the excess over comparators at roughly 74 percentage points for any-grade rash and about 12 points for severe (grade 3–4) rash[6]. In practice, roughly three in four people get a rash and about one in eight get a bad one.

This rash is not a drug allergy — an allergy is an infusion reaction, it happens while the drug is running, and the infusion unit handles it. More to the point: in the 5-year Bonner analysis, patients with grade 2 or higher rash survived longer (HR 0.49, p=0.002)[7]. So do not ask to stop the drug because your face looks bad. Moisturise, stay out of the sun, use antibiotic cream or oral medication where needed, and finish the course.

"Switch to targeted therapy, it's gentler" — two trials overturned that

This is the section I most want to get across. Cetuximab was substituted for cisplatin specifically because it was expected to be gentler, above all in HPV-positive oropharyngeal cancer, where the prognosis is better. Two independent randomised phase 3 trials tested that assumption and agreed.

RTOG 1016 enrolled 849 patients, and the cetuximab arm failed to meet non-inferiority: 5-year overall survival 77.9% versus 84.6%, progression-free survival HR 1.72, locoregional failure HR 2.05. On toxicity, acute moderate-to-severe adverse events were 77.4% versus 81.7%, no significant difference, and late toxicity showed no difference either[8]. De-ESCALaTE enrolled 334 patients with severe toxicity as its primary endpoint, and the arms came out identical (a mean of 4.8 versus 4.8 events per patient, p=0.98) — while 2-year survival was 97.5% versus 89.4% (HR 5.0) and 2-year recurrence 6.0% versus 16.1%[9].

Put plainly: it was not gentler, and it cost tumour control and survival. That conclusion applies to low-risk HPV-positive patients who are able to tolerate cisplatin. For someone who genuinely cannot have cisplatin because of kidney function, hearing or fitness, cetuximab remains a legitimate option. The two statements do not conflict.

Immunotherapy: it works remarkably well for some, and not at all for many

First, the boundary: the evidence for immunotherapy in head and neck cancer is in recurrent or metastatic disease, not curative-intent concurrent chemoradiation.

KEYNOTE-048 enrolled 882 patients using first-line pembrolizumab: in the PD-L1 CPS ≥20 group, median survival with pembrolizumab alone was 14.9 versus 10.7 months; pembrolizumab with chemotherapy in the total population was 13.0 versus 10.7 months[10]. CheckMate 141 enrolled 361 patients who had progressed after platinum; nivolumab gave median survival 7.5 versus 5.1 months and 1-year survival 36.0% versus 16.6%[11].

Here is what matters: neither trial improved progression-free survival[10][11]. Survival curves win, progression-free survival does not, and in plain language that means a minority respond very well and hold it a long time, pulling the average up, while many do not respond at all. The differences in median survival are 4.2 months and 2.4 months — not years. Going in with that expectation makes disappointment less likely.

There is a common misreading here too. In CheckMate 141, grade 3–4 treatment-related adverse events were 13.1% with nivolumab against 35.1% with standard therapy[11], and people conclude that immunotherapy is gentle. In KEYNOTE-048, adverse events led to death in 8% to 12% of patients across the arms[10]. Fewer events is not the same as milder consequences.

Three rules for immune side effects, and which symptoms mean picking up the phone

The ASCO guideline on immune-related adverse events sets management out by grade: grade 1 usually continues with close monitoring (with exceptions among neurological, haematological and cardiac toxicities); grade 2 means suspending the drug for most toxicities, resuming once symptoms fall to grade 1 or below, with corticosteroids possible; grade 3 means suspending and giving high-dose corticosteroids tapered over at least 4 to 6 weeks; grade 4 means permanent discontinuation, other than endocrine problems controllable with hormone replacement[12]. The guideline notes that because high-quality evidence is limited, most of its recommendations are expert consensus.

Whether that works depends on one thing: whether the doctor hears about your symptom early. Hence three rules: report it; do not stop the drug yourself; do not treat it yourself. Immune colitis is not gastroenteritis, and an anti-diarrhoeal off the shelf can mask the warning sign. Do not put steroid cream on a rash before it has been looked at either.

Based on the organ toxicities that guideline covers, contact the hospital if any of these appear:

  • Gut: new or worsening diarrhoea, blood in the stool.

  • Lungs: a new cough, breathlessness, chest tightness.

  • Liver: yellowing of the whites of the eyes or the skin, dark urine.

  • Endocrine (thyroid, pituitary or adrenal): unusual and persistent fatigue, dizziness, feeling cold.

  • Skin and mucosa: a new rash, blistering, ulcers in the mouth or eyes.

  • Heart: palpitations, chest pain, swelling in the legs.

  • Nerves: new weakness, numbness, double vision or difficulty swallowing.

On timing: in a large adjuvant safety analysis with prospectively defined observation windows, carried out in melanoma, 67.7% of patients had one of the selected immune-related adverse events, of which 48.0% occurred in the first three months, most resolving within six months[13]. That is not head and neck data, but it makes the point: most come in the first few months, and they can appear at any time, including after the drug is stopped.

What Taiwan's NHI covers, and the "one or the other" rule

Immunotherapy sits under §9.69 of the National Health Insurance (健保) drug reimbursement regulations. For head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, first-line cover currently exists only for pembrolizumab, and requires PD-L1 CPS ≥ 20; in the second line, pembrolizumab requires TPS ≥ 50% and nivolumab TC ≥ 10%. Two further limits are rarely explained: cover runs for 2 years from the date of the first prescription, and each application is capped at 12 weeks[14].

Cetuximab sits under §9.27, restricted to recurrent or metastatic head and neck squamous cell carcinoma not amenable to local therapy, in patients who have not claimed the drug before, with a total course of 18 weeks applied for every 9 weeks. The most consequential part is the clause added on 1 December 2023: cetuximab and immune checkpoint inhibitors may only be used one or the other, with no switching between them if treatment fails[15]. So the sequence has to be agreed with your doctor at the start. It is not "try this one, and switch if it fails".

Settle the order at the beginning

This whole article is about one thing: the three classes are not ranked from worse to better. There is only whether they suit this particular body of yours, and whether the reimbursement rules will let you double back. So the questions worth asking at your next visit: has my PD-L1 been tested, and what was the number? If I use one first, can I still use the other later? Can my kidney function and hearing take cisplatin?

The second one I especially want you to ask out loud. The order cannot be changed afterwards.

From the physician

Before each treatment, volunteer any tinnitus, muffled hearing, numbness in the hands or feet, diarrhoea, or new breathlessness. These can only be acted on before the next dose is given, and with immunotherapy in particular, the earlier a side effect is reported the simpler the management. Do not wait to see whether it settles by itself.
