AFTER THE DIAGNOSIS

Staged by examination and imaging, not surgery

The stage is set by pelvic examination plus imaging: what each tool answers, why clean imaging does not mean definitely clear, and why a bigger stage number does not have to mean a worse outlook.

Cervical Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 10 references
#Staging#Imaging#Getting Ready

In an American database of more than sixty thousand patients, five-year survival in IIIC1 is higher than in IIIA or IIIB — a bigger stage number does not always mean a worse prognosis. This article explains that counterintuitive result, and lays out what each staging tool can answer and what it misses.

My position first: definitive chemoradiation and brachytherapy are the treatments I deliver every day. So every sentence about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it — not more warmly.

"Why not open me up and take a proper look first?" Behind that question is a very reasonable instinct — seeing is believing. But cervical cancer is staged by pelvic examination plus imaging, not by surgery — and that is not a Taiwanese peculiarity, it is how this cancer's staging system is designed. This article explains why, what question each tool answers, and how to read that Roman numeral on the report — including the small letters that look like stray code, r and p, and why they are worth a second look.

Why this cancer is not staged by operating

The International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) staging of cervical cancer is built on one design principle: it has to work under any level of resources. Worldwide, most patients with this cancer are diagnosed at stages, and in settings, where surgery is not possible or not appropriate — so the staging system cannot depend on a surgical specimen[1]. Since the 2018 revision, imaging and pathological findings can formally enter the stage; examination under anaesthesia, cystoscopy and proctoscopy are no longer a route everyone must pass through, and are ordered according to clinical findings[1]. As for how Taiwanese hospitals handle examination under anaesthesia, I could not find a formal document I could cite — practice varies by hospital; go by what your own attending physician arranges.

FIGO 2018 changed three things, and that is why the report reads the way it does

First, stage IA is defined by depth alone: invasion visible only under the microscope and no deeper than 5 mm is IA — up to 3 mm is IA1, 3 to 5 mm is IA2[2]. Second, IB was split into three sub-stages: IB1 under 2 cm, IB2 from 2 to 4 cm, IB3 from 4 cm up[1]. Third, lymph-node involvement now goes straight to IIIC: pelvic nodes are IIIC1, para-aortic nodes IIIC2, with a required annotation of how the finding was made — r for imaging, p for pathological confirmation[1]. So "IIIC1r" on a report is not stray code; it means "pelvic nodes seen on imaging, not yet confirmed by pathology" — and the reason that small letter exists is precisely that the two methods are not equivalent.

MRI answers "how far has it grown locally"

The European gynaecological oncology societies' 2023 guideline (ESGO/ESTRO/ESP) lists pelvic MRI as a mandatory part of the initial work-up, to measure tumour size and to look for invasion of the tissue beside the cervix (the parametrium) — the only exception being very early disease with negative cone margins[3]. How accurate is it? A meta-analysis using surgical pathology as the gold standard (14 studies, 1,028 patients) measured sensitivity at 76% and specificity at 94% — sensitivity being the share of people who truly have invasion whom imaging catches, specificity the share of people who truly do not in whom imaging also says no[4]. It errs in both directions: among those it calls positive there remain a few false positives, and among those who truly have invasion, MRI misses roughly one in four — so a doctor will not treat "MRI saw nothing" as "definitely nothing", and adds the pelvic examination and the team's judgement on top. Likewise, "MRI says parametrial invasion" does not automatically become stage IIB — the final call is a synthesis of examination, imaging and team discussion[3].

PET has a blind spot that has been measured

For locally advanced disease, or when imaging shows suspicious nodes, the guideline recommends PET-CT before chemoradiation of curative intent, to assess nodes and distant sites[3]. But ACRIN6671/GOG0233, a prospective multicentre trial (153 advanced-stage patients, with surgically removed nodes as the pathological gold standard), measured its limit: PET-CT's sensitivity for para-aortic nodal metastasis was only 50%, and 83% for pelvic nodes[5]. A meta-analysis comparing PET-CT with MRI for node detection points the same way: both tools miss disease, with PET-CT slightly ahead[6]. So when your doctor says "nothing seen on imaging" rather than "definitely nothing there", that is not vagueness — it is honesty. Metastases below the detection limit do not show up.

The Taiwan side: the NHI's covered indications for PET explicitly include staging of cervical cancer and suspected recurrence, but with conditions — CT, MRI and other tests must have been unable to stage the disease or given insufficient information, the medical record must document the necessity, and it cannot be used for routine follow-up[7]. If you do not meet the conditions and are considering paying for it yourself, confirm with the medical affairs office.

A bigger stage number does not automatically mean a worse outlook

This is the section I most want you to take away. A validation study that restaged 62,212 patients in the American National Cancer Database by FIGO 2018 showed the three-way split of IB does separate prognosis: five-year survival for IB1, IB2 and IB3 was 91.6%, 83.3% and 76.1%[8]. But the same dataset holds a cell that stops people short: five-year survival in IIIC1 (positive pelvic nodes) was 60.8% — higher than IIIA's 40.7% and IIIB's 41.4%; IIIC2 was 37.5%[8]. The reason is not that nodal spread does not matter; it is that IIIC mixes very different people — someone with a small local tumour and one positive node sits in the same cell as someone with a large local tumour and several positive nodes[8]. So if your stage number is bigger than another patient's, do not convert it into a life expectancy — the stage is a map for treatment, not a sentence. And one label has to stay attached: these are American database figures and cannot be read directly as Taiwanese ones.

The SCC blood test cannot set a stage — and cannot rule out disease

Squamous cell carcinoma antigen (SCC-Ag) often gets drawn alongside the staging work-up. A meta-analysis of 61 studies showed people with an elevated pre-treatment level had about 2.4 times the recurrence risk and about 3.7 times the risk of death — meaning the group that starts high fares worse overall — so it does correlate with prognosis[9]. But for predicting nodal metastasis its sensitivity is 70% and specificity 63% — not accurate enough on either side[10]; and nowhere in the full text of the European guideline is any tumour marker listed as a required staging or follow-up item[3]. Taiwan's NHI does cover the test[7], and having it drawn does no harm; but "SCC normal" cannot be taken as "no disease", and "elevated" is not a stage.

Once the stage is set, the road forks

The broad direction in one sentence: for node-negative early disease, first choice is usually surgery by a gynaecologic oncologist; for larger tumours or later stages, the standard is chemoradiation with curative intent — how that fork is chosen, and why not operating is not giving up, is the whole of "Surgery or radiotherapy: not operating is not giving up", so I will not expand here[3]. If the team has already told you the route is chemoradiation, save "Getting through five or six weeks of pelvic radiotherapy" for what those weeks actually hold and which situations need reporting the same day — reading it once the stage is final is soon enough.

At the next visit, lay these four questions on top of the report

  • "What is my stage? Was it set by examination plus imaging, or is there pathological confirmation? If there is a IIIC, is the small letter after it r or p?"

  • "How large is the tumour on my MRI? Is there any concern about parametrial invasion?"

  • "Do I need a PET-CT? If the nodes look clean on imaging, how much weight would you put on that?"

  • "At this stage, is the road ahead surgery or chemoradiation? Which specialty leads?"

The answer to the fourth question decides which article you read next. Getting it asked clearly is worth more than memorising any survival number.

From the physician

The commonest misreading of a staging report is treating the Roman numeral as a ranking of life expectancy. At the next visit, get two questions answered: was my stage set by imaging or confirmed by pathology, and which specialty leads from here — the answers decide which article you read next. And do not transplant the American database survival figures onto yourself.
