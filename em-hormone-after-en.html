SPECIAL SITUATIONS

Hormones after treatment

There is one randomised trial, and it closed early with 41% adherence; local or systemic, early or later stage, uterus present or not — the three give different answers.

Endometrial Cancer Guide · Special situations · 16 references
#Menopause & Hormones#Endocrine Therapy#Sexual & Fertility#Bone Health

The most unexpected thing I found: the European endometrial cancer guideline has no recommendation at all on whether hormones can be used once treatment is over. So this article takes its anchors from the menopause and reproductive medicine documents instead, and puts the contraindication on the Taiwanese label in untouched.

Hot flushes, not sleeping, dryness bad enough that sitting is uncomfortable — these tend to get squeezed into the last five minutes of a clinic visit. Local (vaginal) oestrogen is not the same as systemic hormone therapy; early low-risk disease is not the same as later stage; and having the uterus is not the same as having had it removed. Mixed together they produce the two conclusions that are both wrong: "never again for the rest of your life", and "it's fine to top up".

The guideline does not contain this recommendation

Something you may not have expected, first: the European guideline does not have this recommendation in it at all. I went through the main text and the appendices of the 2025 ESGO, ESTRO and ESP version, searching hormone, oestrogen, estrogen and menopause one by one, and there is no recommendation on whether hormone therapy can be used after treatment for endometrial cancer. The only recommendation about hormone replacement anywhere in it sits in the context of risk-reducing surgery for Lynch syndrome: "Oestrogen replacement therapy should be suggested after bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy in premenopausal women (IV, B)" — that is a group having the uterus and ovaries removed to prevent a cancer they do not have, not survivors who have finished treatment. The two cannot be cited interchangeably. The follow-up section goes no further than saying that quality of life, sexual health, menopause management and side effects "should be repeatedly addressed (V, A)"[1]. So when somebody says the European guideline says you may, or says you may not, that sentence has no source. For Lynch, see "Lynch syndrome: what the test means for you and your family".

There is one randomised trial, and it did not finish

GOG-137: after surgery for stage I or II endometrial cancer, randomised double-blind assignment to oestrogen or placebo, 618 in each arm, median follow-up 35.7 months. Recurrence 2.3% (14 women) against 1.9% (12 women); new malignancy 1.3% against 1.6%. Three labels, none of which can be dropped: it closed early after the publication of the Women's Health Initiative, it had too few events, and only 41.1% of participants took the drug throughout the whole treatment period. The authors' conclusion, word for word, is that "this incomplete study cannot conclusively refute or support the safety of exogenous estrogen with regard to risk of endometrial recurrence"[2].

The 2018 Cochrane systematic review screened 2,190 records and included only this one study: relative risk of recurrence 1.17 (95% confidence interval 0.54 to 2.50), rated very low-certainty evidence; alive with no evidence of disease at 36 months, 94.3% against 95.6%. It records that the trial did not report symptom relief, overall survival or progression-free survival, and states outright that "There is no information available regarding use of HRT in higher-stage endometrial cancer (FIGO stage II and above)"[3]. Certainty of evidence is how much confidence there is in an estimate; very low means almost none.

The observational data points two ways

A meta-analysis of 1 randomised trial plus 7 observational studies (1,801 survivors using hormone therapy against 6,015 controls) gives a pooled odds ratio of 0.63 (0.48 to 0.83), which looks like "the ones who take it relapse less". This is textbook healthy-user bias — women who get prescribed it, and who can tolerate it, were healthier to begin with; the time-dependent analysis in the same paper, from 4 studies, gives a disease-free survival hazard ratio of 0.90 (0.28 to 2.87), not significant at all, and the authors' wording is "rather reassuring for the short-term administration"[4].

The same paper carries a signal in the other direction that has to be said out loud: in Black women on continuous oestrogen, the hazard ratio for recurrence was 7.58 (1.96 to 29.31)[4]. The original source is a subgroup analysis of GOG-137: 110 Black women against 1,049 white women, with 5 of 56 recurrences in the oestrogen arm among Black women and 8 of 521 among white women, giving an adjusted relative risk of 11.2 (2.86 to 43.59) after adjusting for age, BMI and grade[5]. The numerator is 5 events and the interval runs from 2.86 to 43.6 — the direction is worth noting, the precision is very poor, and there is no equivalent Taiwanese data for any such group.

Local or systemic, early or later stage, uterus present or not

Local and systemic are not the same thing. A US database study took women aged 18 to 51 who used vaginal oestrogen within a year of diagnosis and matched them 1:1 with non-users, 1,412 in each arm, mean duration of use 1.88 years: hazard ratio for recurrence 0.87 (0.60 to 1.27), no increase[6]. This is a retrospective matched study covering only women aged 51 and under and only short-term use; what it says is "no increase was seen within that range".

The second dividing line is stage and risk group. The 2020 joint position statement of the European Menopause and Andropause Society and the International Gynecologic Cancer Society uses two sentences pointing opposite ways: women with low-grade, early-stage disease "may consider" systemic or topical oestrogens, while in more advanced disease hormone therapy "may stimulate tumor growth" and non-hormonal approaches are recommended. In the original: "women with low-grade, early-stage endometrial cancer may consider systemic or topical estrogens. However, menopausal hormone therapy may stimulate tumor growth in patients with more advanced disease, and non-hormonal approaches are recommended"[7]. The 2024 ESHRE guideline on premature ovarian insufficiency says that for iatrogenic ovarian insufficiency caused by early-stage low-risk endometrial adenocarcinoma, healthcare professionals "could consider" hormone therapy, as there is no evidence that it increases the risk of recurrence — but it labels that one itself as a conditional recommendation on low-certainty evidence; the same guideline is a strong recommendation for squamous cell carcinoma of the cervix, and says hormone therapy may be associated with a slightly increased recurrence risk in cervical adenocarcinoma[8]. Which side your report falls on decides which of those two passages is addressed to you; how the boxes are drawn is in "After the operation, do you still need radiotherapy".

The third line is whether the uterus is still there. For a woman who still has hers and is taking hormone therapy, the ESHRE guideline says a progestogen must be combined with the oestrogen, precisely in order to prevent endometrial hyperplasia and endometrial cancer[8].

The label says one thing, the guideline says another

Contraindication 2 on the Taiwanese label for oral conjugated oestrogens reads, word for word: "known or suspected oestrogen-dependent malignant neoplasia (for example endometrial cancer, endometrial hyperplasia)". The warnings field also states that "in women with an intact uterus, taking oestrogen alone increases the risk of endometrial cancer"[9].

Put that next to the EMAS and IGCS sentence "may consider systemic or topical estrogens" and you have the real shape of this question: the label says one thing, the international guideline says another, and the wording simply differs. I am not going to smooth it over for either side, and I am not going to explain for you whether "known or suspected" covers someone who has completed treatment and has no evidence of disease — that is a judgement for your gynaecological oncologist and the doctor writing the prescription. One limitation: I could not obtain the full label text for vaginal oestrogen preparations, so the passage above applies to the oral formulation only. In the NHI drug reimbursement regulations, the clause-number field returned for estradiol products is "X" throughout, and searches for conjugated oestrogens and for tibolone return 0 hits[10] — that means no specific clause could be found, not that reimbursement does or does not exist.

The non-hormonal options have evidence, graded finely

First, the document most often passed around second-hand. The 2022 hormone therapy position statement of the North American Menopause Society says hormone therapy remains "the most effective treatment" for vasomotor symptoms and the genitourinary syndrome of menopause and prevents bone loss and fracture; that for women under 60 or within 10 years of menopause onset and with no contraindications, the benefit-risk ratio is favourable; and that for genitourinary syndrome of menopause not relieved by over-the-counter products in women without an indication for systemic hormone therapy, it recommends low-dose vaginal oestrogen, vaginal DHEA or oral ospemifene[11]. All three sentences hold — for the population it is talking about, which is women going through the menopause, not cancer survivors. Whether you have a contraindication, and whether you count as having an indication, is exactly what the sections above are taking apart; it does not transfer directly.

The same society's 2023 non-hormone statement grades every item (Level I is good and consistent scientific evidence, II limited or inconsistent, III consensus and expert opinion). Recommended: cognitive behavioural therapy, clinical hypnosis, SSRIs and SNRIs, gabapentin and fezolinetant are all Level I; oxybutynin is I to II; weight loss and stellate ganglion block are II to III. Not recommended for hot flushes: paced respiration (I), supplements and herbal products (I to II), and cooling techniques, avoiding triggers, exercise, yoga, mindfulness, relaxation training, soy and its metabolites, cannabinoids, acupuncture (II), clonidine, dietary modification and pregabalin. Its summary sentence puts women who are not good candidates for hormone therapy because of contraindications such as oestrogen-dependent cancers directly into the target group[12]. To be clear: "not recommended for treating hot flushes" is not "no good for anything else" — what exercise does for fatigue, fitness and mood is a separate line.

NK3 and NK1-3 receptor antagonists split into two layers. Two phase III trials of elinzanetant (about 400 participants each) reduced symptom frequency per 24 hours at week 12 by about 3.2 more than placebo[13]; a pooled phase III analysis of fezolinetant likewise showed a higher proportion achieving 50% or greater improvement than placebo[14]. But these trials enrolled women going through a normal menopause, and I could not find any randomised trial of an NK3 antagonist in endometrial cancer survivors — using them in this group is extrapolation. On top of that, a review has already assembled a signal that "emerging data from meta-analysis and pooled analysis consistently showed a significant increase in the risk of nonbenign neoplasms" with fezolinetant, and calls for long-term safety studies[15] — which matters particularly for cancer survivors. The approval and reimbursement status of these two drugs in Taiwan was not checked for this piece, so nothing is written here about whether Taiwan has them.

The other half of the bill: bone and heart

Menopause caused by removing the ovaries costs more than hot flushes — dryness, mood, cognition, bone and cardiovascular risk; the Manchester International Consensus lists the whole string, and I have copied it item by item into "Lynch syndrome: what the test means for you and your family". That list was written for the Lynch risk-reducing surgery population in the first place, and I did not find long-term fracture or cardiovascular cohorts specific to endometrial cancer, so carrying it across is extrapolation.

On bone density, the 2024 ESHRE guideline is the most specific: a dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry scan is recommended at diagnosis (strong recommendation, low-certainty evidence); where bone density is normal and hormone therapy is being taken regularly, there is little value in repeating it within five years; where there is already osteoporosis or low bone density, repeat every one to three years according to individual risk; a fall in bone density should prompt a review of the hormone therapy; and bisphosphonates should only be used on the advice of an osteoporosis specialist, with particular caution in women wanting to become pregnant[8].

The Taiwanese clause is a different set of rules. Item 33064B in the NHI fee schedule, "dual-energy X-ray bone densitometry", 600 points, lists among its indications the cancer groups "prostate cancer patients before and after androgen deprivation therapy…" and "breast cancer patients before and after treatment with aromatase inhibitors…"; the payment rules read "where a repeat bone density examination is required by the clinical condition, the interval shall be two years or more, and this examination is limited to three occasions" and "screening examinations are not covered by this insurance"[16]. "Surgical menopause from removal of the ovaries for a gynaecological cancer" is not something I could find in the clause; the international guideline's "once at diagnosis, then every one to three years" and the clause's "interval of two years or more, limited to three occasions" are two different sets of rules. Whether it can be claimed, ask your doctor and the medical affairs office.

Turn the symptoms into numbers, or the question won't move

Count the symptoms first: how many hot flushes a day, how many times they wake you, whether the dryness is affecting daily life or sex. Turning up empty-handed and saying "it's all right" usually earns "let's watch it for now". Four questions to ask outright: which box am I in for stage, histological type and risk group (that is what decides whether the EMAS and IGCS sentence applies to me)? If we start with something local and vaginal, what is your view? If I do not use hormones, which of the Level I options should I try first? And has my bone density ever been measured, and when should it be? Early menopause and hormone therapy in cervical cancer run on a different framework — see "Early menopause: hormone therapy is not forbidden".

From the physician

Count the symptoms before you go in: how many hot flushes a day, how many times they wake you, whether the dryness is affecting daily life or sex. The label says one thing and the international guideline says another, and that is a question for your gynaecological oncologist and the doctor writing the prescription; I am not smoothing it over for either side. And ask one more: has my bone density ever been measured?
