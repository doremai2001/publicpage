HYPERTHERMIA: THE EVIDENCE, SITE BY SITE

Three pelvic sites, three different grades of evidence

The colostomy-free survival gap in that anal cancer cohort is large, but it was not randomised. Prostate does not yet have a randomised trial at all.

Next-Generation Therapy Guide · Hyperthermia: the evidence, site by site · 6 references
#Hyperthermia#Evidence

The colostomy-free survival gap in the anal cancer data is the most affecting number in this whole set. It is also the number that most needs you to look once at the study design and once at the toxicity column before you look at it.

Warning 1 (what this displaces): Most of the treatments in this topic are paid out of pocket, and most sit outside standard treatment. Before you decide, you need to know that what they displace is usually not time but three things of real value: standard treatment you have not yet used up, money you will need for later lines, and your eligibility for clinical trials (many trials exclude people who have recently received other experimental treatments). And there is one more, harder to see — physical reserve. The strength burnt through in a course that does not work may no longer be there when a genuinely useful option appears. So the question is not whether this is worth trying — it is what you give up by doing it.

Deep pelvic heating needs an intraluminal probe, needs you to lie still for an hour, and runs over a long course. The cost of all those sessions, and the travelling, lands on top of a course of treatment that was already long.

Warning 2 (finding the right doctor): Do not make this kind of decision alone, and do not make it on the word of the person offering the treatment. The doctor you are looking for is one willing to tell you "this is not right for you" — if they never once say who this is NOT for, that is not a consultation, it is a sale.

My position first: proton therapy and hyperthermia are both technologies within my own field, and the hospital I work at has the equipment for them. So I draw every line in this topic tighter, not looser.

These three sites share the same deep pelvic heating technology, but the grade of the evidence differs enormously between them. I will take them in order, and put the study design in front of every one.

Anal cancer: the best-looking numbers here, and not from a randomised trial

A consecutive cohort from one German centre, 2000 to 2015: 112 patients with stage I to IV anal cancer received concurrent chemoradiotherapy based on 5-FU and mitomycin, of whom 50 also had deep regional hyperthermia; if the response was insufficient four to six weeks after chemoradiotherapy, a brachytherapy boost was added. Median follow-up 41 months, and at five years — overall survival 95.8% against 74.5% (p = 0.045), disease-free survival 89.1% against 70.4% (p = 0.027), local recurrence-free survival 97.7% against 78.7% (p = 0.006), and colostomy-free survival 87.7% against 69.0% (p = 0.016); the adjusted hazard ratio for death was 0.25[1].

But the same data recorded two things that were worse in the heated group: grade 3–4 haematological toxicity of 66% against 43% (p = 0.032), and late telangiectasia of 38.0% against 16.1% (p = 0.009)[1]. This is one of the few datasets in the whole series that clearly records hyperthermia adding toxicity. When you weigh up the survival figures above, these two numbers have to be on the same table — because in a course built on 5-FU plus mitomycin, haematological toxicity means treatment delays, infection risk and transfusion.

And one more thing worth noticing: the authors of this study write in their own conclusion that the possible benefit of hyperthermia has to be confirmed in a prospective randomised trial, and that they have already started one[1]. When the people presenting these good-looking numbers say that themselves, I have no reason to put it more confidently than they do.

Colostomy-free survival is one of the endpoints closest to the life of an anal cancer patient, and that gap is hard to ignore. But look carefully at the design: this is a consecutive cohort, not a randomised allocation. Who was heated and who was not depended on the clinical judgement and the practicalities of the day, and those judgements may themselves be related to prognosis — the person who can manage an extra trip each week, who can lie still for an hour, who is in better shape, lives longer anyway. This is the fundamental problem with every non-randomised comparison, and statistical adjustment only deals with part of it.

Rectal cancer: prospective, but single-arm

A German prospective phase II trial: from 2012 to 2017 it enrolled 78 patients with locally advanced rectal cancer for preoperative chemoradiotherapy plus deep regional heating twice a week, with pathological complete response as the primary endpoint. Median follow-up 54 months. Radiotherapy was completed by 99%, two cycles of chemotherapy by 91%, and 77% received eight or more heating sessions. The pathological complete response rate was 14%, with another half reaching Dworak grade 3 or 4 regression; at three years, overall survival was 94%, disease-free survival 81%, local control 96% and distant control 87%. And the patients with a higher cumulative temperature had more marked tumour regression[2].

That last sentence is one of the key points of this article: a dose-effect relationship again. But the study has no control arm at all, so the 14% pathological complete response rate cannot tell you how much more you got by adding the heat — the pathological complete response rate of preoperative chemoradiotherapy on its own falls in a similar range.

Prostate: retrospective data and feasibility, no randomised comparison yet

A Japanese retrospective analysis: 121 patients with high-risk localised prostate cancer, all of them given intensity-modulated radiotherapy of 76 Gy in 38 fractions plus neoadjuvant hormone therapy, of whom 70 also had hyperthermia, a median of five sessions; measured with an intrarectal probe, the median thermal dose was 7.5 minutes; median follow-up 64 months. Adding hyperthermia predicted better clinical relapse-free survival, and a higher thermal dose (above roughly 7 minutes) predicted better biochemical disease-free survival; there was no significant difference between the groups in acute or late toxicity of grade 2 or above[3]. For the specific percentages in this dataset, all I could obtain while checking was the abstract; the figures in the full text need checking against the full paper, so I am not writing them here.

The German HTProstate phase II trial deals with a different situation: biochemical recurrence after radical prostatectomy, salvage radiotherapy of 70 Gy in 35 fractions plus seven to ten deep heating sessions. The design is prospective, multicentre and non-randomised, and the primary endpoint is toxicity and feasibility. The planned interim analysis, after the first 50 patients had completed treatment and three months of follow-up: acute grade 2 genitourinary toxicity 10%, gastrointestinal 4%, nothing of grade 3 or above; hyperthermia-related grade 2 and grade 3 symptoms 4% and 2%; and seven or more heating sessions were achievable[4]. Note what the primary endpoint of that trial is: safety and feasibility, not efficacy.

There is a systematic review covering the prostate hyperthermia literature as a whole[5]. But the bottom line is clear: at this cancer site there is, so far, no randomised comparison that has read out.

One thing all three share: whether the thermal dose was recorded

The rectal trial found that patients with a higher cumulative temperature had more marked tumour regression[2], and the prostate retrospective analysis found that patients with a higher thermal dose had better biochemical control[3]. This is the dose-effect relationship the sixth article sets out, appearing once more in the pelvis.

What that means for you in practice: deep heating in the pelvis is a great deal harder than heating something superficial, and centres differ widely in whether they can reach a meaningful thermal dose. So at these three sites, the question “roughly what range do your thermal dose records fall in” matters more than it does in breast or head and neck[6].

Standard treatment at all three sites has been replaced in the last decade

All three have changed. The chemoradiotherapy technique for anal cancer has moved from two-dimensional planning to intensity modulation, and toxicity has fallen; rectal cancer has gained total neoadjuvant therapy and organ preservation, the watch-and-wait strategy; the radiotherapy dose, the fractionation and the systemic therapy for prostate cancer are all different. The most recent recruitment in any of the studies above closed around 2017.

The anal cancer figure cannot be lent to the other two sites

Reasonable: in anal or rectal cancer being treated with standard concurrent chemoradiotherapy, where the team has deep heating and thermometry, discussing it as an add-on option that has cohort and single-arm data but no randomised evidence. For prostate cancer I would keep the discussion inside the context of a clinical trial.

Unreasonable: taking that colostomy-free survival gap from anal cancer and using it to support the other two sites. It is the same machine; it is not the same evidence.

One sentence you can take to your next appointment: “The study behind this recommendation — was it randomised?” For all three sites in this article the answer is no. Knowing that does not mean you cannot have it, but it does mean that when you decide, the number in your head should be discounted.

Facts checked: September 2026.

From the physician

What these three sites have in common is that none of the hyperthermia data is randomised. When you see a large gap between two numbers, ask first: were these two groups of people alike to begin with? With non-randomised studies the problem is always there.
