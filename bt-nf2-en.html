WHAT COMES AFTER

More than one, NF2, and what is inherited

The term NF2 has been retired, two or more meningiomas is a major diagnostic criterion, more than one meningioma does not mean NF2, and how genetic counselling actually starts.

Benign Brain Tumour Guide · What comes after · 15 references
#Genetics & Family#Hearing#Screening#Decisions

The word "multiple" does not say on its own why. It may be one gene, it may be several other genes, it may be a course of radiation given decades ago — telling them apart runs in a particular order, and the first step is not a blood test.

If your report says two or more meningiomas, or a vestibular schwannoma (also called acoustic neuroma) in both ears, this article is for you. One gene can explain both of those apparently unrelated things at once — several meningiomas, and a tumour in each ear. Which is why this question cannot be split in half between two different clinics.

Not a rename — the word itself was retired

A 2022 international consensus reorganised the names and the diagnostic criteria for this group of diseases. What the original says is not "renamed" but this: in the revised nomenclature, the term "neurofibromatosis 2" has been retired, to improve diagnostic specificity. The new name is NF2-related schwannomatosis, abbreviated NF2-SWN; the reason given is to stress how much these conditions overlap in their presentation, and to reduce misdiagnosis against neurofibromatosis type 1. The new criteria also write mosaicism directly into the text[1].

If you go looking things up holding an old report that says "NF2", you will find two sets of vocabulary. They refer to the same thing, but the content of the new criteria is not identical to the old.

What the criteria say: two or more meningiomas is a major criterion

The criteria are reprinted word for word below; meeting any one of them is diagnostic[2].

  • Bilateral vestibular schwannomas.

  • The same pathogenic NF2 variant found in at least two anatomically distinct NF2-related tumours (schwannoma, meningioma or ependymoma). Note: if the variant allele fraction in unaffected tissue such as blood is clearly below 50%, the diagnosis is mosaic.

  • Two major criteria, or one major criterion plus two minor ones. The major criteria are a unilateral vestibular schwannoma, a first-degree relative other than a sibling with NF2-SWN, two or more meningiomas (a single one counts only as a minor criterion), and a pathogenic NF2 variant found in unaffected tissue. The minor criteria are ependymoma, meningioma and schwannoma, together with juvenile cataract, retinal hamartoma and epiretinal membrane under the age of 40.

Two things are easy to get wrong. First, bilateral acoustic neuromas carry no upper age limit: a cut-off at 70 was proposed in 2019, and the consensus decided against adding it, the stated reason being "to avoid clinicians not investigating older patients". Second, where there is a unilateral acoustic neuroma plus two or more schwannomas elsewhere, LZTR1 variants should be excluded before NF2-SWN is confirmed[2].

One gene, two kinds of tumour

NF2-SWN is an autosomal dominant tumour predisposition syndrome, and carriers are prone to schwannomas, meningiomas and ependymomas[2]. The reason this is not a coincidence sits at the other end: NF2 is also the commonest driver mutation in sporadic meningioma — the reason Alliance A071401 enrolled only meningiomas carrying a somatic NF2 mutation is given word for word as "the predominance of NF2 mutations in meningioma"[3]. It is the principal tumour suppressor gene shared by these two tumours in the first place; someone growing both is looking at two exits off the same broken road.

There is a further layer that joins up with "Can radiotherapy grow a second tumour": in radiation-induced meningiomas, NF2 point mutations are less frequent than in the sporadic kind, while gene fusion events are more frequent, presumed to be the result of misrepair after radiation-induced double-strand breaks[4]. The same gene, broken by two completely different mechanisms.

More than one meningioma does not mean NF2

Two meningiomas on the report — does that settle it as NF2? This is the misreading this article most needs to block. The differential list in the same review is thorough: multiple intracranial meningiomas can also occur in SMARCB1-related schwannomatosis on chromosome 22; where there are no schwannomas, there are also BAP1 tumour predisposition syndrome, SUFU-related familial meningioma predisposition, and the familial clear-cell meningioma caused by SMARCE1. The last sentence is the one most often skipped: the history has to establish past radiation exposure, because meningioma is a common secondary tumour after remote radiation exposure in the first place[2] — and among radiation-induced meningiomas, 17.3% are multiple[5]. The international consensus puts it this way: compared with the sporadic kind, NF2-SWN patients present younger and are more likely to grow several meningiomas, so people with this presentation should be screened for germline NF2 and SMARCE1 variants[4]. Whether it is done and how it is done is the business of a genetic counselling clinic, not something to book a test for yourself.

In NF2-SWN most meningiomas are under surveillance, not under the knife

A meta-analysis of 15 studies, 937 patients and 3,637 meningiomas gives one directly quotable sentence: up to eight in ten people with NF2-SWN develop meningioma over a lifetime. Across the 2,082 tumours under surveillance the weighted mean growth rate was 0.508 cm³ per year; the proportion of patients who grew a new meningioma during surveillance was 24.6%, but with a very wide 95% confidence interval (2.73 to 58.7) and only three studies contributing data — the range in the bracket is where that estimate plausibly sits. Only 203 tumours were surgically removed (pooled recurrence risk 12.5%) and 665 had stereotactic radiosurgery (progression risk 6.29%)[6]. Watch the denominators: those two added together come to far fewer than the number under surveillance — "up to eight in ten develop one" is not "eight in ten need treating".

The international consensus adds another set of figures: about 10% of these meningiomas grow quickly (one study defined quickly as more than 2 cm³ per year), while the rest do not grow or grow very slowly; 20% of patients develop new meningiomas. The same passage also writes the unwelcome half: NF2-SWN patients with meningioma have a significantly higher risk of death than those without, while care by a multidisciplinary team at a high-volume centre has been shown to improve outcomes[4]. The guideline also says the imaging interval needs adjusting: growth rates of the acoustic neuromas in this group vary more, so more frequent imaging than in the sporadic kind may be needed; and in people with bilateral tumours, the growth rate on one side may accelerate after the tumour on the other side is removed[7]. The general rules of follow-up are in "How follow-up is scheduled, and when it stops"; this section is its exception.

What "a hearing response of 36 to 41%" actually means

Does "a hearing response of 36%" mean that 36 people in a hundred get their hearing back? No. The primary endpoint in all three prospective trials is a hearing response defined by the word recognition score — an improvement in the proportion of a standard word list understood, sustained for a period of time, not a subjective sense that things sound better[8].

In 14 NF2 patients with progressive hearing loss in the target ear who took the drug for 46 weeks, confirmed hearing response (improvement sustained more than three months) was 5/14, 36% (95% confidence interval 13 to 65%), and nobody's hearing got worse[8]. That study later carried a correction which I have not obtained, so I went to independent sources for the two figures below. The 22-patient high-dose trial gave 9/22, 41%, at six months, and the authors conclude that the high dose does not appear more effective than the standard dose[9]. A systematic review of 9 studies and 176 people is in the same range: hearing improved in 36%, stable in 46%, worse in 18%; tumours partially shrank in 40%, stable in 50%, progressed in 10%; serious adverse reactions 13%, and the authors point out that some patients' tumours regrew after the drug was stopped[10]. A 20-patient maintenance trial shows the time dimension: freedom from hearing deterioration in the target ear was 95% at 48 weeks, 89% at 72 weeks and down to 70% at 98 weeks[11].

This road is open only for NF2-related acoustic neuromas. The 2020 European vestibular schwannoma guideline puts it very briefly: apart from bevacizumab used in NF2, drug treatment has no role[12]. People with the sporadic kind should not apply these numbers to themselves; what the NHI clause says is in "If it comes back, what can be done".

Mosaicism, genetic counselling, and the list I could not find

Mosaic means the variant is present in only some of the cells, not in every one. It is commoner than most people think: among 1,055 de novo cases with no family history in the previous generation, mosaicism was confirmed or presumed in 232 (22.0%); and because the variant detection rate in the non-mosaic form is itself only 36.7%, the authors calculate that the overall mosaic proportion may be as high as 59.7%. The proportion also varies enormously with age at onset: 21.7% in those presenting with bilateral acoustic neuromas before the age of 20, and 80.7% in those aged 60 and over. The authors' concluding sentence is that the risk to offspring is very small, and broadly correlates with the variant allele fraction detected in blood[13]. I am not writing a percentage for a relative's risk — that has to be calculated by a genetic counsellor from your own test results.

Two things to hold in mind together: someone with the mosaic form can still be severely affected and needs full clinical monitoring and intervention; but the chance of passing it to the next generation really is lower than with a germline variant. Testing is not one blood sample and done either: blood has to be tested, and ideally two or more related tumours from different regions of the nervous system as well, before mosaic and germline can be told apart[2].

What is settled on the Taiwan side is this: the NHI clauses do contain the role of a "genetic counselling centre" — the regulation for 25021B, chromosome analysis (special), says word for word that it is restricted to application by genetic counselling centres accredited by the Ministry of Health and Welfare, and that use as general screening falls outside the scope of reimbursement[14]. What I could not find, I will say plainly: there is no test item in the whole schedule named after NF2 or neurofibromatosis[14]. As for whether NF2-SWN is on Taiwan's announced rare disease list, Article 3 of the Rare Disease Control and Orphan Drug Act says the central competent authority designates and announces it on a prevalence basis — I can find the statutory source, I could not obtain the list; ask a genetic counselling centre[15].

Before your next appointment, set the family history out as a table: who has had a brain or spinal tumour, hearing problems, a cataract in childhood — several of the criteria depend on it. The question to ask in the room is "does this presentation need referring for genetic counselling", rather than going off to have a test yourself; and if you already have the diagnosis, add one more: "should my imaging follow-up be closer than for the sporadic kind" — the guideline says in as many words that it may need to be.

From the physician

Do not go and book a genetic test yourself. With several meningiomas, or a tumour in each ear, it is for the doctor to judge whether you should be referred for genetic counselling; a relative's risk figure has to be worked out by a counsellor from your own test results. What comes first is that family history table, and it is quicker than any blood test.
