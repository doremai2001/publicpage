THE DECISIONS THAT ARE YOURS

A trial is not treatment designed for you

What randomisation, a placebo arm and those twenty consent-form items actually mean, and the questions worth asking.

Patient Education · The Decisions That Are Yours · 15 references
#Clinical Trials#Decisions

Two things up front: across 57 RTOG phase 3 trials investigators judged the standard treatment preferable in 71% of comparisons; and whatever you sign, Article 79-2 of the Medical Care Act says anyone who withdraws consent still receives routine treatment. Not following the consent form is not a failure of yours.

"Doctor, should I join this trial?" usually turns up as the consultation is running out, and the person asking has settled the answer already: a trial is newer, so a trial is better. The first thing I do is take that equals sign away, because the data do not support it. A clinical trial exists to answer a question nobody has an answer to. It is not a treatment designed for you. This article does not talk you into joining or out of it; it is about how to read one, and what to ask.

Randomisation is allowed precisely because nobody knows which side is better

Randomisation gets read as a coin toss, when the precondition is a good deal stricter: allocating a patient at random only stands up while the professional community has no consensus on which of the two approaches is better. That "we don't know" is not a courtesy. An analysis of every completed phase 3 randomised trial run by the US National Cancer Institute's cooperative groups since 1955 — 624 trials, 781 randomised comparisons, 216,451 patients — found that only 30% of trials reached statistical significance; the original investigators judged risk and benefit to favour the new treatment in 41%; and pooling the lot gave a hazard ratio for overall survival of 0.95 (99% CI 0.93–0.98), leaning towards the new treatments, but by very little[1]. The same team's 2012 Cochrane methodology review put 743 randomised trials and 297,744 patients together and saw a roughly symmetrical spread of differences between new and established treatments: the new one wins half the time and loses half[2].

Radiation oncology's own report card is the one I think patients should see

I am a radiation oncologist, so this section uses my own field's data. An analysis covering every completed RTOG phase 3 trial from 1968 to 2002 — 57 trials, 12,734 patients, with a 95% publication rate and no inappropriate choice of control arm — found this. Investigators judged the standard treatment preferable in 71% of comparisons; pooling the data, the odds ratio for survival between new and standard treatment was 1.01 (99% CI 0.96–1.07, P=.5), meaning the two sides were the same; and treatment-related mortality was worse in the experimental arm (odds ratio 1.76, 99% CI 1.01–3.07, P=.008)[3].

The odds ratio needs plain words: it divides one group's ratio of "happened to did not happen" by the other's, and it is not a multiple of probability, so 1.76 does not mean the risk became 1.76 times as big. What it tells you is the direction — deaths caused by the treatment itself were commoner in the experimental arm. The authors' own conclusion was that oncology has no predictable pattern: sometimes innovation is better, sometimes the reverse, and most of the time there is no substantial difference.

The placebo arm is usually "standard treatment plus placebo" — but there are exceptions, and I am not going to mislead you

"Will I be put in the group that gets sugar pills?" is the commonest fear in clinic. For most oncology trials the answer is no: what the placebo replaces is the trial drug, not your anticancer treatment. KEYNOTE-355 is the typical shape — a triple-negative breast cancer trial across 209 centres in 29 countries, both arms on chemotherapy, the difference being whether pembrolizumab or placebo was added on top[4].

But "oncology trials never have a bare placebo arm" is false. The CORRECT trial enrolled patients with metastatic colorectal cancer who had progressed after every approved standard treatment had been used up, randomising them to best supportive care plus regorafenib or best supportive care plus placebo[5]. The premise of that design is exactly one of the acceptable conditions the ASCO expert consensus sets out: existing treatments work poorly, or there is no effective treatment at all. The same consensus carries other ethical conditions — full disclosure is required, and people allocated to placebo must not be made appreciably more likely to die or to suffer irreversible harm[6].

So the question to ask is not "is there a placebo" but "what do I get if I am allocated to the control arm". The answer is in the consent form, because the regulations require the probability of allocation to each arm to be stated.

One thing with nothing to do with joining and everything to do with safety belongs here. The classes of drug named above have their own emergency lists, inside a trial or in routine treatment alike. During treatment, a single reading of 38.0°C or above on an ear or axillary thermometer, chills and shaking, sudden breathlessness or chest pain, or swelling and pain in one calf means the emergency department the same day. Anyone on an immune checkpoint inhibitor adds one more: new diarrhoea, rash, cough, breathlessness, unusual tiredness or palpitations should not be handled by you as a cold or gastroenteritis — contact the team the same day. Reporting these inside a trial carries an extra meaning: they are precisely what the trial is there to collect. Reporting will not get you thrown out; concealing them is what stops you, and everyone after you, from getting the right answer.

A phase 1 trial is asking about dose, not about efficacy

Phase 1 is the easiest to misread. An analysis of non-paediatric phase 1 trials run by the US National Cancer Institute from 1991 to 2002 — 460 trials, 11,935 people — found an overall objective response rate of 10.6%. That is a blended figure: trials containing already-approved anticancer drugs pull the average up, and in the "classic" phase 1 design using a single trial agent the response rate was 4.4%. The other half deserves looking at too: the toxic death rate was 0.49%, and among the 3,465 people with individual data, 14.3% had at least one grade 4 toxicity[7].

An ethics commentary on the same numbers argues that, because surrogate endpoints such as tumour shrinkage carry their own uncertainty, these aggregate figures cannot yield a definite "rate of clinical benefit"; but that phase 1 does offer the possibility of direct medical benefit, and a fully informed patient choosing to take part is making a reasonable decision[8]. I am keeping both of those sentences, because cutting either one would be dishonest.

What to watch for is the "therapeutic misconception" — believing every part of a trial is arranged for your benefit. A qualitative study interviewing 14 patients with end-stage cancer in phase 1 trials found they held unrealistic expectations of therapeutic benefit; at the same time they were positive about taking part, valuing the close medical attention and the feeling of being treated as a distinct person[9]. That feeling of being seen is real. It is simply a separate matter from whether the drug works.

"Can I withdraw?" — that one is written into Article 79-2 of the Medical Care Act

This is the question I am most often asked quietly, usually with "will the hospital give up on me?" attached. In Taiwan the answer is fixed by two pieces of legislation.

Article 79-2 of the Medical Care Act (醫療法): "A medical institution shall provide routine treatment to a person who declines to participate in a human trial or a participant who withdraws consent, and shall not diminish their legitimate medical rights."[10] Article 9 of the Regulations for Good Clinical Practice (藥品優良臨床試驗作業準則): "A subject may withdraw from a clinical trial at any time without giving a reason." Article 8 of the same regulations separately provides that the investigator and associated personnel may not coerce or unduly influence a subject into taking part, nor influence their willingness to continue taking part[11].

Without giving a reason means you do not have to explain. In practice one thing needs doing: tell the investigator, so follow-up and routine treatment join up behind you. Do not simply disappear.

Of the twenty items on that consent form, a few lines are worth reading word by word

A consent form is not a signing ceremony. Article 79 of the Medical Care Act lists at least eight matters that must be disclosed, of which three are most often skipped: other possible treatment options, the right to withdraw consent at any time, and the compensation or insurance arrangements for harm; the same article requires sufficient time to consider and no coercion[10]. Article 22 of the Regulations for Good Clinical Practice goes further, and of its twenty items two lines are worth following with your finger: "the clinical trial is research" and "the trial treatments and the probability of random allocation to each"[11].

Several more articles stand on your side: Article 19 requires all oral and written information to use everyday, non-technical language; Article 18 provides that a consent form may not contain wording making you waive legal rights or releasing the investigator and the institution from liability, and that such wording is void even if written in; Article 23 requires you to receive a signed and dated copy before the trial begins[11]. If you cannot follow it, that is a failure of Article 19, not a failure of yours.

The review layer is not a formality either. The Human Subjects Research Act (人體研究法) requires research to pass an ethics review committee before it can be carried out, with at least five members of whom two-fifths come from outside the institution, and approved projects to be audited at least once a year, with power to order suspension or termination when adverse events are out of the ordinary[12].

If your doctor is the one who raises it, here is what to ask

First, something that changes how you read the word "opportunity". A meta-analysis of 13 studies and 8,883 patients found the patient's own institution simply had no suitable trial in 55.6% of cases (95% CI 43.7–67.3%), and where one existed a further 21.5% were excluded for not meeting the eligibility criteria[13]. What patients say puts them off is fairly consistent: dislike of randomisation, discomfort with the research process, complex and strict protocols, and the presence of a placebo or no-treatment arm[14]. Every one is reasonable, and worth saying in clinic rather than swallowing.

The questions: what is this trial trying to answer, and what is my probability of being allocated to each arm? If I am in the control arm, is what I get the current standard treatment? How many extra hospital trips and which extra tests, over roughly what period? Which costs does the trial pay and which do I pay? Who do I contact, 24 hours a day, if an adverse reaction appears? And if I withdraw, who takes over my treatment?

To search for trials running in Taiwan yourself, the official portal is the Taiwan Clinical Trial Information website of the Food and Drug Administration (食品藥物管理署)[15]. Once you have found something it comes back to the same act: print it out and take it to clinic. How to ask about the cost line is set out in "How to ask about a self-pay item". People who do not join a trial are not getting the second-rate thing — what the control arm receives is the current standard treatment, and that is what I prescribe for most of my patients.

From the physician

Whether your hospital runs a suitable trial and whether you meet the entry criteria are two separate questions, and together they exclude most people — I cannot judge either for you in an article. If a doctor raises it, take the consent form home before signing, and ask whether the control arm gets the current standard treatment and who to call at 3am.
