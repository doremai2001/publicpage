RADIOIODINE

Whether to have radioiodine

On radioiodine, the hardest evidence in the guideline is not the sentence that says give it. It is the sentence that says do not give it routinely — which is the reverse of most people's intuition.

Many people assume low risk simply means no radioiodine. But the box the trials actually cleared had solitary microcarcinoma of 1 cm or less and every node-positive patient excluded from it; so that "low risk" may not be the low risk written on your own report.

The operation is over, the report is back, and the next sentence is often this one: shall we book you in for radioiodine? Some people are told they have to. Some are told they do not. Some take the same report to two places and come home with two answers. Nobody there is lying to you. This question has moved a long way in fifteen years.

Radioiodine (I-131) is given by nuclear medicine, which is not my department. This article deals only with whether. What those days are actually like is in 〈Getting through those few days〉; what happens when it stops working is in 〈After radioiodine stops working〉; how much gland comes out in the first place is in 〈Half the gland, or all of it〉.

Radioiodine is not one thing. It is three

Most of the argument is one word doing three jobs. The first job is clearing away the normal thyroid tissue left behind: however clean the surgery, a little remains, it does you no harm sitting there, but it makes the thyroglobulin number you will be followed on impossible to read. The second is adjuvant treatment: assume cancer cells nobody can see are still somewhere, and sweep once with radioiodine to lower the chance of recurrence. The third is treating disease you can already see. The 2025 American Thyroid Association guideline splits its advice into four sentences; the first three cover the first two jobs, and the fourth is about distant metastases[1]. The 2022 European Thyroid Association consensus cuts it the same way[2]. What someone in the low-risk box is being offered is almost always the first job.

Two randomised trials, and what they asked was whether

Both are non-inferiority trials, and both fixed the margin, agreed in advance, at five percentage points.

ESTIMABL2, in France, took 776 people, all of whom had had a total thyroidectomy, and randomised them to have radioiodine or not. Event-free rates at three years were 95.6% without it and 95.9% with it; at five years 698 were evaluable, 93.2% against 94.8%, both inside the margin[3][4]. An "event" here is a composite — an abnormality on imaging or on ultrasound, a rise in thyroglobulin or in the antibodies. It is not recurrence, and it is certainly not survival.

What it proved has to be written in the same sentence as who it kept out: once solitary papillary microcarcinoma of 1 cm or less had been excluded outright (the trial's own stated reason was that these people do not need it), and every node-positive patient and everything pT2 and above had been excluded as well, the people left in that one box did no worse for not having radioiodine[3]. Who was left: multifocal disease summing to between 1 and 2 cm, or a single tumour of 1 to 2 cm, with nodes negative or not assessed.

IoN, in the UK, asked the same question across 33 centres in 504 people, with median follow-up of 6.8 and 6.6 years. Five-year recurrence-free rates were 97.9% (95% confidence interval 96.1 to 99.7) without ablation and 96.3% (93.9 to 98.7) with it[5].

The figure the trial reports is not those two subtracted. It separately estimates a 5-year absolute risk difference of 0.5 percentage points (95% confidence interval −2.2 to 3.2), signed as the excess risk carried by leaving ablation out, and non-inferiority is declared because the top of that interval sits under the 5-point margin fixed in advance[5]. Subtract the two rates and you get 1.6 points, pointing the other way. How the 0.5 was estimated I cannot tell you: the paper's methods section was not obtainable. Its endpoint is a different animal too: locoregional recurrent or persistent structural disease, distant metastases and death from thyroid cancer counted together, and across the whole trial there were 17 recurrences. The paper says it is still in follow-up. And the authors' conclusion is narrower than their own entry criteria — pT1, pT2, nodes negative or not assessed, no adverse features.

The part of IoN worth reading twice

Of the 46 people staged pT3, 4 recurred (9%); of the 458 staged pT1 or pT2, 13 did (3%). Of the 47 with positive central compartment nodes, 6 recurred (13%); of the 457 node-negative or not assessed, 11 did (2%)[5].

Two things, and they have to be held together. First, the people at higher risk really are at higher risk — threefold, and more than sixfold. Second, the sentence the paper writes next: those rates were similar among the people who did not have ablation. Then the limit. Failing to see a difference inside a subgroup is not the same as there being none.

The recommendation strengths come in an order nobody expects

The 2025 guideline sorts recurrence risk into four boxes and hangs a probability on each: low (under 10%), low-intermediate (10 to 15%), intermediate-high (16 to 30%), high (over 30%)[1]. Those four percentages are probabilities of structural or biochemical recurrence. They are not death rates, and they are not stage — stage is a separate system, and it is in 〈Why the stage depends on your age〉.

Now read the strengths of those four recommendations in order. For low risk, "not routinely" is a strong recommendation on high certainty evidence. For high risk, "routinely" is a strong recommendation on moderate certainty. For distant metastases present at diagnosis, "give it" is also strong, on moderate certainty. For the two middle boxes, "may be considered" is a conditional recommendation on low certainty[1]. Laid out that way it is plain enough: the hardest evidence in this whole question is the sentence that says do not do it routinely. The guideline explains its own softness in the middle — there are very few studies of people who sit only in the middle. A systematic review lays it barer still, though it is counting a different set of people — the middle box of the older three-tier stratification: 11 observational studies saw a benefit from ablation and 13 did not, and its authors add that only a few of them had properly stratified populations[6]. The boundaries have been redrawn since, and that middle is not today's two middle boxes; the shape of the disagreement has not changed.

This article cannot lean all one way either. For people at high risk, and for people who have distant metastases from the start, the guideline says give it routinely. The European consensus likewise lists high risk and known structural disease as indications (that consensus attaches no strength or certainty grade to any of its recommendations), and it names the reason low risk is so hard to prove anything in — the base rate is tiny: dying of thyroid cancer under 1%, persistent or recurrent disease 2 to 3%. It keeps one sentence for microcarcinoma: papillary microcarcinoma of 1 cm or less, with no other high-risk features, should not have it[2] — that sentence covers 1 cm and under, and does not stretch upward onto 1 to 4 cm.

How much is a different question

People string four trials into one line and say all four prove radioiodine can be scaled back. No. In HiLo and in ESTIMABL1, every single person had radioiodine; what was randomised was the activity, low or higher (1.1 against 3.7 GBq — that is how those two trials are identified, it is nobody's prescription), and the method of preparation[7][8]. On whether to have it at all, they answer nothing.

Their primary endpoints are surrogates too: whether the remnant tissue had been cleared six to nine months later. In HiLo the success rate was 85.0% on the low activity against 88.9% on the higher[7]; followed to a median of 6.5 years, cumulative recurrence at seven years was 5.9% against 7.3%, hazard ratio 1.10 (95% confidence interval 0.47 to 2.59)[9]. How much you are given this time is set by your hospital's nuclear medicine department.

The other side of the ledger

On second primary cancers I am putting three sets of numbers down together, because they do not point the same way and picking one would be choosing a position on your behalf. A 2009 meta-analysis, treated against untreated: relative risk of any second primary cancer 1.19 (95% confidence interval 1.04 to 1.36), leukaemia 2.5 (1.13 to 5.53) — but it pooled two studies and 16,502 people[10]. A 2018 one: 0.98 (0.76 to 1.27) unadjusted, and 1.16 (0.97 to 1.39) after adjustment for confounders, not statistically significant[11]. A 2022 critical review gathered ten studies: solid second cancers between 1.14 and 1.84, most of them not significant; haematological malignancies between 1.30 and 2.50, two-thirds of them significant; and the grade it gave that evidence was very low (low for the blood arm)[12].

The blood arm is the more consistent one, but the absolute numbers have to sit beside it. In a database analysis of 148,215 people, after a median 6.5 years, 783 across both arms developed a subsequent haematological malignancy — around five per thousand. On that base, the hazard ratio for acute myeloid leukaemia was 1.79 (1.13 to 2.82) and for chronic myeloid leukaemia 3.44 (1.87 to 6.36), and it was visible in the low- and intermediate-risk groups as well (that analysis used the older three-tier stratification)[13]. Write the denominator out, or "more than three times" gets read as three in ten people getting leukaemia.

A relationship with cumulative activity does exist. A cohort pooling Sweden, Italy and France, 6,841 people with papillary and follicular cancer, 62% of them treated with radioiodine, worked out that for each further unit of I-131 given there were 14.4 excess solid cancers and 0.8 excess leukaemias per 100,000 person-years[14]. That is not a reason not to have it. It is a reason to think before a further course.

On survival, this is as far as I can go

I will not write that radioiodine does not lengthen life in low-risk patients, because no randomised trial has been run with overall survival as its primary endpoint (IoN's composite endpoint counts death from thyroid cancer, which is not overall survival). Pool the two trials, 1,280 people: hazard ratio for recurrence-free survival 0.96 (0.80 to 1.15), all-cause death 1.28 (0.48 to 3.41) — intervals wide enough to straddle 1[15]. That says no difference is visible. It does not say equally safe.

One more thing explains why you hear different answers. In 2017 the two nuclear medicine societies and the two thyroid associations, European and American, set up an intersocietal working group on exactly these controversies. Their 2020 progress report says the available evidence was found insufficient to provide any definitive guidance, and that they could not arrive at consensus[16]. When four societies cannot settle it between them, two doctors giving you two accounts is ordinary.

Whether to have it: how to ask

Asking at the right level usually beats asking one more person. Take the report in with you and go down these five:

  • "Which of the guideline's four recurrence-risk boxes does my report put me in?"
  • "When you recommend it, is that so the follow-up numbers can be read, or to treat cancer cells that might still be there?"
  • "For my box, is the guideline's advice a strong recommendation or a conditional one?"
  • "If I do not have it, how does follow-up get arranged, and what would bring us back to this decision?"
  • "If I do have it, might I need a second course later?"

And if you land in one of the two middle boxes, one more sentence, said before the rest: "I am more frightened of recurrence" — or "I am more frightened of another dose of radiation". Then: "Knowing that, does it change what you would advise?"

From the physician

Three sentences you can read out at the next appointment: "Which of the guideline's four recurrence-risk boxes does my report put me in?" "When you recommend it, is that so the follow-up numbers can be read, or to treat cancer cells that might still be there?" "For my box, is that a strong recommendation or a conditional one?" If the answer is conditional, add one more: "Then which way do you lean, and why?"
