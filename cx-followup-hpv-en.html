AFTER TREATMENT

Follow-up, vaccines and smears from here on

Every three to four months for the first two years, a symptom review plus a pelvic examination — while for smears after radiotherapy, what the guideline writes is not recommended. Which population the vaccine evidence belongs to also has to be labelled clearly.

Cervical Cancer Guide · After Treatment · 9 references
#Follow-up#HPV & Vaccines#Screening#NHI Coverage

"Treatment is over — why are there fewer tests, not more?" That sparseness has sources. By the end you will know which of the "not scheduled" items are the guideline being followed, and which kinds of discomfort mean not waiting for the next visit.

My position first: definitive chemoradiation and brachytherapy are the treatments I deliver every day. So every sentence about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it — not more warmly.

On the day treatment ends, many people assume what follows is "a smear at every visit and a whole-body scan every year". The real follow-up table is nothing like that: it is sparser than you imagine — and for those who have had radiotherapy, what the guideline says about smears is "not recommended". Three things need separating clearly first: how follow-up is scheduled; whether getting vaccinated now is of any use; and what the smear-screening system has to do with you now.

The skeleton of follow-up is simpler than you imagine

The evidence-based guideline from Ontario, Canada writes the frequency out clearly: every 3 to 4 months for the first two years, every 6 to 12 months in years three to five, and back to general gynaecological care after five years; the content of each visit is a symptom review plus a full physical examination (including a pelvic examination); PET, other imaging and tumour markers are not recommended as routine[1]. Europe's ESGO 2023 guideline points the same way: follow-up individualised by risk, with imaging and blood tests kept for "symptoms or suspected recurrence" — that is the wording of a [IV, A] recommendation, not cost-cutting[2]. One sentence to stop a misreading: what is not recommended is "routine" imaging — it does not mean no scan even when there are symptoms; symptom-triggered testing is exactly the kind the guideline wants doctors to use[2].

But the base under this table is thinner than it looks: how close follow-up should be has never been answered by a good randomised trial. The 2026 clinical practice statement of the Society of Gynecologic Oncology (SGO) admits it itself: the evidence in this field is weak, mostly retrospective, and practice varies widely between institutions[3]. So if your follow-up table differs from someone else's in a patient group, it is mostly not because anyone is cutting corners — the evidence simply cannot hold up one standard table.

The real surveillance instrument is you — the symptoms between visits

The same SGO statement makes the central point: the most effective way of detecting recurrence is symptom review plus physical examination[3]. Most recurrences are caught because of symptoms or an abnormal pelvic examination, not swept up by routine tests. So what I ask patients to watch is a list — anything on it that appears between two visits and persists beyond two or three weeks means coming back early to say so: abnormal vaginal bleeding or discharge; bleeding after sex; persistent pain fixed in one spot in the pelvis, buttock or leg; swelling of one leg; a cough or breathlessness that becomes the norm; losing weight for no reason. This list costs nothing, and it is more useful than most tests. What roads exist once something is caught is the business of the next article, "After recurrence: the roads that remain" — not expanded here.

A follow-up visit does not mean a smear — after radiotherapy, the guideline writes "not recommended"

This section surprises a lot of people. Cytology smears of the cervix after radiotherapy: the ESGO recommendation is "not recommended", level [IV, D]; for those whose surgery has left only a vaginal vault, vault smears are likewise [IV, D] not recommended[2]; the Ontario guideline says vault cytology at most once a year, and no more often[1]. The reason is not that smears are bad — it is that radiation-induced cellular change makes interpretation unreliable: an "abnormal" report drags in a string of unnecessary investigations, and a "normal" one guarantees nothing either. So when the doctor does only a pelvic examination at review and books no smear, it is mostly not an omission; it is the guideline being followed. The exception is those treated with fertility preservation whose cervix is still in place: that group's follow-up should include HPV testing, guideline level [V, A][2].

Using HPV testing to catch recurrence? Two sources, opposite directions

The Ontario guideline mentions in one line that HPV DNA testing "may" be a more sensitive method of detecting recurrence after radiotherapy[1]. The measured data poured cold water on that: one US institution looked back at 262 survivors, and among those who had high-risk HPV testing, positivity did not differ between those who recurred and those who did not (21% against 24%) — and not a single recurrence was caught by HPV testing; a positive result bought nothing but more tests, and the authors' conclusion is that routine use is not supported[4]. One guideline says possibly useful; one measurement says it was not — this question does not have an answer yet. I do not put it into my own routine; if your hospital schedules it, it is fair to ask one question: what is it meant to answer?

Vaccination after treatment: the pre-cancer evidence cannot be extrapolated to you

"I've already had it — is the vaccine any use to me now?" First pin down where the evidence lives. The numbers exist for the pre-cancer population treated with conisation: a 2022 meta-analysis in the BMJ found that those vaccinated after local excision had roughly four-tenths the risk of recurrent CIN2-or-worse lesions of the unvaccinated (relative risk 0.43) — but GRADE rated the evidence quality very low to moderate, most of the included studies were observational, and the authors themselves say the data cannot yet settle the question[5]. As for people who have already had invasive cancer — whether vaccination can prevent recurrence: the direct evidence is zero. I have searched the literature databases, and it is zero. If anyone quotes that 0.43 to persuade you into paying for the vaccine, you should know it is a number from the pre-cancer population, not from yours. Whether to vaccinate then becomes an ordinary adult catch-up question: the US Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices recommends catch-up vaccination up to age 26; for ages 27 to 45 there is no routine recommendation — it becomes shared decision-making, because the older you are, the more likely you have already been exposed, and the benefit diminishes[6]. For Taiwan's official age guidance on self-paid adult catch-up vaccination I could not find a provision I could cite — discuss it with gynaecology or family medicine.

For your daughter and your family, Taiwan has a clear answer

On your own side the evidence is unsettled; on the next generation's side the policy is clear. From September 2025, Taiwan's publicly funded HPV vaccination expands from junior-high girls to all junior-high students — boys included — using the 9-valent vaccine, 2 doses for those under 15, at least 6 months apart, given at school vaccination sessions; anyone who misses a session can take the catch-up notice to a contracted clinic[7]. If there is a junior-high child at home, this costs nothing and needs no agonising: let them have it. Adult sisters take the sentence from the previous section: catch-up under 26; at 27 to 45, talk to a doctor[6].

Your own smears: two systems — do not blend them together

Publicly funded screening is a system for the "general population": from 2025, a publicly funded smear every 3 years at ages 25 to 29, one available every year from age 30 (with the official advice being at least once every 3 years), and one publicly funded HPV test each at ages 35, 45 and 65[8][9]. You are not in that system right now — for the first five years after treatment you are on the follow-up table above, and whether smears are done, and how often, is decided by the follow-up protocol, not by screening policy. Those who have had radical surgery no longer have a cervix; all that can be smeared is the vaginal vault, and as the earlier section said, the guideline advises against doing that routinely[2]; after radiotherapy the cervix is still there, but the cytology cannot be read reliably, and it is equally not recommended[2]. When you return to general gynaecological care after five years[1], confirm with the doctor then which box you belong in. As to whether the publicly funded HPV test is clinician-collected or self-collected, the official page does not say — go by what the participating clinic explains on site.

Beyond the follow-up table: three boxes for the coming year's calendar

Box one: book the whole of the next twelve months of visits now — in the first two years that is every 3 to 4 months, so three to four visits a year[1]; do not wait for a symptom to remind you to register. Box two: copy the symptom list from the second section into your phone, and set one rule: any item persisting beyond two or three weeks means an earlier visit — no waiting for the next slot. Box three, the family: a junior-high child means watching for September's vaccination notice[7], and adult sisters follow the publicly funded schedule for smears and HPV testing[8]. As for whether you yourself should have any additional test, ask it this way at the next visit: "Given my stage and my treatment, what will you order over the coming year, what won't you order, and why?" Understand the reasons behind the "won't" — and the follow-up table becomes truly your own.

From the physician

No smear on the follow-up schedule is mostly not an omission — the guideline never recommended one. Copy the symptom list into your phone, and any item persisting beyond two or three weeks means an earlier visit; if there is a junior-high student at home, do not miss September's publicly funded 9-valent vaccine notice — boys get it too.
