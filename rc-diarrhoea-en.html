DURING TREATMENT

Ten trips to the toilet and nothing feels finished

Which week it starts, which week is worst, what the guidelines really say about probiotics and sucralfate, and when to call the same day.

Rectal Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 10 references
#Side Effects#Bowel Function#Daily Life#Nutrition

Most of what gets recommended for this — sucralfate, mesalazine, misoprostol — the guidelines actively recommend against. Here is what the evidence levels actually say, and the one decision you must never make on your own.

By week three or four of pelvic radiotherapy the sentence I hear most is "I'm on the toilet a dozen times a day and it never feels finished." The field has to cross the rectum and the anal canal, and that lining is one of the fastest-renewing tissues in the body, so it breaks down early and obviously. Nothing has gone wrong.

What you need first is the shape of the curve: which week gets difficult, which is worst, and how long it keeps deteriorating after the last fraction. Know that and you will not phone at the peak asking whether treatment has failed.

How many people, and how bad does it get?

About 80% of patients having pelvic radiotherapy develop gastrointestinal symptoms during the course; up to a third are already malnourished before it starts, and up to four-fifths lose weight during it[1].

But "has symptoms" and "severe" are different things. The best-documented rectal chemoradiation cohort — 177 patients, 50.4 Gy with 5-FU or capecitabine — put grade 2 or worse diarrhoea, bad enough to disrupt daily life and need treating, at 25%, and grade 3 or worse at only 5%. Grade 2 or worse radiation proctitis was 32%; grade 3 or worse proctitis was 0%[2].

Needing admission is the minority. The 27% quoted everywhere from the German CAO/ARO/AIO-94 trial is the total of grade 3 and 4 acute toxicity of every kind with preoperative chemoradiation, against 40% postoperatively — not the diarrhoea rate alone[3]. When you see it written up as "a third of patients get severe diarrhoea", put a question mark next to it.

Which week does it start, and how long after the last fraction?

The 2025 British Society of Gastroenterology (BSG) guidance on gastrointestinal symptoms after cancer treatment, 103 recommendations long, is blunt: acute GI symptoms usually begin in the second week after radiotherapy starts, tend to peak in the last week of treatment, and continue for at least one to two weeks after it finishes[4].

The same rectal cohort published week by week. For grade 2 or worse diarrhoea, women went from 1.4% in week one to 33.3% in week five (7.4%, 15.7% and 24.2% in weeks two to four); men over the same weeks were 0%, 2.3%, 8.6%, 10.8% and 12.2%. The proctitis curve is steeper: 3.4% to 38.2% in patients under 60, and 0% to 23.4% in those 60 and over[2].

The shape matters more than the numbers: two quiet weeks, trouble from week three, the last week worst, then a few more days of deterioration after the final fraction before it turns.

Now the counter-intuitive part, for anyone on short-course radiotherapy, five fractions of 5 Gy. The same guidance states that with large fractions acute symptoms may not start before treatment is completed[4].

You may walk out on the fifth day feeling fine and start with the diarrhoea and urgency a week later. Unwarned, that gap is almost always read as "something has gone wrong". It has not. The two schedules put the trouble in different places.

Same dose — so why does the person next to me barely notice?

How much bowel sits in the high-dose region. The same study's dose–volume analysis found the volume of bowel receiving 45 Gy or more (V45) was the strongest predictor of grade 2 or worse diarrhoea. Nobody with a V45 under 3% had it; at a V45 of 9% the risk rose from under 10% to over 20%; with a V45 above 35%, more than half. Anal canal volume receiving 15 Gy or more tracked with proctitis, more weakly[2].

That is what intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) is for — moving high dose off the small bowel. In the same patients, grade 2 or worse diarrhoea in week five was 10.8% with IMRT against 32.3% with conventional three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (p=0.001)[2].

What the guidelines back, and what they rule out by name

The MASCC/ISOO mucositis guideline has a whole gastrointestinal section. I quote the levels as printed[5]:

  • Oral sucralfate for treating radiotherapy-induced gastrointestinal mucositis is a Level I recommendation against — an against verdict at the strongest evidence tier there is. That is the opposite direction to the folk advice.

  • Sucralfate enemas do carry a suggestion in favour (Level III), but read the indication: to treat chronic radiation proctitis in patients with rectal bleeding — months or years after the course, not week-four tenesmus.

  • Oral 5-ASA, mesalazine and olsalazine, for preventing acute radiotherapy diarrhoea: Level I against. Misoprostol suppositories: Level I against.

  • The Level II recommendation for octreotide is confined to chemotherapy- or transplant-related diarrhoea when loperamide has failed. Radiotherapy diarrhoea is not in it.

BSG 2025 is plainer still: the studies of these agents are "few, small and often contradictory so these agents should not be used except in clinical trials" — and the list names sucralfate, octreotide, misoprostol and amifostine[4]. The 2019 MASCC systematic review likewise could not produce a single new guideline for any agent[6].

Probiotics are a suggestion, not a recommendation — and there is a safety clause

This is what families bring into the room most often. Probiotics containing Lactobacillus, for preventing diarrhoea during radiotherapy or chemoradiation in pelvic tumours, sit in the guideline as a suggestion at Level of Evidence III — the weakest tier that still yields a guideline at all. The 2019 review and the 2020 update both left it there[7]. A suggestion is one rung below a recommendation.

The caveat matters more than the level. Wedlake's review of nutritional strategies in pelvic radiotherapy puts it without hedging: probiotics show promise, but cannot enter routine clinical practice without rigorous safety analysis, not least in immunocompromised patients[8]. You are on concurrent 5-FU or capecitabine and your white count will fall. Live-organism preparations in a neutropenic patient are a documented route to bacteraemia.

Do not start a probiotic without your treating team's agreement. If you are already on one, stop it and tell your doctor when you are neutropenic or febrile.

What you can actually do, day by day

Loperamide is the standard first-line antidiarrhoeal for uncomplicated diarrhoea, and chronic low- to intermediate-grade symptoms can be managed on continued loperamide[9]. Honestly, that position is extrapolated from chemotherapy diarrhoea, not grown from a radiotherapy-specific randomised trial. Take it as prescribed, and do not keep escalating the dose yourself to push symptoms down — it masks obstruction and can precipitate toxic megacolon. BSG says it is generally safe to start loperamide while waiting for stool results, but to reassess regularly to exclude toxic dilatation of the colon[4].

One sentence has to be here: however bad it gets, never decide on your own to miss today's fraction or push the course back. The length of the course is part of the treatment, and gaps in the middle cost you effect. When you cannot cope, phone the same day so the team can adjust your supportive medication; if a break is needed, the doctor makes that call with you. That decision is taken together, not at home.

If diarrhoea persists long after the course ends, take one more term to clinic: bile-acid diarrhoea. BSG lists it as a frequent and, crucially, testable cause after cancer treatment — diagnosed on a SeHCAT scan, with sequestrants used after the diagnosis, not tried first to see[4].

Taichung Veterans General Hospital's radiotherapy handout for colorectal cancer teaches a bland, low-residue diet, plenty of fluids, a warm sitz bath after opening your bowels, and care of the skin around the anus[10]. About the sitz bath I will be straight: in what I could find, there is no guideline-level evidence that sitz baths or topical steroids treat acute radiation proctitis. They are comfort measures, not treatment, and having done one is no reason to delay reporting a symptom. The evidence on low-residue diets is messier than people assume — that is written up in "Low-residue eating has less evidence than you think".

Call the same day — do not save these for your next appointment

You are on chemotherapy at the same time. For any of these, contact the team that day or go to the emergency department:

  • A fever of 38 °C or above, especially with diarrhoea — treated as possible neutropenic sepsis, not as something to watch for a day.

  • Severe diarrhoea with dehydration: dizziness, blacking out on standing, very little urine, a dry mouth.

  • Mouth ulcers bad enough that you cannot swallow.

  • Numbness or tingling in the hands and feet that is worsening. On oxaliplatin, watch for cold-induced neuropathy — tingling in hands, feet or throat that sharpens or lasts longer on touching cold water, drinking something cold or going into cold air.

  • On capecitabine, redness, peeling, tingling or pain in the palms and soles bad enough to stop you walking (hand-foot syndrome). Report it; do not reduce or stop the drug yourself.

  • Severe abdominal pain, a hard distended abdomen, no flatus and no stool — this can be obstruction or ischaemic bowel and needs surgical assessment that day[4].

  • Heavy rectal bleeding, or diarrhoea with chills and shivering[10].

Diarrhoea with fever, abdominal pain or dehydration counts as complicated diarrhoea and may need antibiotics and admission[9]. Plain diarrhoea you can start managing yourself; diarrhoea plus anything else you should not.

Get hold of these before week three arrives

Two numbers first: roughly what the bowel V45 is on your plan, and whether you are being treated with IMRT. They decide which band of risk you sit in, and you can ask for both across the desk.

Then a threshold and a phone number. When may you take the antidiarrhoeal yourself, and at what point should you ring instead — ask your doctor for one concrete sentence. Where do you call if things worsen at night or at the weekend? Put it in your phone before you need it. Too many people hold out until Monday morning and then say "actually it started on Friday night". That weekend of dehydration was avoidable.

From the physician

Your bowel V45 and whether you are on IMRT are the two numbers that decide which risk band you sit in, and both are available across the desk at your next appointment. Nothing here replaces the threshold your own doctor sets for when to phone rather than wait. If you take one thing away, take the rule about never rescheduling a fraction yourself.
