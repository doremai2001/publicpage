AFTER THE DIAGNOSIS

The few lines on the report that change treatment

Grade Group, PSA density, positive cores, cribriform, intraductal — which lines on the report actually change what happens next.

Prostate Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 18 references
#Prostate#Staging#Biomarkers#Decisions#Getting Ready

Ten minutes in clinic is not enough for this, and most of what you read online is written about someone else's report. Take yours out, and go line by line.

A prostate cancer pathology report is a few short lines, and they settle more than any scan. Clinic gives you under ten minutes on them, so this is for reading at home against your own report.

The order below is set by one thing: can the item change your treatment. What you can follow but that changes nothing is noise.

PSA has no safe value, and it is not specific to cancer

The PCPT placebo arm held 2,950 men whose PSA had never exceeded 4.0 ng/mL and whose rectal examination was normal. All were biopsied in year seven: 449 (15.2%) had prostate cancer, and 67 of those (14.9%) were Gleason ≥7. By stratum: 6.6% for PSA ≤0.5, 10.1% for 0.6–1.0, 17.0% for 1.1–2.0, 23.9% for 2.1–3.0, 26.9% for 3.1–4.0; the share of high-grade cancer within those climbed from 12.5% to 25.0%[1].

Read that 15.2% carefully. It is a prevalence from biopsying everyone at seven years regardless of the number, not "15% of men with a normal PSA have a cancer to deal with today", and not an argument for biopsying everyone. It establishes one thing: 4.0 is not a wall. PSA is a continuous risk, not a pass mark.

The reverse holds too. The Ministry of Health and Welfare's page states that PSA is affected by age, race, benign prostatic enlargement, prostate infection and certain medical procedures[2]. One high reading is not cancer. Ask first what you did in the days before the test.

Three derived numbers on the report, and only one of them holds up

Free PSA percentage: in 773 men (379 with cancer, 394 benign) across several centres, all with a normal rectal examination and total PSA 4.0 to 10.0 ng/mL, a cut-off of free PSA ≤25% detected 95% of cancers while avoiding 20% of unnecessary biopsies; the lower the free PSA the higher the individual risk (8% to 56%); it was an independent predictor there (odds ratio 3.2, 95% confidence interval 2.5–4.1) while total PSA gave only 1.0 (0.92–1.11)[3]. An odds ratio divides one group's odds of having against not having by the other's — not a multiple of probability, so 3.2 is not 3.2 times the chance of cancer; the 95% confidence interval in brackets is the range the true value plausibly sits in, and narrower is stronger. That 25% holds only for a normal rectal examination, total PSA 4.0 to 10.0, and that assay platform. Taiwanese hospitals differ, so use the reference range on your own report.

PSA velocity, the rate of rise: in 5,519 biopsied men from the PCPT control arm, adding velocity to a multivariable model lifted discrimination only from 0.702 to 0.709; biopsying men with a normal PSA and rectal examination but high velocity would mean roughly one cancer per seven biopsies. The authors' conclusion is hard: this measure should not be incorporated into clinical guidelines[4]. Men bring three years of PSA graphs and ask about the slope; I steer back to the absolute value and density.

PSA density, PSA divided by prostate volume, is the one that stands. In the Johns Hopkins cohort of 1,298 men on active surveillance, each 0.1 rise in density carried a hazard ratio of 1.21 (95% confidence interval 1.12–1.46) for pathological upgrading and 1.38 (1.22–1.56) for going to radical treatment[5]. A hazard ratio is not a multiple of probability; it compares the relative rate of events over the same stretch of time. Data on 405 Taiwanese men from National Taiwan University Hospital and Linkou Chang Gung agrees: density ≥0.15 predicted later reclassification (hazard ratio 1.84, 1.17–2.88)[6].

Why the report now says Grade Group instead of "six out of ten"

The 2014 ISUP consensus converted Gleason into five Grade Groups: GG1 is Gleason ≤6, GG2 is 3+4, GG3 is 4+3, GG4 is 8, GG5 is 9 to 10. It is blunt about why the old wording had to go: told his score is "6 out of 10", a patient hears mid-range and believes he has a more aggressive cancer than he does[7]. In reality 6 is the system's lowest score.

The five tiers are not a rebranding. 20,845 radical prostatectomies at five academic centres, 2005 to 2014, validated in 5,501 radiotherapy patients at two more centres: with Gleason 6 as reference, hazard ratios for biochemical recurrence were 1.9 for 3+4, 5.1 for 4+3, 8.0 for 8 and 11.7 for 9–10[8]. Look between 3+4 and 4+3: both print as "7", nearly threefold apart. There is a large step between 8 and 9 too.

The limitation belongs in the same breath: that endpoint is biochemical recurrence, not death. 11.7 cannot be turned into "11.7 times the risk of dying".

"Gleason 6 isn't cancer" describes surgical pathology, not your biopsy

A line has circulated for a few years: Gleason 6 should not be called cancer at all. There is hard data behind it. Four academic centres whole-organ sectioned 14,123 radical prostatectomy specimens with pelvic node dissection; 22 had node metastases, and all 19 that could be re-reviewed were upgraded. The authors' conclusion, word for word: no tumour with GS ≤6 showed lymph node metastasis[9]. Population data runs the same way: in SEER (19,594 men) and the NCDB (57,540 men) with pathological Gleason ≤6 and node dissection, node-positive rates were 0.2% and 0.18%[10].

There is a wrong turn here that is easy to take, and it does harm.

Both datasets describe the final pathology after radical surgery — the whole prostate out and sectioned throughout. What you hold is a biopsy report: a few needles through an organ of thirty or fifty grams. Biopsies underestimate. A meta-analysis of 17 studies from 9 countries covering 6,039 men on active surveillance found a pooled upgrade rate at first confirmatory biopsy of 20% (95% confidence interval 19–21%)[11]. One in five had a grade that was an underestimate.

There is only one honest reading. Gleason 6 on final pathology hardly ever metastasises, which is the pathological basis for monitoring rather than treating it at once; but a biopsy reporting 6 does not mean there is nothing higher-grade inside you, which is why confirmatory biopsy exists. A biopsy of 6 plus not coming back is the dangerous reading here. What monitoring involves and when to come off it is in "Active surveillance is neither stalling nor gambling".

Two small words on the report matter more than the score

Cribriform and intraductal. The 2019 ISUP consensus requires invasive cribriform carcinoma to be stated in the report and brings intraductal carcinoma into grading[12]; the 2014 consensus had already made cribriform always pattern 4, and gave intraductal carcinoma with no invasive component no Gleason score but a note that it almost always accompanies invasive prostate cancer[7].

The numbers. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 31 studies (mean follow-up 62.87 months) found cribriform associated with extraprostatic extension (odds ratio 1.96) and with worse biochemical recurrence and cancer-specific mortality; every study included was observational, mean quality score 6.27[13]. For intraductal carcinoma, 13 studies and 4,179 patients gave hazard ratios of 2.31 (1.96–2.73) for progression-free survival, 1.89 (1.28–2.77) for cancer-specific survival and 2.14 (1.53–3.01) for overall survival[14].

Write that as "associated with worse outcomes", not "causes them": observational data, and reading these two patterns has a recognised agreement problem between pathologists. They do change decisions, though — the two words are exclusion and exit criteria for active surveillance, and the guideline wording and sources are in "Active surveillance is neither stalling nor gambling".

How many cores, what proportion, and which box you end up in

The number and percentage of positive cores are not decoration. Among 1,056 men with T1c to T3N0M0 disease treated with radiotherapy, median follow-up 7.6 years, more than 50% of cores positive carried a hazard ratio for distant metastasis of 4.01 (95% confidence interval 1.86–8.61), and on multivariable analysis independently predicted distant metastasis, cancer-specific survival and overall survival; T stage and perineural invasion predicted only locoregional recurrence[15].

One grey area first. Among 315 men with a biopsy of Gleason 6 or 3+4 who went to radical surgery, grouping by "highest pattern 4 on any single core below 5%" gave a group whose adverse pathology and biochemical recurrence did not differ significantly from Gleason 6; grouping the same men by overall pattern 4 below 5% made them significantly worse[16]. One phenomenon, two ways of counting, opposite conclusions. "My pattern 4 is only a trace" is not automatically Gleason 6.

Last comes risk grouping, and there are two systems. EAU: low risk is ISUP GG1, PSA under 10 ng/mL, cT1-2; intermediate splits into favourable (GG2 with PSA under 10, or GG1 with PSA 10–20) and unfavourable (GG2 with PSA 10–20, or GG3); high risk is GG4 or GG5, or PSA above 20 — with no very-low and no very-high box[17]. NCCN's 2026 patient version has five: low, favorable intermediate, unfavorable intermediate, high, very high, splitting the intermediate pair on whether more than half the cores are positive, with a PSA threshold above 40 for very high risk[18]. The same man can land in different boxes. Nobody miscalculated; the definitions differ.

One line on T stage. T2a is tumour in half of one lobe or less, T2b more than half but not both lobes, T2c both lobes; T3a is outside the prostate but not into the seminal vesicles, T3b into them; T4 invades bladder, rectum, pelvic wall or pelvic muscle[18]. On what clinical T stage rests, NCCN is direct: the rectal examination, or examination of prostate tissue. It is felt, not read off an MRI[18]. What MRI can actually do for staging is in "Two scans, answering different questions".

Four questions to ask with the report open in front of you

First, which Grade Group am I? If it is a 7, is it 3+4 or 4+3?

Second, does cribriform or intraductal appear anywhere on it?

Third, how many cores are positive, what proportion, and what is my PSA density?

Fourth, which risk grouping are you using and which box am I in — would the other system put me elsewhere?

If your grade sits on the line that changes treatment, there is a fifth: can this biopsy be sent for a second-opinion read? Why that is worth asking is in "This first month: order matters more than speed".

From the physician

Two reports with the same Gleason score can lead to different treatments, because grade, density, core burden and pattern do not move together. If your grade sits on the line between 3+4 and 4+3, ask at your next visit whether the biopsy can go for a second-opinion read — that single question changes the decision more often than any extra scan.
