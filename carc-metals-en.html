AT WORK

Electroplating, welding, and that old well

Hexavalent chromium goes for the nose and not the nasopharynx, nickel jewellery is an allergy problem rather than a cancer one, the evidence tying cadmium to prostate cancer does not hold up — and blackfoot disease remains one of the world's most important arsenic datasets.

These four metals get written onto the same exam paper, and so get remembered as the same thing. Their target organs differ, the strength of the evidence differs, and even the answer to “what happens if I wear nickel earrings” is not in the cancer box at all.

In clinic I always ask what work you used to do. The four commonest answers: beside the electroplating tank, the sparks of welding, the gas lines of a semiconductor plant, and a well in the south sunk decades ago. They damage different organs, so I write them separately — several parts of the version that mixes them together online are wrong, and the most expensive error is calling sinonasal cancer nasopharyngeal cancer.

Hexavalent chromium goes for the nose before it goes for the lung

Monograph 100C of the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) places hexavalent chromium compounds in Group 1, with sufficient evidence for lung cancer and a positive association also observed with cancer of the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses[1]. Group 1 describes how certain the evidence is, not how dangerous the agent is — "Group 1 is not a danger ranking" and this topic's entry page, "Carcinogens at work and at home", cover that in full. Let me block one misreading straight away: it is the nasal cavity and sinuses, not nasopharyngeal cancer; the lead role in nasopharyngeal cancer belongs to formaldehyde, and that question is in "How much should the formaldehyde in a new home worry you?".

What I usually see first in clinic is not cancer either. The Reference Guideline for the Recognition of Poisoning by Chromic Acid and its Salts or Dichromic Acid and its Salts, from the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), Ministry of Labor, is quite specific: inhaling mist containing hexavalent chromium can produce a painless ulcer of the nasal septum in as little as two weeks; chromium ulcers on skin are sharply demarcated deep round holes; the high-risk trades are stainless steel, welding, electroplating and anodising, paint, textiles, tanning and glassmaking[2]. Painless is the key word.

Here is the magnitude. A meta-analysis of 47 studies puts lung cancer mortality in chromate production workers at about 1.41 times that of the general population (standardised mortality ratio 1.41, 95% confidence interval 1.35 to 1.47, the bracket being the range in which the estimate plausibly sits)[1] — a standardised mortality ratio is this group's mortality divided by that of an age-matched general population, 1.0 meaning the same, and some literature multiplies it by 100, so the lung cancer figure of 186 (145 to 228) among 714 workers at the Painesville chromate plant in the US, followed for 24,535 person-years[3], means deaths from lung cancer ran at about 1.86 times the expected number, not that "86% of them get lung cancer"; what it multiplies is the baseline risk each person already carries. The absolute risk for hexavalent chromium workers in Taiwan I could not find, and will not invent for you. Taiwan's permissible exposure concentration for hexavalent chromium is 0.05 mg/m³, and the guideline also records that metallic chromium and trivalent chromium have not been classified as human carcinogens[2]. Welding counts too: IARC Monograph 118, in 2018, moved welding fume from Group 2B up to Group 1 (lung cancer)[4].

Nickel metal and nickel compounds sit a grade apart, and the earring question is not about cancer

In the IARC classification list, nickel metal and alloys (CAS 7440-02-0) are Group 2B, the limited-evidence grade[5]; what sits in Group 1 is nickel compounds — the OSHA guideline records that IARC in 2009 placed water-soluble and water-insoluble nickel compounds in Group 1, with lung cancer, sinonasal cancer and nasal cancer as the targets, a minimum exposure of 6 months, and minimum induction periods of 15 years for lung and nasal cancer and 10 years or more for sinonasal cancer[6].

People often ask me whether wearing nickel-containing earrings causes cancer. The problem with nickel in jewellery is contact dermatitis and allergy, not cancer: a meta-analysis puts the odds ratio for nickel allergy with ear or body piercing in the general population at 5.9 (95% confidence interval 3.6 to 9.4, 5,333 people) — that compares two ratios of allergic against not allergic, not a probability multiplied by 5.9[7].

"Cadmium causes prostate cancer" no longer stands up

I memorised that sentence as a student, and it was the answer in my exam notes for years. Monograph 100C of IARC concludes that cadmium and cadmium compounds are Group 1, with sufficient evidence for lung cancer only; kidney cancer and prostate cancer are no more than "a positive association has been observed", and the wording used for prostate cancer is "suggestive but inconsistent"[8].

A 2016 meta-analysis of 12 cohort and 9 case-control studies concludes that the available evidence does not support an increased risk of prostate cancer from cadmium exposure: relative risk in the general population 1.05 (0.91 to 1.22), with the standardised mortality ratios and odds ratios in occupational populations also crossing the line of no effect[9]. An updated meta-analysis in 2024 gives 1.11 (0.85 to 1.45) overall, which likewise did not reach statistical significance[10].

The way I put it in clinic: it was once suspected, the overall evidence does not currently support it, and a signal survives only in some subgroups. The gap lies in OSHA's Reference Guideline for the Recognition of Poisoning Caused by Occupational Exposure to Cadmium and its Compounds, which says cadmium "may" be related to kidney cancer, pancreatic cancer and prostate cancer, citing studies from the 1970s and 1980s[11]; recognition still goes by the guideline.

The lung cancer line is measurable even at very low environmental doses: across three prospective populations totalling 20,459 people, each doubling of urinary cadmium gave a relative risk for total cancer of 1.22 (1.13 to 1.31) and for lung cancer 1.68 (1.47 to 1.92), in populations whose geometric mean urinary cadmium was only 0.25 to 0.93 μg/g creatinine[12]. The two "times" here are not the same thing: what doubles is the exposure marker, and 1.68 is the ratio of two groups' incidence rates, not anyone's probability becoming 1.68 times itself; there is no citable estimate of the absolute risk from cadmium, and that is the most honest thing I can give you. For ordinary people cadmium comes mainly from tobacco: each cigarette contains about 1.7 μg of cadmium, of which about 10% is inhaled while smoking[8] — the effect of smoking on lung cancer is far larger than that of cadmium, and I will not package quitting as "cadmium protection".

Blackfoot disease was Taiwan's price, and became the world's evidence

Along Taiwan's south-western coast from the 1910s to the late 1970s, residents drank untreated artesian well water with arsenic concentrations mostly above 100 μg/L[13], and the consequence was blackfoot disease (烏腳病); the Blackfoot Disease Medical Memorial Hall in Beimen, Tainan, and Dr Wang Jin-ho's Jinho Clinic are still standing today[14]. I wrote this section slowly: it was decades of loss for an entire community and should not be treated as a curiosity; and because the records were complete enough, it became the world's most important source of evidence on the carcinogenicity of inorganic arsenic.

A 1985 paper in Cancer Research compared cancer mortality in the endemic area against the national population: bladder cancer 1,100 in men and 2,009 in women, that is eleven-fold and twenty-fold, skin cancer 534/652, lung cancer 320/413[15]. A 1989 paper in the American Journal of Epidemiology matched well-water arsenic concentration in 42 villages against mortality from 1973 to 1986: bladder, kidney, skin and lung cancer showed a dose-response, while nasopharyngeal, oesophageal, stomach, colon and cervical cancer and leukaemia showed no association[16] — "arsenic causes leukaemia" does not hold in this dataset.

The Yilan group had a private well per household. A 2001 cohort study of 8,102 people gave a standardised incidence ratio for urinary tract cancer of 2.05 (1.22 to 3.24); with well-water arsenic of 10 μg/L or below as the reference, the relative risk of transitional cell carcinoma at 10.1 to 50, 50.1 to 100 and above 100 μg/L was 1.9, 8.2 and 15.3[17]. I want you to read two numbers together: 15.3 is alarming, but the overall incidence ratio in the same cohort was only 2.05 — few people fell in that highest exposure band to begin with. The skin leaves a mark first: residents who already had arsenic-related skin cancer went on to develop lung cancer at 4.64 times the rate of those without such changes (hazard ratio 4.64, 2.92 to 7.38) — a hazard ratio is a ratio of rates, not "probability becoming 4.64 times"[18]. Anyone with a history of high-arsenic drinking water, I ask to have the keratoses on palms and soles looked at by a dermatologist.

Once the mains water arrived, those cancers really did fall

A 2024 paper in Environment International analysed six arsenic-related cancers using Taiwan Cancer Registry data from 1995 to 2019: incidence in the endemic area stayed higher than elsewhere across the whole period, but the gap began to narrow from the 1960 birth cohort onwards, a timing that matches the installation of mains water; the excess in four of the cancers fell to almost zero in cohorts born after the mains supply, while hepatocellular carcinoma and skin cancer remained raised[19]. Stop the exposure and the risk comes down — that is the premise of this whole topic.

Then magnitude, because I do not want anyone finishing this worried about their own tap. Estimated from Taiwanese high-arsenic-area data, drinking 1 litre a day of water containing 50 μg/L of arsenic can carry a lifetime risk of death from liver, lung, kidney or bladder cancer as high as 13 per 1,000 people[20]. Taiwan's current Drinking Water Quality Standards set the maximum limit for arsenic at 0.01 mg/L, that is 10 μg/L[21]; the endemic area at the time was above 100 μg/L, and it was drunk for decades. Households on mains water I will not send for testing; those with a private well they actually drink from are the ones who should go.

Workplace arsenic is not well-water arsenic, and a urine figure is not something to order casually

The arsine used in the semiconductor industry is a different exposure again: a potent haemolytic agent, with acute poisoning presenting as haemolysis and a biological half-life for urinary metabolites of about 15 days[22]. Item 1.2.15 of the Schedule of Occupational Diseases under the Labor Occupational Accident Insurance lists diseases caused by arsine, and items 7.5 to 7.8 in the seventh category, cancer, list the cancer corresponding to each of these four metals[23].

All four operations fall inside the scope of the special health examination, with different biological monitoring thresholds[24]: arsenic (number 19) tests urinary inorganic arsenic and metabolites, threshold total inorganic arsenic 35 μg/L; chromate (number 24) tests post-shift urinary chromium, 30 μg/g creatinine; cadmium (number 25) tests urinary cadmium, 5 μg/g creatinine; nickel (number 26) tests urinary nickel, 30 μg/g creatinine. Those figures, together with the permissible concentrations and the drinking water limit above, are all triggers for graded management and not safety values — staying under one only means the procedure need not start this time, not that the exposure leaves no trace. The process from Grade 3 and Grade 4 onwards is written out in "Washing your hands in solvent".

One more thing I always say before sampling: total urinary arsenic is pushed up substantially by the non-toxic organic arsenic in seafood. OSHA's Reference Guideline for the Recognition of Occupational Arsenic Poisoning requires that no seafood or other arsenic-rich food has been eaten within the 48 hours before sampling; for the 72 hours that circulates online I could find no basis[25].

Who is keeping the record? Settle that first

Those changes in the nose and the skin described above should not be put down to dry weather or a small cut: go to occupational medicine and explain what your work involves[2]. Nor will I tell you to pay for your own urinary arsenic, cadmium, chromium or nickel: interpretation needs workplace environmental measurement alongside it, a single figure on its own means nothing, and it can be thrown off by last night's plate of seafood[24]; if it is to be tested, go through the statutory health examination[25]. For weekend welding and DIY, open the doors and windows and fit extraction before you reach for a mask; engineering control comes before protective equipment, and "The price of cutting one stone benchtop" says why.

For anyone still in these operations, the right move is to complete the annual special health examination and be followed up by occupational medicine, not to resign first — the moment you leave, environmental monitoring, health examinations and the employer's responsibility are all cut off with you. For anyone already diagnosed with one of these cancers who has the work history, the occupational disease recognition route exists; what exposure evidence to prepare and which clinic to attend sit in the same place in "Leave that asbestos roof alone"; recognition is decided case by case by the competent authority, and that article gives you no guarantee either.

What these four metals have in common is that the harm is tied to concentration and to years, and that under Taiwanese law somebody is responsible for measuring those two things for you and keeping the record. Most exposed people will not get cancer from this; what I want you to confirm is that those records genuinely exist.

From the physician

Well water in the blackfoot disease endemic area ran above 100 μg/L of arsenic, and was drunk for decades; today's mains water standard is 10 μg/L. That gap is what I most want you to see in this piece. Households with a private well they drink from should send it for testing; households on mains water should put the effort into the workplace instead.
