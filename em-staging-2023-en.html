DIAGNOSIS AND STAGING

The staging changed; your treatment does not change with it

The number of sub-stages went from 9 to 21, and between two and three in ten women move stage; but the randomised trials that decide radiotherapy and chemotherapy enrolled patients under the old staging.

Endometrial Cancer Guide · Diagnosis and staging · 17 references
#Staging#Evidence#Molecular Testing#Decisions

A stage that jumps up one box does not mean the tumour has grown; it means the language used to describe it has been replaced. This article covers which three variables the new staging writes into the stage, why two British societies decided to carry on using the 2009 version, and a study from Taiwan in which specialists working out the new stage by hand were right only a little over 60% of the time.

The sentence that causes the most panic in my clinic is this one: "Doctor, I was stage I — how did I become stage II on a different report?" Most of the time the answer is very dull: the tumour has not changed, the language used to describe tumours was changed in 2023. I would like you to hold on to one line first: a change to the staging of endometrial cancer does not automatically change your treatment.

Why it was changed: outcomes inside one "stage I" differ enormously

The new staging was not designed to make life complicated. A validation study that re-ran 129,146 patients from the American National Cancer Database showed that the old FIGO 2009 system put people with widely different outlooks in the same box: 5-year overall survival for FIGO 2009 stage IIIA was 63.9%, and once it is split under the 2023 system it becomes 88.0% for IA3, 62.4% for IIIA1 and 55.7% for IIIA2[1]. A single-institution cohort of 2,067 saw the same direction, and more extreme: the women reassigned to the new IA3 had 5-year overall survival of 100%, and under the 2009 system they were stage IIIA, a box with 70.3% survival[2].

It holds the other way too: in the same cohort, women labelled IICm_p53abn had 5-year overall survival of 73%, and under the 2009 system most of them were simply stage II, a box with 86.8%[2]. The old staging mixed together people with very different outlooks; that is the problem the revision set out to deal with.

What actually changed

The most visible thing is scale: the number of sub-stages went from 9 to 21, which is the figure FIGO gives in its own 2025 report[3]. The reason there are more of them is that three new variables have been written into the stage.

The first is histological type. The 2023 version sorts histology into "non-aggressive" (grade 1 to grade 2 endometrioid carcinoma) and "aggressive" (grade 3 endometrioid, serous, clear cell, undifferentiated, mixed, mesonephric-like and gastrointestinal-type mucinous carcinoma, and carcinosarcoma); with the same depth of myometrial invasion, the two land in different boxes[4]. The second is lymphovascular space invasion: a non-aggressive histological type with invasion reaching "substantial" extent is assigned to IIB regardless of how far the local invasion goes[4]. The document spells out the consequence itself: "The number of women with Stage II tumors will markedly increase"[4].

The third is molecular classification. Where the molecular subtype is known, a lower-case m and a subscript are added after the stage: POLE-mutated disease confined to the uterine corpus or extending only to the cervix is brought down to IAm_POLEmut regardless of lymphovascular space invasion and histological type, and p53-abnormal disease confined to the corpus with any myometrial invasion is moved up to IICm_p53abn[4]. It is worth stressing that the wording in the original for this part is "encouraged" and "if available and feasible" — encouraged, not required[4]. The molecular terms are explained in "Two kinds of endometrial cancer, now four molecular groups".

One thing did not change: positive peritoneal cytology is still not included in the stage, and FIGO 2023 puts it together with intraluminal tubal floating tumour fragments and calls the issue a controversial one[4]. At the same time, a cohort of 720 saw positive peritoneal cytology associated with poorer outcomes on multivariable analysis, and the authors' conclusion states it directly: leaving peritoneal cytology out of prognostic assessment "may risk undertreatment"[5]. Not entering the stage does not mean it carries no information.

Substantial lymphovascular space invasion: a variable pathologists do not yet read the same way now decides the stage

This is the part I think is most worth opening up. The definition FIGO 2023 adopts is invasion of 5 or more vessels; FIGO's own 2025 report sets out the thresholds other documents use alongside it: NCCN uses 4 or more, and the 2022 ICCR and the 2019 ISGyP use 3 or more[3]. The same tumour, the same slide, and a different document can mean a different stage.

The reading itself is not solid ground either. Data quoted in one review give an agreement coefficient of 0.6 for pathologists deciding whether lymphovascular space invasion is present at all, and 0.56 for deciding whether it is substantial[6] (these are figures quoted by that review). FIGO's 2025 report lists this among the drawbacks of the new staging, saying in its own words that the new system incorporates variables whose definitions are still evolving and which show considerable interobserver variability, and giving lymphovascular space invasion as its example[3].

Your stage may move, but the tumour has not

Re-running old cohorts under the new staging gives very different proportions of stage movement from one series to another: 19.9% among 4,566 patients across 18 Spanish centres[7], 26.0% up and 4.8% down in that New York cohort of 2,067[2], and as high as 56.2% in a Shanghai cohort of 331[8]. The direction is mostly upward, but "upward" means landing in a higher-numbered box after switching to a different way of describing things; it does not mean the tumour has grown.

The other side of honesty belongs here as well. A Korean cohort of 472 patients enrolled as FIGO 2009 stage I to II found that among non-aggressive histological types the 2023 version stratified better; but in the 84 patients with aggressive histology, the 2023 version separated neither overall survival nor recurrence-free survival, while the 2009 version still had stratifying power for recurrence-free survival[9]. A study of 291 patients from two Turkish centres found that once the pathological factors making up the stage were adjusted for, stage movement itself no longer independently predicted survival[10]. Even in that best-looking New York dataset, the authors write in the paper that small patient numbers in each sub-stage and concerns about over-fitting limit the analysis[2].

The trials that decide treatment enrolled patients under the old staging

This is the most important sentence in the article. PORTEC-1 recruited between 1990 and 1997, and its enrolment definition was "grade 1 with deep (50% or more) myometrial invasion, grade 2 with any invasion, or grade 3 with superficial (less than 50%) invasion" — that is the language of the FIGO 1988 era[11]. GOG-99 enrolled "FIGO stage IB, IC and II (occult)", and the sub-stages IB and IC exist only in the 1988 version[12]. The most recent of them, PORTEC-3, recruited between 2006 and 2013, and the protocol reads "FIGO 2009 stage I"[13].

Put another way: your stage may be written in the 2023 version, but the randomised trials that decide "radiotherapy or not, chemotherapy added or not" enrolled patients described in the 2009 or the 1988 version. Whether any adjuvant treatment trial has randomised patients enrolled under the 2023 version, I did not find, so all I can say is: none of the major trials I found were. FIGO 2023's own wording is still guarded — for strategies that de-escalate or intensify post-operative treatment according to molecular profile, the original says they "are being defined"[4]. So a changed stage number does not trigger a treatment change by itself; that decision goes back to the gynaecological oncology and radiation oncology clinics, and it is dealt with in "After the operation, do you still need radiotherapy".

Not everyone has moved across, either

The 2025 update from the three European societies (ESGO/ESTRO/ESP) writes FIGO 2023 into its guideline explicitly[14]. On the other side, FIGO's own 2025 report is quite clear: in the UK, the British Gynecological Cancer Society (BGCS) and the British Association of Gynecological Pathologists (BAGP) have recommended that the 2009 staging system should continue to be used[3]. That is not "some people grumbled but everyone went along with it"; it is a national society keeping the old version in writing.

The academic objections are out in the open too. A 2024 commentary by ten gynaecological pathologists is titled "FIGO 2023 endometrial cancer staging: too much, too soon?", and argues that the impact and evidence of the new system should first be assessed at a multi-institutional, global level by all the relevant societies before pathologists and clinicians adopt it[15]. FIGO's side has replied, in these terms: incorporating pathological and "not mandatory" molecular features into the new staging "has generated some controversy", and several validations published recently have demonstrated the higher prognostic precision of FIGO 2023 compared with FIGO 2009[16]. Both sides are worth hearing.

FIGO's 2025 report lists two further drawbacks of its own: in places without immunohistochemistry and molecular testing, this staging effectively cannot be applied; and the same person's stage may change at different points in time, which confuses patients and doctors alike[3]. The same document also gives the new staging a paragraph of credit: it marks out a group with an extremely good outlook, and so creates the chance to avoid overtreatment and unnecessary toxicity[3]. How far that evidence has actually got, and what its limits are, is a whole article — "How molecular classification changes your treatment" — and I am not drawing a conclusion here.

Do not convert it yourself — look at which version the report says

There is a study from Taipei Veterans General Hospital that measured the error rate of conversion directly: determining the FIGO 2023 stage by the traditional method of looking it up in a table was correct only 58.1% of the time; obstetrician-gynaecologists using the same method managed 62.6%; in molecular classification scenarios it fell to 49.0%, and in IA3 scenarios it was 58.3%[17]. So please do not take a conversion table off the internet and re-stage your own report — specialists doing this by hand get it wrong 37.4% to 41.9% of the time.

Which version the Taiwan Cancer Registry and individual hospitals are using now, I could not find in any official or professional-society announcement, so I am not writing it. In practice what you need to do is simple: turn to the line on the pathology report that gives the stage and look at whether FIGO 2009 or FIGO 2023 is marked beside it. If nothing is marked, say the question out loud.

There are two more sentences worth taking to your next appointment. One is for the report: "Does my report say substantial lymphovascular space invasion?" That is the field that moves most under the new staging. The other is for the decision: "If you switched to the other version, which box would I be in, and would the treatment recommendation be different?" That one matters most, and what the answer is in your case can only be worked out by the gynaecological oncologists and radiation oncologists holding the complete report. For a lot of people the recommendation will not move when the version changes; but for those whose report says substantial lymphovascular space invasion, this box really can change the recommendation. So say the question out loud rather than assuming the answer yourself.

From the physician

The line on the report that gives the stage usually says beside it whether it is FIGO 2009 or 2023; if it does not, ask. When the number goes up, do not rush to look up a new survival figure — ask this instead: "If you used the other version, which box would I be in, and would the treatment recommendation be different?"
