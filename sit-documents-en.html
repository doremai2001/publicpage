IS LETTING GO EASIER

Three documents, three different things

The dividing line is artificial nutrition and hydration: the declaration does not cover it, an advance decision lets you try it and stop.

Situation Guides · Is Letting Go Easier · 10 references
#Law & rights#Palliative care#End of life#Decisions#Family

First, the misunderstanding that stops people signing: none of these means "do not take him to hospital". The Patient Right to Autonomy Act requires appropriate emergency treatment for a patient in critical condition. Activation takes two specialist physicians, and an advance decision needs two palliative team consultations as well. I copy those checkpoints out one by one.

"The hospice form" — I have heard that phrase in clinic more times than I can count, and it means something different every time. Taiwan has two statutes and three separate things that get talked about as one: a hospice palliative care declaration, an advance decision, and the thing everybody calls DNR. Run them together and a family finds out at the bedside that the one they signed does not reach the situation in front of them. A word to readers who know this subject in English: these are instruments of Taiwanese statute, not the same scheme as a living will, an advance directive, a DNR order, a health care proxy or a POLST form, and those names carry defaults Taiwan's law does not. I use the Taiwanese names.

The hospice palliative care declaration: it reaches resuscitation and life-sustaining treatment

Its statute is the Hospice Palliative Care Act (安寧緩和醫療條例), current text the amendment of 109 年 12 月 29 日, promulgated 110 年 1 月 20 日[1]. It applies to a terminally ill patient, as the Act defines it — Article 3: a person "suffering from a severe injury or illness which a physician has diagnosed as incurable, where there is medical evidence that the course of the disease will progress to death in the near future, which is unavoidable".

The current official reference form is the one the Ministry of Health and Welfare (衛福部) announced as amended on 111 年 12 月 7 日 — the advance declaration for hospice palliative care and life-sustaining treatment choices (預立安寧緩和醫療暨維生醫療抉擇意願書). Its body has three separate tick-boxes: hospice palliative care, withholding cardiopulmonary resuscitation (不施行心肺復甦術), and withholding life-sustaining treatment[2]. It does not cover artificial nutrition and hydration — the tube feed and the drip. That is the most important difference between it and an advance decision.

Who may sign: the current text of Article 5 says "an adult with legal capacity". The statute prints no age figure, so I print none either[1]. No consultation is required, but Article 4 requires two or more witnesses with full legal capacity present, and staff of the institution delivering that care may not witness. Article 6-1 governs registration on the National Health Insurance card: the declaration must be scanned into the central competent authority's database before registration, and the registration has the same legal effect as the original[1].

The advance decision: the thing it also reaches is artificial nutrition and hydration

Its statute is the Patient Right to Autonomy Act (病人自主權利法), in force since 108 年 1 月 6 日; its current text is likewise the 109 年 12 月 29 日 amendment, promulgated 110 年 1 月 20 日[3][4]. Article 8: "a person with full legal capacity may make an advance decision (預立醫療決定, AD), and may withdraw or vary it in writing at any time". Article 2 of the enforcement rules adds one more requirement — being enrolled in National Health Insurance and holding an NHI card[5].

Its reach is wider and deeper. Five clinical conditions apply: terminal illness, irreversible coma, a permanent vegetative state, severe dementia, and other states announced by the central competent authority. Under each, the official form lets you choose separately, for life-sustaining treatment as the Act defines it and for artificial nutrition and hydration, one of four options: refuse it; try it for a defined period and then stop; leave it to the health care agent (醫療委任代理人); accept it[6].

The price is a stricter procedure. All three requirements in Article 9, paragraph 1 must be met: an advance care planning consultation (預立醫療照護諮商, ACP) provided by a medical institution, with its seal on the decision; notarisation, or two or more witnesses with full legal capacity present; and registration on the NHI card. Paragraph 2 requires the declarant, at least one relative within the second degree of kinship, and the health care agent if one is named, to attend the consultation; such a relative who has died, is missing or has special reasons need not[3]. The consultation team is specified: one specialist physician, one nurse, one psychologist or social worker[7].

The witness restrictions are what practice most often gets wrong: the health care agent, the responsible care team, and anyone who stands to benefit from the declarant's death cannot witness (heirs excepted); and Article 7 of the management regulations adds that for an inpatient, the attending physician and the nurses responsible for their care cannot witness[3][7].

DNR is not a standalone legal document

"DNR" is not a term in any Taiwanese statute. What the law writes is withholding cardiopulmonary resuscitation (不施行心肺復甦術). What people call DNR is, in law, one of the two documents in Article 7 of the Hospice Palliative Care Act: the declaration the patient signed in advance, or, where the patient is already unconscious or unable to express a clear wish and has never signed one, a consent form signed by the nearest relatives (同意書)[1]. I did not obtain that consent form's official reference version, so I do not describe its fields.

Article 7 has another provision that is widely misread: where the requirements are met, cardiopulmonary resuscitation or life-sustaining treatment already given may be terminated or withdrawn[1]. "Once he is intubated there is no way back" does not hold as a matter of statute. How far the relatives' order reaches is in "When he cannot decide, who decides".

The three side by side, in one look

  • Hospice palliative care declaration | Hospice Palliative Care Act | no consultation, two or more witnesses | does not cover artificial nutrition and hydration[1]

  • Advance decision | Patient Right to Autonomy Act | consultation and institutional seal mandatory, plus notarisation or two or more witnesses | covers artificial nutrition and hydration, with the option to try it for a period and then stop[3]

  • DNR | not a document in its own right; the declaration or the nearest relatives' consent form under Article 7 | no consultation | what has already been given may be terminated or withdrawn[1]

Nothing takes effect automatically — the checkpoints are here, and that is the good news

What families fear is not the signing. It is whether a signature will be acted on carelessly.

  • For the declaration and the consent form to take effect, two physicians with the relevant specialist qualifications must diagnose the patient as terminally ill (Article 7 of the Act)[1].

  • For an advance decision to be activated, the five clinical conditions must be confirmed by two physicians with the relevant specialist qualifications and then by at least two consultations with the palliative care team (Article 14, paragraph 2)[3].

  • None of these documents means "do not take him to hospital in an emergency." Article 7 of the Patient Right to Autonomy Act requires appropriate emergency treatment for a patient in critical condition, unless Article 14 and the relevant provisions of the Hospice Palliative Care Act apply. Somebody brought to the emergency department after a road accident is not left unresuscitated because he signed one of these[3].

  • A physician who cannot carry out an advance decision, on professional or personal grounds, may decline, but must say so; where treatment is terminated, withdrawn or withheld, palliative care and other appropriate management must be provided, and where they cannot be, a referral suggested and help given (Articles 14 and 16)[3].

All of them can be withdrawn, and changing your mind has a formal procedure

Article 12, paragraph 3 says that where an advance decision registered on the NHI card is inconsistent with a wish the declarant expresses in writing during clinical care, the variation of the advance decision must be completed; paragraph 4 leaves that procedure to be announced by the central competent authority[3]. I did not obtain that announcement, so this is as far as I go: there is a formal variation procedure, and for how things are handled before it is complete, ask your medical team or the consultation institution directly.

What the consultation costs: no national price is set in law

Article 9 of the management regulations is one sentence: "a consultation institution may, with the approval of the municipal or county (city) competent authority, charge a consultation fee." The fee is therefore set by each institution and approved by the local authority; the regulations set no national price. Article 5 requires the institution to disclose that information, fees included, before the consultation[7]. So "ask the price before you go" is not haggling; it is a disclosure the regulations require. I found no official schedule of charges, so I print no figure at all.

Does the NHI pay? Yes, but narrowly, and to the hospital. The indication for the advance care planning consultation fee is limited to inpatients with full legal capacity meeting one of four situations; it may be claimed once per person per lifetime, and not at all where the NHI card already carries the registration[8]. Most people who sign at an outpatient clinic fall outside that scope. All of the above was checked on 2026-08-30; for the version in force and the fees, ask your case manager (個管師), the medical social work office (社工室) or the hospital billing office (醫務課).

Before you go and sign, work out which one you are signing

Work out which one you are signing, then ask: is this an advance care planning consultation institution, and what is the fee? Which of my relatives within the second degree has to be there? Who can witness, and who cannot? Once it is signed, who uploads the registration to the NHI card?

One figure, for scale: as of 112 年 1 月 6 日 there were 234 advance care planning consultation institutions nationwide, and 43,718 people had completed an advance decision and registered it on their NHI card[9] — a figure three years and eight months old, so look the current position up separately. A last thing that confuses cancer patients: the diseases announced under Article 14, paragraph 1, item 5 (eleven categories on 109 年 1 月 6 日, mostly rare or neurodegenerative)[10] do not include cancer; a cancer patient reaching an advance decision normally does so through item 1, terminal illness. How the three hospice services are started is in "Three kinds of hospice care: how they differ, how to start".

From the physician

What goes wrong before a signature is usually not resolve; it is signing the wrong one of the three. Ask five things first: is this an advance care planning consultation institution, what is the fee, which second-degree relative has to attend, who may not witness, and who uploads the registration to the NHI card. The statutes have been amended, so take the institution's own advance disclosure as current.
