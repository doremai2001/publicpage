THE BODY DURING TREATMENT

The tiredness rest doesn't fix

Nine in ten patients get it and barely half mention it — and the treatment with the strongest evidence is movement, not rest.

Patient Education · The Body During Treatment · 15 references
#Side Effects#Quality of Life#Daily Life

Fatigue during treatment is not weak stamina. It has a definition, a 0-to-10 scale and a threshold at which you are supposed to speak up, and most people cross it without saying a word.

"I slept ten hours and I still woke up tired." I hear that most weeks, usually with "but I'm probably just not very strong" after it. This piece is about the "but". Fatigue during treatment is not weak stamina: it has a name, a scale and a guideline, and the treatment with the most consistent evidence runs against instinct — not rest, but movement.

Taiwan's own figures: nine in ten have it, barely half say so

Management of Cancer-Related Fatigue: A Guideline for Taiwan (癌因性疲憊症之臨床治療指引, Taiwan Society of Cancer Palliative Medicine and Taiwan Oncology Nursing Society, 2017) cites a national survey of 1,207 cancer patients at 23 institutions: 92% had fatigue during their illness, a quarter of it moderate to severe; 83.5% tried something themselves, but only 56.2% raised it with a doctor or nurse[1].

The same gap shows internationally: across 57 studies and 34,310 patients, severe fatigue reported by patients themselves was 23.6%; recorded by physicians, 1.6%[2]. Twenty-fold is not indifference: in ten minutes of clinic, fatigue queues behind the scan, the blood count and the next cycle, and neither side raises it.

"Tired" and "cancer-related fatigue" are not the same word

The guideline's definition has two load-bearing parts: it is out of proportion to what you did that day, and rest does not clear it[1]. Which is why a relative's "go and lie down for a while" sounds reasonable and does nothing.

Fatigue of any severity runs at 43.0% (95% confidence interval 39.2–47.2; the interval is where the estimate plausibly sits, narrower meaning steadier). Severe fatigue: 15.9% before treatment, 33.8% during, 24.1% after[2]. A second meta-analysis, 84 studies and 144,813 people, puts pooled prevalence at 52%[3].

The same paper lists what makes fatigue heavier: insomnia odds ratio 2.83, pain 2.64, depression 2.23, concurrent chemoradiotherapy 2.25, performance status 6.58[3]. An odds ratio divides one group's odds of the event happening against not happening by the other's; on common events it exaggerates the real gap in probability, so this is not "the chance more than doubles". Fatigue rarely grows alone — underneath sit poor sleep, pain and low mood, all three treatable.

Put four treatments side by side and the winner is not a drug

One meta-analysis set four commonly recommended approaches in one framework, pooling 113 randomised trials and 11,525 participants: exercise weighted effect size 0.30 (0.25–0.36), psychological and behavioural interventions 0.27, both combined 0.26, drugs 0.09 (0.00–0.19, P=.05, not statistically significant). The authors were blunt: prescribe exercise or psychological interventions first-line[4]. ASCO's survivorship fatigue guideline agrees — screen everyone, assess moderate-to-severe fatigue properly, treat correctable causes first; what has evidence is physical activity and psychological or mind-body work[5].

The size of the effect deserves honesty. Cochrane pooled 56 randomised trials and 4,068 people: standardised mean difference −0.27 (−0.37 to −0.17) — more negative means more effective, and 0.27 is measurable but undramatic; aerobic exercise reached significance, resistance training and other forms did not; breast and prostate cancer showed benefit, haematological malignancies did not[6]. Exercise is the most consistent evidence we have, and it buys less tired, not "back to before".

Taiwan's guideline also puts regular exercise during treatment at its top grade[1]. The same guideline lists an astragalus polysaccharide injection at that grade; what differs is the scale of the evidence. Behind exercise sits that meta-analysis of 113 randomised trials[4]; what the guideline describes for the injection is preliminary clinical trial work, with a pivotal trial of 58 patients[1]. The same grade label does not mean the same quantity of evidence. I could not find a formal document setting out that injection's reimbursement or self-pay status; confirm with your case manager (個管師) or the hospital pharmacy. How far to move, and when to stop, is in "Move, or rest, during treatment".

Why the drug box is still empty

The drug I am asked about most is methylphenidate. The 2010 Cochrane drug review analysed 31 studies and 7,104 people, found it better than placebo (Z=2.83, P=0.005), and said in the same paper that large randomised trials were needed to confirm this[7]. That trial finished in 2024. MePFAC — multicentre, double-blind, placebo-controlled, 162 advanced cancer patients, dose individually titrated — put the fatigue score at six weeks 1.97 points better than placebo (95% CI −0.95 to 4.90, P=.186), not significant; across ten weeks 2.20 points, which the authors state did not cross the 5-point minimally clinically important difference[8]. A 2026 meta-analysis of 9 randomised trials and 668 people gave an effect size of 0.23, but its trial sequential analysis showed the information size still insufficient to confirm efficacy[9]. The evidence remains divided, and the largest, most rigorously designed trial found no difference.

Taiwan adds a layer. In section 1 of the National Health Insurance (健保) drug reimbursement regulations, extended-release methylphenidate is listed for attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder; modafinil for excessive daytime sleepiness in narcolepsy[10]. Cancer-related fatigue is in neither. Whether to use one, and whether it is self-paid, is worked out with your attending physician, not asked for by name.

Steroids have one positive trial: 84 evaluable advanced cancer patients, fatigue improving 9 points on dexamethasone against 3.1 on placebo at two weeks (P=.008)[11]. Its boundaries: fourteen days, advanced disease, three or more moderate-to-severe symptoms at once; Taiwan's guideline confines it to end-stage disease, fatigue with anorexia, or brain or bone metastasis pain[1]. Mid-course, with cure as the aim, you are not in that frame.

Ginseng comes up almost monthly. N07C2, multicentre and double-blind, 364 people: the primary endpoint at four weeks showed no difference (P=.07), significance appearing only at eight weeks (P=.003)[12]. A trial that missed its primary endpoint cannot hold up "ginseng improves fatigue"; and Taiwan's guideline itself lists ginseng preparations interacting with warfarin as something to watch[1]. Supplements get their full treatment in "Which supplements really do collide with your drugs". One sentence belongs here: list everything you take, gifts included, and show it to your doctor.

Some tiredness has a cause, and the cause can be repaired

The guideline puts "find the correctable causes" ahead of everything else[5]. These are the ones clinic really finds.

  • Anaemia — which does not mean "a shot to build blood and it's sorted". Cochrane pooled 91 trials and 20,102 people: erythropoiesis-stimulating agents cut transfusion need (relative risk 0.65), but the hazard ratio for death during the study period was 1.17 (1.06–1.29; a hazard ratio compares how fast events occur in the two groups, 1 meaning the same rate) and thromboembolic events relative risk 1.52 (1.34–1.74)[13]. A decision an oncologist weighs alongside the state of your disease, not a tonic.

  • Hypothyroidism — keep this in mind if your treated volume included the neck. A review of 111 papers put the median incidence after radiotherapy at 36% (range 3–79%), recent estimates around forty to fifty per cent[14]. A blood test finds it; it is still filed under "you're bound to be tired after treatment".

  • Poor sleep, pain, low mood — the odds ratios above[3]. Each has its own management, and clearing one often loosens the fatigue too.

Which kind of tiredness cannot wait for the next appointment

Fatigue during treatment builds slowly, in waves. The other kind has to be separated out: suddenly much more tired over a short period, breathless after a few steps, fever or shaking chills, swelling and pain in one calf. Do not file these under "it's just the fatigue" — fever is in "The fever that cannot wait until morning", breathlessness and a swollen calf in "A swollen calf: can it wait until tomorrow?", both ending the same way: same day, not overnight at home.

When you cannot go on, the button to press is not pause

What I least want to see: a week is too much, so the patient alone decides to skip a session, or rings to push the course back. The total length of a course is part of whether it works, and interrupting or stretching it carries a price you cannot feel at the time.

The right move is plain. On the day you cannot go on, ring the team or your case manager (個管師) and let the doctor decide: adjust the dose, delay a cycle, or treat another cause first. "Let's skip this one" lands differently when a doctor has assessed it.

How to actually say it out loud

The guideline's threshold is easy to remember: score your fatigue 0 to 10 — 1–3 mild, 4–6 moderate, 7 up severe — and 4 or more should be said[1]. A Chinese-language tool exists: the Taiwanese version of the Brief Fatigue Inventory, validated in 439 Taiwanese patients[15]. Do not say "not too bad". Give the number, then what it stops you doing — showering, the stairs, picking up a grandchild.

Three questions for your next appointment: does this fatigue warrant blood tests for anaemia or thyroid? At my stage, how far can I take exercise? If a week becomes impossible, which number do I ring, and by when? Put them in your phone first — that is what makes those ten minutes usable.

From the physician

How tired you are, and why, differs from person to person — anaemia, thyroid function, sleep, pain and mood all have to be checked before anyone can say what yours is. Score it out of 10 and bring the number to your next appointment; from 4 upwards it belongs in the conversation. And on a week you cannot go on, ring the team rather than skipping a session yourself.
