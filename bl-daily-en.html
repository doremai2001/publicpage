TAIWAN, AND AFTERWARDS

A stoma, a neobladder, and getting back to your days

The days after discharge: the ring of skin around a stoma, a neobladder at 84% by day and six in ten at night, the denominator under that return-to-work group, and the one Taiwanese assessment provision I could find against the subsidy I could not.

Bladder Cancer Guide · Taiwan, and afterwards · 7 references
#Urostomy#Quality of Life#Work & Travel#Rehabilitation

With a neobladder, about 84% stay dry through the day and only six in ten through the night; with a stoma, what goes wrong most is not the stoma but the ring of skin around it. The days after discharge are largely those two things, plus a whole row of small matters nobody has studied — and I have listed those empty cells for you as well.

Once the bladder is gone, the urine has to come out somewhere else. Why this operation is the standard treatment, what each of the three urinary diversions costs and gives, and how to talk your way to a decision with your doctor are all in "Cystectomy and urinary diversion"; this article does not rewrite any of that and does not do the comparing for you. This one picks up the day after discharge: you now have a stoma or a new bladder, and these are the days as they actually go.

Two kinds of passage follow, and it is worth saying so. Where there is a denominator I write the numbers, with the study design and the number of people attached. Where I could not find anything, I write that I could not find anything, rather than inventing something from impression. Several of the things most often asked after discharge fall into that second kind.

First, a set of signals: these mean going back the same day

The guideline lists urinary tract infection, pyelonephritis, and leakage or stricture at the join between ureter and ileum among the early complications of this operation within thirty days[1]. At home, this is what those look like when you see them. Fever or chills; pain in one flank or one side of the back; urine in the bag suddenly dropping off, with almost nothing all day; urine leaking from the abdominal wound or from around the stoma. Any one of those means contacting the treating team or coming back the same day, not waiting for the date in the diary. When you telephone, start with the date of the operation and which diversion was done, so that the person on the other end can pick the thread up. Anyone with a neobladder has one more to remember: passing no urine at all through the day, with the lower abdomen distending, is also a same-day matter.

Living with a stoma: what goes wrong most is the ring of skin around it

A systematic review of 14 studies — mostly retrospective single-centre work, running up to 2008 — reports an incidence range of 15% to 65%[2]. The problems come in two families. One grows on the stoma and the abdominal wall: parastomal hernia, prolapse, stenosis, retraction. The other grows on the surrounding skin: irritant contact dermatitis, pseudoverrucous lesions and alkaline crystal deposits are the chemical group; pressure ulceration, injury from pulling adhesive off and mucocutaneous separation are the mechanical group; and then there are infections such as candidiasis and folliculitis, and allergic contact dermatitis[2].

That width from 15% to 65% tells you something in itself: definitions and follow-up differ enormously between centres. I am copying the authors' own concluding line out: these peristomal complications are generally underestimated and under-reported[2]. So red skin, itching, broken skin, a baseplate that will not stay stuck — none of that is clumsiness on your part. It is the commonest problem in this box, and there are things that can be done about it.

Changing the bag, bathing, swimming, going away: this is a cell I could not fill

How many days between bag changes, how many weeks before it stops being awkward, whether you can soak in a bath or get in the water, how many spares to pack for a long trip, whether to connect an overnight drainage bag — these are the most practical questions there are, and I have been through the full text of both guidelines and the ostomy care literature without finding a single quantitative study or provision I could cite. I am not making numbers up for you, because you would hold your own life up against them and conclude you were behind.

What can be done is to change who the question goes to. There are people whose job this is: the stoma care nurse (wound, ostomy and continence nurse), and on the hospital side usually a case manager who can pick it up. Which hospitals in Taiwan have that post, and how the educational materials are obtained, I likewise could not find citable information on — so settle before discharge who takes this over.

A neobladder: the day and the night are two different things

A meta-analysis of 59 papers, whose authors state plainly that heterogeneity was high, gives this: at twelve months after surgery, daytime continence 84.2% (95% confidence interval 78.7% to 89.1%; a confidence interval means that if you ran the same study many times over, the answer would land in that range) and night-time continence 61.7% (51.9% to 71.1%), with the authors concluding that "the high rate of nocturnal incontinence remains unresolved"[3]. The night cell is a problem the whole field has yet to solve. It is not a sign that you have not done well enough. Which means the night has to be designed for as the normal state of affairs, not corrected as a failure.

A neobladder is not a swap for the bladder you had — emptying it takes abdominal pressure working together with a relaxing sphincter, and that is learned from scratch[1]. How completely it empties varies a great deal between centres: the residual volume range in that same analysis is 4.9 to 101.6 millilitres[3]. Some proportion of people have to learn clean intermittent self-catheterisation, but a rate with a denominator on it is something I could find neither in the full guideline text nor in that analysis, so I am not giving a percentage — ask your urologist. Kidney function, metabolic changes and the vitamins that need following for years are not in this article; they are in the one pointed to at the top.

Going back to work, and the half that is still there a year on

A German prospective study: of 842 people who underwent three weeks of inpatient rehabilitation after surgery between April 2018 and December 2019, 230 (27.3%) had been in work before the operation. Of those 230, 86.8% were back at work at six months and 80.6% at one year; quality of life went on improving, but at one year 51.0% still had high psychological distress. In the linear regression, returning to work was a predictor of better quality of life a year on; the same paper also sets the unadjusted scores side by side — a global health score of 69.8 in those who were back at work against 55.1 in those who were not (p=0.004; that is the EORTC QLQ-C30 scale, 0 to 100, with higher being better)[4].

The denominator has to be said out loud: that is Germany, in people who had three weeks of inpatient rehabilitation after surgery, and who had jobs before the operation. It cannot be read as "eight in ten people who have a bladder removed go back to work". A sense of the timescale can be borrowed, though: for most people quality of life drops after surgery and comes back to the pre-operative level at around twelve months[1]. The psychological box has a figure of its own: among patients with muscle-invasive bladder cancer and no psychiatric history, 31% were newly diagnosed with a mental health disorder after the cancer diagnosis[1]. For mood and sleep, see "The kind of sleeplessness that won't lift".

The Taiwan cell: one thing found, several not

The one I found is specific. In Appendix 2-A, category six, of the Regulations for Disability Assessment, s610 structure of the urinary system, grade 2, reads "a permanent artificial bladder, passing urine through the abdominal wall for life"; and b620 urination function, grade 2, first sub-paragraph, reads "a bladder fistula, passing urine through the abdominal wall for life". The grade key to that appendix states that "disability grade 2 means moderate"[5]. But the overall grade still has to be computed together with activity, participation and environmental factors, and that is done by the assessing institution — and assessment is assessment, subsidy is subsidy. They are two different things.

The ones I could not find, I write as zero hits. In the appendix to Article 4 of the Regulations on Subsidies for Assistive Device Costs for Persons with Disabilities, I searched the full text: 「造口」 (stoma), 「尿袋」 (urine bag), 「排泄」 (excretion), 「失禁」 (incontinence) and 「導尿」 (catheterisation) returned 0 hits each[6]; in the appendix to Article 5 of the Regulations on Subsidies for Medical Rehabilitation Costs and Medical Assistive Devices for Persons with Disabilities, the same five search terms were also 0 hits throughout[7]. Zero hits means "no listed item can be found in these two central schedules". It does not mean "there is no subsidy", and it does not mean "there is one" — I am not inferring either.

What can be quoted is the text of the regulation itself. Article 3 of the assistive device regulation fixes the proportions, in translation of the clause: "1. Low-income households: the full amount of the maximum subsidy. 2. Lower-middle-income households: 75 per cent of the maximum subsidy. 3. General households other than the preceding two categories: 50 per cent of the maximum subsidy." Article 7, paragraph 3, reads "as a principle, assistive device subsidies are limited to four items per person per two-year period; the same item may not be subsidised again within its service life". Article 7, paragraph 2, reads "the competent authority of a special municipality or county (city) may, taking its financial position into account, add subsidised items or raise the maximum subsidy amount" — so individual counties and cities may have their own. And Article 16 reads "an applicant who purchases an assistive device before the competent authority of the special municipality or county (city) has approved it under Article 13 shall not be subsidised"[6] — buy it first and apply afterwards and there is no subsidy; do not get the order the wrong way round. For NHI special-materials payment for ostomy supplies I found no listed item I could quote — ask the medical affairs office, your case manager or the social worker, and the social affairs bureau and assistive-device centre of the district where you are registered.

The cells this article leaves empty, listed here

The empty cells go together at the end, because they are the first things needed in the month after discharge and the citable research happens to stop just short of them. Cell one: how many days between bag changes, how many weeks before it comes easily, whether you can get in the water, how much to take when travelling — no citable research; ask the stoma care nurse. Cell two: what proportion of people have to learn clean intermittent self-catheterisation — no rate with a denominator in the full guideline text or in that 59-paper analysis; ask your urologist.

Cell three: subsidy for ostomy supplies — zero hits in the two central schedules, and no listed item found for NHI special materials; ask your case manager, the social worker and the assistive-device centre where you are registered. Cell four: which hospitals have a stoma care nurse, and how the educational materials are obtained — I found no citable information; before discharge, ask your ward nurse who takes this over.

Four empty cells do not mean these four things are unimportant. Quite the opposite. What it means is that these answers are not in the papers, they are in people. Writing down "who do I go to for these four" before you leave is worth more than memorising any percentage.

From the physician

Settle two things on the day you are discharged: the date of the next appointment, and which number to ring when the stoma or the neobladder goes wrong. Fever, pain in one flank, and a bag with almost no urine in it all day are the three that mean going back the same day. And one that catches a lot of people out: buy ostomy supplies first and apply for the subsidy afterwards and you will not get it — ask the social worker or the assistive-device centre before you buy.
