DIAGNOSIS AND STAGING

Why there is suddenly so much thyroid cancer

Overdiagnosis is a proportion you can only compute by looking back across a whole population, and it cannot tell you which kind the nodule in your neck is.

Thyroid Cancer Guide · Diagnosis and staging · 10 references
#Screening#Evidence#Prevention#Decisions

Taiwan's reported thyroid cancer cases rose roughly 4.9-fold over twenty-four years. But overdiagnosis is not misdiagnosis, and it does not mean "it isn't really cancer"; and the line "more of it, no more deaths" is a stronger claim than the evidence.

People ask me in clinic whether thyroid cancer has become more common. It has, a great deal more. The second question is usually whether that means many people are treated who did not need to be. That one is far harder, and getting it wrong costs something in both directions.

This article is about why the number of diagnoses exploded, and about a concept called overdiagnosis. Everything here is a statistic about a whole country and a whole generation, and it can only be calculated looking backwards. It cannot tell you which kind the nodule in your neck is. How your nodule gets sorted is in 〈A scan found a nodule. Now what?〉 and 〈Why the stage depends on your age〉.

Taiwan's numbers first, laid flat

In the national cancer registry, reported new cases of thyroid malignancy stood at 1,228 in 1999 and 6,045 in 2023 (1,507 men, 4,538 women) — roughly 4.9 times over twenty-four years. Some points in between: 2,433 in 2009, 3,737 in 2015, 4,974 in 2020[1]. In the same year it ranked 11th by incidence among men and 4th among women.

The other side of the same report: 225 people died of thyroid malignancy in 2023 (81 men, 144 women), 0.42% of all cancer deaths, ranking 23rd by mortality among men and 23rd among women as well[1].

Age-standardising means pulling different populations onto one age structure before comparing them, otherwise anywhere with more old people will look like it has more cancer. Against the 2000 world standard population, the age-standardised incidence rate is 9.49 in men and 28.12 in women per 100,000; the age-standardised mortality rate is 0.39 in men and 0.50 in women[1]. Which is to say that on being diagnosed, women run about three times men, while on dying of this disease the gap between the sexes narrows to about one point three times. The sex ratio at the incidence end and at the death end is not the same number.

Taiwan's rising female curve also has a peer-reviewed version: age-standardised incidence went from 7.37 per 100,000 in 1995–1999 to 20.53 in 2015–2019, an annual percentage change of 5.9% (95% confidence interval 5.3–6.5)[2]. Those authors write in their own conclusion that they hypothesise overdiagnosis may be the main reason — that is a hypothesis, not an estimate, and I am writing it with exactly that much force.

One more thing I owe you: I could not find an official year-by-year series of age-standardised thyroid cancer mortality for Taiwan. So the 0.39 and 0.50 above are a single year standing alone, and I will not use them to say Taiwan's death rate has been flat all along.

What overdiagnosis is, and more importantly what it is not

The guideline's definition is short: overdiagnosis means diagnosing cancers that would not, if left in place, result in symptoms or death[3].

The next three sentences matter most. First, overdiagnosis is not misdiagnosis. The pathologist did not get it wrong; those really are cancer cells. Second, overdiagnosis does not mean "it isn't really cancer". It is about whether this cancer would trouble the person during the rest of their life, not about whether the diagnosis was correct. Third, and this is the crucial one: overdiagnosis can only be computed at population level and only in hindsight. The arithmetic is the number of people diagnosed minus the number in that same population who would ever have developed symptoms. No test existing today can tell me whether your nodule belongs to that difference.

That Korean curve

Korea is the most studied example. Between 1999 and 2008 incidence rose 6.4-fold (95% confidence interval 4.9–8.4), from 6.4 to 40.7 per 100,000; of that increase, 94.4% were tumours smaller than 20 mm and 97.1% were localised or regional disease[4]. In case counts: 3,407 in 1999, 44,748 in 2012, 25,523 in 2015, 30,676 in 2019[5].

Age-standardised incidence tracked like this: 24.09 per 100,000 in 2005, 74.83 in 2012, 42.52 in 2015, then flat to 2018. And the under-30 group has shown no significant decline since its 2015 peak — if anything, signs of rising again[6].

But the order of events does not line up

The version online usually runs: in 2014 Korean doctors publicly called for an end to thyroid ultrasound screening, so operations fell and incidence fell, proving it was all overdiagnosis.

The problem with that story is chronology. The study says it clearly: incidence declined between 2012 and 2016, before the public debate that began in 2014[7]. And after 2015 it rose again[5]. You cannot explain something that happened first by something that happened afterwards. The single-line causal story — one public appeal and the curve bends — is one I do not write.

"The death rate never moved" is not right either

Start with a number that gets misused here. A Korean nationwide cohort followed 434,228 people who had already been diagnosed with thyroid cancer (352,678 women, 81,550 men; mean age 48.6; median follow-up 7.4 years) and counted deaths from thyroid cancer per 1,000 person-years inside that group, indexed to the year of diagnosis: 1.94 in 2005, 0.76 in 2013, 2.70 in 2018, the rise concentrated in those who had a total thyroidectomy and those who had no thyroid surgery at all[8].

That denominator is people already diagnosed, not the population of Korea. In the years when screening was pouring large numbers of low-risk cases into that denominator, the death rate inside the group had to fall; once screening fell away, it rose again. All it can tell you is that the make-up of the diagnosed group changed. The authors say so themselves: their curve does not line up with Korea's population mortality rate, which fell continuously to 2015.

The American side is not simple either. The 2025 guideline records that between 1974 and 2013, incidence rose across all stages of disease, and that over that same period the mortality rate increased annually by 1.1% in advanced disease[3]. The same document notes that American incidence peaked at 14.9 per 100,000 in 2015, and that between 2015 and 2017 a decline appeared for the first time in thirty years.

So "more of it, and no more deaths" is a stronger claim than the evidence. What rebuts it is the American sentence: the part that went up sits in advanced disease. As for the overall trend in mortality across countries, I have no series I can cite, so I do not write one.

The 63-country estimate, and why Taiwan is not in it

The largest estimate covers 2013 to 2017, 63 countries and 385 cancer registries: of 2,297,057 cases, 1,736,133 (75.6%) were attributed to overdiagnosis, 1,368,181 of them in women and 367,952 in men. Countries where the female overdiagnosis share exceeded 85% were Cyprus, China, South Korea and Turkey; the estimate was zero in Uganda, Zimbabwe, and Trinidad and Tobago[9]. That 75.6% is a population-level difference, and there is no box in that equation for you.

The same paper carries one more contrast: the international gap between the highest and lowest female incidence had opened to 101.4 per 100,000 by 2012, while the international gap in mortality stayed around 1.0 to 2.0 per 100,000 throughout[9].

As for Taiwan: the paper's abstract does not name Taiwan, the full text is not open access, and I did not obtain the supplementary material. So Taiwan's overdiagnosis share is something I could not find. I will not infer it from other countries' numbers; those are different things.

What the official bodies say about screening

The US Preventive Services Task Force issued a grade D recommendation in 2017: against screening asymptomatic adults for thyroid cancer. The reasoning is moderate certainty, benefits bounded as no greater than small, harms bounded as at least moderate, net benefit negative[10].

But that recommendation is addressed to asymptomatic adults, not to everybody. The same document lists separately the factors that substantially raise risk: radiation to the head and neck in childhood, exposure to radioactive fallout, a first-degree relative with thyroid cancer, and genetic conditions such as familial medullary thyroid cancer or multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2[10]. Those groups are not inside the scope of that grade D. If you are one of them, this passage is not written for you.

Taiwan's publicly funded cancer screening programme does not include thyroid cancer — the full list and what it means are in 〈How this goes in Taiwan〉. That is a description of how the system currently stands, not me recommending that you do or do not do anything.

So what does this mean for you, sitting in the room

Two things, pointing opposite ways, and both have to hold.

First, a population statistic cannot be read as a conclusion about a person. "Three-quarters of cases worldwide are attributed to overdiagnosis" cannot be rewritten as "so mine probably doesn't need attention". The first is a proportion computed backwards over an entire population; the second is a judgement made today about one person, and we do not have that instrument. What decides how aggressively you are managed is the histological type, size, invasion and nodes on your report, plus your age — in 〈Papillary, follicular, NIFTP: reading the report〉 and 〈Why the stage depends on your age〉.

Second, it cannot be read as "finding things early was always pointless" either. The death rate in the advanced-disease box has been moving, and screening in high-risk groups was never inside that grade D.

One more thing worth being clear about: the road that genuinely does allow you to not operate has written inclusion and exit criteria and is not decided by yourself, and that is in 〈Watching instead of operating, on conditions〉.

The question to ask is not "how bad is it"

If you have come in holding a screening report, ask these two: "Under this report's grading, does mine go for further investigation or for follow-up? If follow-up, how long until the next one?" If you already have a diagnosis, ask these three: "Which recurrence risk group am I in?" "What is the recommended management for that box, and what does the more aggressive and the more conservative approach each cost?" "If I take the more conservative route, when do I come back, and what would bring me back sooner?" When you get to that last one, put the date into the calendar on your phone before you leave the room. A population statistic cannot help you. That date can.

From the physician

If you are holding a screening report, do not go looking up survival rates. Copy down the grade and the recommendation on it, and ask in clinic: does this one go for further investigation, or for follow-up? If follow-up, which month is the next one? Put that month in your calendar before you leave the room.
