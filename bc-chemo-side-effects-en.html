DURING TREATMENT

Which side effects mean phoning the same day

From chemotherapy and HER2 targeted therapy through to CDK4/6, PARP and immunotherapy, every line names the drug and a concrete symptom, and never says "see a doctor if you feel unwell".

Grade 3 or above thrombocytopenia with T-DM1 in Asian patients is 45%, against 15% in the overall population — the label itself states that the incidence and severity are higher in Asian patients. With T-DXd's interstitial lung disease, remember the timing: median onset at 5.5 months, 87% within twelve months, and on adjudication more than half of the events began earlier than had originally been judged.

This is where the whole series keeps its emergency warnings. I do not write "contact your doctor if you feel unwell", because that sentence tells you nothing: every line here names the drug, gives a symptom you can recognise yourself, and says what to do that day. One dividing line first — saying the symptom out loud is your job; reducing the dose, delaying it or stopping the drug is the treatment team's.

The fever line needs no judgement from you: if you measure it, phone

A single oral temperature of 38.3 °C or above during chemotherapy, or 38.0 °C or above sustained for an hour, means phoning, not waiting for tomorrow's clinic. That threshold is not one I invented: it is the definition of febrile neutropenia, that temperature together with an absolute neutrophil count below 500/µL, or one expected to fall below 500 within 48 hours; the European definition requires 38.0 °C sustained for two hours[1], and the original source is the 2010 update of the Infectious Diseases Society of America guideline[2]. It is an oncological emergency: the 2018 ASCO and Infectious Diseases Society of America guideline requires the first dose of empirical antibiotics within one hour of triage[3]. Shaking chills, a change in alertness, a drop in blood pressure and rapid breathing are equally reasons to come back to the hospital the same day.

White cell growth factor (G-CSF) is often misunderstood as "have the injection and you won't get a fever". It lowers the risk; it does not remove it. Section 4 of Taiwan's NHI drug reimbursement regulations sets it up as secondary prevention: both formulations require that after chemotherapy the white cell count has already fallen below 1000/cumm, or the neutrophil count below 500/cumm[4]. ASCO's recommendations on the use of white cell growth factors are a different document[5]; what the provision says and whether an injection is clinically indicated are two separate questions to ask.

The infused drugs are used across subtypes, and each has its own same-day signal

  • With doxorubicin there are two entirely different things to watch. One is the heart: breathlessness, breathlessness on lying flat, swollen ankles, a rapid gain in weight over a short period — that is what acute left ventricular failure looks like. The other is the injection site: severe pain, redness and swelling or blistering where the needle is, which is extravasation, and the label states that it causes tissue necrosis requiring wide excision and skin grafting and that the infusion must be stopped immediately[6].

  • Paclitaxel: breathlessness, low blood pressure, swelling of the face or throat, or hives all over, during the infusion or just as it ends. The label records severe hypersensitivity reactions in 2% to 4%, and fatal cases have occurred even with premedication[7].

  • Docetaxel: hypersensitivity reactions again, plus two more. Feet swollen too much for your shoes, generalised swelling, breathlessness at rest, or a pleural effusion needing drainage, is severe fluid retention; broken mucous membranes together with large areas of blistering and peeling skin is a severe skin reaction[8].

  • Cyclophosphamide: blood in the urine, pain on passing urine, or being unable to pass it — haemorrhagic cystitis[9].

  • Carboplatin: a rash, breathlessness or low blood pressure within minutes of the dose; and black stools, or bleeding from the gums or the nose, which is what a low platelet count looks like[10].

T-DXd and the lungs: this one is written in more detail than the rest

Trastuzumab deruxtecan is used in HER2-positive breast cancer (the metastatic indications belong to another article). The first item in the label's boxed warning is interstitial lung disease and pneumonitis, including fatal cases[11]. The numbers belong in the text: at 5.4 mg/kg as a single agent, interstitial lung disease occurred in 12% of patients, was fatal in 0.9%, and the median time to first occurrence was 5.5 months[11]. In a pooled analysis of nine single-agent trials and 1,150 patients, independently adjudicated drug-related interstitial lung disease was 15.4%, of which 2.2% was grade 5 (fatal), 87.0% of first events occurred within twelve months, and in 53.2% of events the adjudicated date of onset was earlier than the investigators had originally judged it to be[12].

Put into one sentence: the early symptoms are a dry cough, breathlessness after walking a short distance, and a slight fever — thoroughly unremarkable, and even professionals looking back at them often underestimate when they began. So the rule is that those three symptoms mean phoning the same day, not waiting until the breathlessness is bad. The label requires treatment to be held and the patient observed even at grade 1, with no symptoms at all[11].

HER2 targeted therapy and the heart: there is a schedule you can follow

Trastuzumab and pertuzumab are used in HER2-positive breast cancer. What to say the same day is a decline in cardiac function: breathlessness, breathlessness on lying flat, swollen legs, less stamina for activity; fever and chills, breathlessness or low blood pressure during the infusion or within the twenty-four hours after it also need saying[13]. Pertuzumab's boxed warning is likewise about left ventricular dysfunction, with and without symptoms[14].

The incidence has a denominator: in the joint analysis of NSABP B-31 and N9831, congestive heart failure occurred in 64/2000 (3.2%) against 21/1655 (1.3%) in the control arm[13]. Anthracyclines carry a boxed warning of their own about cardiomyopathy rising with cumulative dose[6] — used one after the other, the risks stack. The monitoring schedule set out in the label is: left ventricular ejection fraction measured once before treatment, every three months during treatment and at its completion, every four weeks if the drug is held for cardiac dysfunction, and every six months for at least two years after adjuvant treatment finishes[13]. How these cardiac tests are charged in Taiwan and how often they are done, I could not find an NHI provision I could cite; all I could find was section 9 of the drug reimbursement regulations listing a left ventricular ejection fraction below 45% as an exclusion criterion for certain drugs[15] — that one is to confirm with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.

T-DM1: the platelet figure in Asian patients has to be in the body text

Ado-trastuzumab emtansine carries a boxed warning covering hepatotoxicity (including liver failure and death) and cardiac toxicity. What to say the same day is bruising of the skin, pinpoint bleeding spots, bleeding from the gums or nose, black stools, and also yellowing of the skin or the whites of the eyes, dark urine and pain in the upper right abdomen[16]. And there is a set of lung symptoms as well: a new dry cough, breathlessness after walking a short distance, a slight fever — the T-DM1 label lists interstitial lung disease too, and if these appear do not wait for the next appointment[16] — this one cannot be told apart from radiation pneumonitis by its symptoms, and not being able to tell them apart is the reason to phone, not the reason to watch and wait. The platelet numbers matter particularly for readers in Taiwan: grade 3 or above thrombocytopenia in EMILIA overall was 15%, and the label states in so many words that the incidence and severity are higher in Asian patients — in the same trial the figure in Asian patients was 45%[16]. In those receiving adjuvant radiotherapy at the same time, radiation pneumonitis was 1.8% (11/623)[16].

The ones you take home, plus the class that can turn up after you stop

CDK4/6 inhibitors are used in hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative disease. The first hurdle with abemaciclib is diarrhoea: incidence 81% to 90% (grade 3 in 8% to 20%), with a median onset on day 6 to 8 — start managing it and tell the team at the first loose stool, not when you are dehydrated[17]. There is another item that has nothing to do with the gut and is equally a same-day matter: swelling and pain in one calf, or sudden chest pain or breathlessness, which is what venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism look like; abemaciclib's label lists venous thromboembolism among its warnings, and if any of these appears do not wait for tomorrow's clinic[17]. Palbociclib in PALOMA-2 and PALOMA-3 gave neutropenia in 80% to 83% and febrile neutropenia in 1.8%[18]. Ribociclib adds two more things to watch: a rash with fever or broken mucous membranes (severe skin reactions), and palpitations or fainting (QT prolongation)[19]. Common to all three: fever, a new cough or breathlessness (interstitial lung disease), and jaundice with dark urine.

PARP inhibitors are used in BRCA-related breast cancer. With olaparib, watch for persistent tiredness, pallor, bruising and repeated infections — the cumulative incidence of myelodysplastic syndrome or acute leukaemia is about 1.2% (26/2219), and 54% of those were fatal[20]. With talazoparib as a single agent, grade 3 or above anaemia was 39%, and extreme tiredness, palpitations, bruising and bleeding all need saying[21]. With both, watch also for pain and swelling in one leg, or sudden chest pain or breathlessness (thrombosis).

Immune checkpoint inhibitors are used mainly in triple-negative breast cancer, and the rule here is completely different from the ones above: a new symptom in any organ may be an immune-related adverse event, and it can appear after the drug has been stopped. The figure in the pembrolizumab label is immune-mediated pneumonitis in 3.4% (94/2799, including 0.1% fatal, and higher in those who have had thoracic radiotherapy)[22]. Cough, diarrhoea or blood in the stool, jaundice with dark urine, and extreme tiredness with dizziness and nausea all need saying, including after the course has finished.

Three things that are not emergencies but become permanent if left

Numbness, tingling and pain in the hands and feet are not an emergency, but they are time-sensitive. A meta-analysis across drugs (31 studies, 4,179 patients, enrolling not only breast cancer but patients with a range of cancers receiving chemotherapy) found the prevalence of peripheral neuropathy after chemotherapy was 68.1% in the first month, 60.0% at three months, and still 30.0% at six months or beyond[23]. The threshold for reporting it is not "I can't bear it"; it is not being able to fasten a button, not holding a cup steadily, not feeling the ground underfoot.

Nails are worth knowing about in advance too: the docetaxel label gives nail changes in 31% to 41% in metastatic breast cancer[8]. Blood collecting under a nail, severe pain, or pus needs looking at. Scalp cooling has randomised evidence: among 182 women with breast cancer in the SCALP trial, 48/95 (50.5%) in the cooling group kept their hair, against 0/47 in the control group[24]. But "success" there is defined as losing less than half your hair, not as not losing any, and the evidence is weaker for anthracycline-containing regimens. How scalp cooling devices are charged in Taiwan I could not find an official document for — that is one to confirm with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.

A phone card and a thermometer, beside the pill box

Do three things. First, copy the drug names off the bag and practise until you can say them — at midnight the first thing you will be asked on the phone is what you are on, and the words "chemotherapy drugs" are no help. Second, on the same sheet of paper write the numbers for the chemotherapy day unit, your case manager, and whoever to ring at night and at weekends, and stick it beside the pill box. Third, keep a thermometer in the house, take your temperature once a day and write it down; anyone on T-DXd should add one more line: any dry cough today, and any more breathlessness than yesterday.

How the drugs themselves are ordered, and which one belongs to which line, is not this article's business — the sequence of the HER2 drugs is in "HER2-positive: how the drugs are ordered", and the side effects of endocrine therapy are in "The side effects that actually make people stop". This article is responsible for one thing only: letting you know which symptom is worth interrupting someone's day for.

From the physician

On a call at midnight the first thing you will be asked is what drug you are on. Today, copy the drug names down and practise saying them, write them with the chemotherapy day unit's, your case manager's and the night and weekend numbers on one sheet beside the pill box, and check there is a working thermometer in the house.
