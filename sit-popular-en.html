BEYOND STANDARD TREATMENT

The five things the internet keeps recommending

Hyperbaric oxygen, keto and fasting, high-dose vitamin C, mistletoe, antiparasitics. Five origins; the evidence stops at five different heights.

Situation Guides · Beyond Standard Treatment · 21 references
#Reading evidence#Decisions#Regulation

Of the five, the one I most want read to the end is vitamin C, because it is really a lesson about control groups: the 1976 report claimed survival 4.2 times longer, with the controls picked out of medical records; two double-blind randomised trials later, the difference had gone. The other four follow the same order.

People who look these things up are not being fooled. They want to do something more. So I am not going to write "stop believing this stuff". Each of these has an origin, and setting out the origin is more use than denying the claim. Equally, where there is no evidence I am not going to write "it might help" for the sake of sounding even-handed. The two rulers I judge this section with are "New does not mean better" and "Approved, covered, effective: three different things".

Hyperbaric oxygen: randomised trials exist, but not for treating cancer

It starts from two opposite ideas. One: solid tumours are hypoxic, hypoxic cells resist radiation, so raising dissolved oxygen ought to help. Two: the opposite fear that "oxygen feeds the cancer". Both appear in the same review, which concludes that there is no evidence that hyperbaric oxygen stimulates tumour growth or increases recurrence[1].

What does have randomised trials is a different question: late radiation tissue injury. A Cochrane review of 18 studies and 1,071 patients found a relative risk of 1.39 for complete resolution or clear improvement of tissue injury (a relative risk divides one group's event rate by the other's; above 1 means it happened more often) and 0.24 for wound dehiscence after head and neck surgery, both at low certainty; no difference was seen in one-year mortality, but the interval around that estimate is wide (relative risk 0.93, 95% confidence interval 0.47 to 1.83, low certainty) — which means the question is unsettled, not that no difference has been established. The two highest-certainty findings are harms: reduced visual acuity 4.03 and ear barotrauma 9.08 — the latter only in trials whose control arm had no sham pressurisation; with a sham control it was 1.07[2].

Taiwan has an NHI code for it: 47054C, "general hyperbaric oxygen therapy", 720 points, effective 2023-10-01[3] (verified 2026-08-30). 47054C carries no indication list, so whether your course counts is decided on review — ask your case manager (個管師).

Keto and fasting: the starting point is real science; the human data stops at phase 2

The source is Warburg's 1956 Science paper[4] — cancer cells rely heavily on glycolysis even when oxygen is available. A chain of reasoning grows out of that: cancer eats sugar, take the sugar away and starve it. The fasting strand is newer and comes from animal work: short-term fasting protected tumour-bearing mice from chemotherapy toxicity while increasing its effect.

A meta-analysis of randomised trials of low-carbohydrate ketogenic diets as adjunctive cancer therapy found only six: blood lipids, body weight, fasting glucose and insulin all showed no significant difference, and the authors' words are that the evidence is insufficient to support a benefit of a low-carbohydrate ketogenic diet for antitumour treatment[5].

The two best-designed studies both stop at phase 2. DIRECT was a multicentre randomised phase 2 trial in 131 patients with HER2-negative breast cancer, using a fasting-mimicking diet during neoadjuvant chemotherapy (that is a calorie-restricted formula diet, not eating nothing): no difference in toxicity between the arms, more complete or partial radiological responses (odds ratio 3.168, p=0.039 — an odds ratio divides one group's ratio of "happened to did not happen" by the other's; it does not mean the probability became 3.168 times as big), and the authors conclude that further exploration is encouraged[6]. The pancreatic study was a phase 2 screening trial, 32 evaluable patients, one-sided significance level set at 0.20: progression-free survival 8.5 against 6.2 months (one-sided p=0.096), overall survival 13.7 against 10.2 months (one-sided p=0.107). A screening trial deliberately loosens the threshold to 0.20 in order to decide whether a formal trial is worth running, not whether the thing works; the authors write that the study was underpowered to draw definitive conclusions[7].

This level supports "worth researching further". It does not support changing or replacing any treatment. Both trials ran under medical supervision, in people without diabetes and above a weight floor, with chemotherapy given in full. Someone already losing weight who fasts or goes keto alone is the most concrete harm in this section: cachexia and sarcopenia are not solved by eating less. If you are on insulin, a sulfonylurea or a steroid, do not do this without discussing it with your team.

High-dose vitamin C: a lesson in why a control group matters

In 1976 Cameron and Pauling published in PNAS: 100 patients with terminal cancer given supplemental ascorbate, compared with 1,000 "similar patients treated in the same way" picked out of the records, reporting mean survival more than 4.2 times longer (over 210 days against 50 days) and asserting it was "of definite value" in treating advanced cancer[8].

The problem is those 1,000: not randomly allocated, selected from records, entering at a different time point from the referred patients, who joined at the moment of being labelled terminal. The 4.2 times does not survive that. Two double-blind randomised trials then tested it directly: 150 patients in 1979, no difference in symptoms, performance status, appetite, weight or survival[9]; and in 1985 a further 100 patients with advanced colorectal cancer who had never had chemotherapy — exactly Pauling's objection to the earlier trial — concluding that high-dose vitamin C is not effective against advanced malignant disease, whether or not chemotherapy had been given before[10].

Intravenous use is a separate question: a vein reaches concentrations oral dosing cannot, and in cell experiments those concentrations selectively kill cancer cell lines[11]. That is mechanistic work, not clinical evidence. And the modern clinical test of it is negative too: a 47-patient double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial in castration-resistant prostate cancer found a PSA decline of at least half in 41% against 33% (p=0.44), with no difference in quality of life or radiographic progression-free survival, and the trial was stopped for futility at a pre-specified interim analysis[12].

Mistletoe: nearly every positive result comes from a trial without a placebo

Mistletoe extract comes out of anthroposophic medicine — a system of therapy, not a hypothesis from tumour biology. A systematic review by anthroposophic researchers themselves is more cautious than the usual retelling: the stronger evidence is for quality of life and chemotherapy side effects, while a survival benefit has "been shown, but does not withstand criticism"[13]. The 2008 version of the Cochrane review covered 21 trials and 3,484 patients and says plainly that the reporting quality of the randomised trials was poor and the evidence that it affects survival is weak[14].

The positive trial most often produced is an open-label, single-centre randomised trial in 220 patients with advanced pancreatic cancer, stopped early for better interim survival, with better quality of life too[15] — no placebo, and the endpoint filled in by the patients themselves; those two facts travel with the result. The best-designed trial happens to test the same population: MISTRAL was double-blind, placebo-controlled and multicentre, 143 against 147 patients with advanced exocrine pancreatic cancer, subcutaneous injections three times a week for nine months: adjusted hazard ratio for overall survival 1.13, medians 7.8 against 8.3 months; quality of life compared across seven time points over nine months gave p=0.86, no difference; adverse events were similar apart from injection-site skin reactions, 66% against 1%[16]. The thing previously most believed in did not appear under the strictest design.

Antiparasitics: the evidence stops at cells and animals; the liver injury is biopsy-proven

Two origins. Fenbendazole is a veterinary anthelmintic: even the review most sympathetic to it states it is not approved for human use by the American or European regulators, that human pharmacokinetics and safety are "not well documented", and that clinical trials must be conducted to assess its anticancer effect, dose and tolerability[17]. The Taiwanese regulatory position I did not verify, so please do not reason across from the American or European status — and do not read a direction into the fact that I have not written it. Why it became popular is described in the medical literature as social media promotion[18]. Mebendazole and ivermectin have a more orthodox origin: benzimidazoles disrupt microtubules, the same broad direction as paclitaxel, and there are signals in cell experiments. "The same broad direction" goes as far as mechanism and no further: paclitaxel went through the whole human trial programme and has a dose, an indication and toxicity data; these do not, in cancer.

The highest level of human evidence is a phase 1 dose-finding trial of mebendazole in recurrent glioma, 11 people in the entire study, which produced a recommended dose and adverse event frequencies[19]. A phase 1 trial answers dose and tolerability. It does not answer whether something works.

The harms are concrete. There is biopsy-proven severe drug-induced liver injury: a 67-year-old woman who took veterinary fenbendazole on her own presented with jaundice, liver function recovering three months after stopping[18]; and a 65-year-old man with prostate cancer who, on the advice of an online patient community, alternated veterinary fenbendazole and ivermectin for three months, with ALT 1,764 U/L, AST 1,132 U/L, total bilirubin 12.9 mg/dL, and causality assessed as "highly probable"[20].

There is a quieter danger too: a case report exists specifically on how to distinguish fenbendazole-induced liver injury from immunotherapy-induced hepatitis[21]. A patient on immunotherapy who is also taking an antiparasitic without telling the team may have a rise in liver enzymes handled as an immune side effect.

Whether or not you decide to try, please do these three things

  1. Tell your medical team. Not so you can be told off. Rising liver enzymes, weight loss, electrolyte abnormalities — if the doctor does not know what you are taking, they get attributed elsewhere, and what gets stopped may be the treatment you need.

  2. Ask "will it collide with my drugs" and "does it work" as two separate questions. Interactions are in "Which supplements really do collide with your drugs"; this article deals only with levels of evidence.

  3. Jaundice, dark urine, right upper abdominal pain or unexplained extreme tiredness: contact the team the same day. Do not stop your regular treatment first.

From the physician

Whether to try something is not decided here; but write down what you are taking and bring the list to clinic. If rising liver enzymes or weight loss have no explanation, what gets stopped may be the treatment you are on. Anyone already losing weight should not fast alone, and anyone on insulin or a steroid should discuss it first. Jaundice, dark urine or right upper abdominal pain: contact the team the same day.
