BEFORE AND JUST AFTER DIAGNOSIS

Ask whether surgery is possible before you ask about chemotherapy

The first fork in lung cancer is not which treatment, but whether the tumour can be removed completely and safely — and that answer sets the order of everything else.

Lung Cancer Guide · Before and Just After Diagnosis · 11 references
#Lung#Staging#Decisions#Surgery#Treatment Pathway

Most people arrive with “do I need chemotherapy” prepared. It is a fair question in the wrong position, and this is what has to be answered first.

At the first lung cancer appointment, the question most people have prepared is "do I need chemotherapy". The doctor will push that one back, not out of reluctance, but because it comes second.

The first fork in lung cancer is not a treatment. It is a sentence that sounds simple and takes a whole team to answer: can this tumour still be removed completely and safely. The answer to that decides the order of everything afterwards, chemotherapy included.

The first fork has one question on it: can it come out completely and safely

The NCCN patient guideline puts it plainly: surgery may be an option for you if all of the cancer can be safely removed, and to decide whether you can safely undergo surgery the surgeon will consider three things — where the cancer is inside and outside your lungs, the health of your lungs, and your overall health. Cancer that can be safely and completely removed is called resectable[1].

Only the first of those three is visible on a scan. So "can I have surgery" is not a question you can answer for yourself holding a CT report; it is assessed by thoracic surgery together with the multidisciplinary team. What you can do in clinic is ask the three apart: is the problem the position, the lung function, or my general condition? Each of the three answers leads down a different road.

"Are the lungs good enough" has a pathway, and the team does the arithmetic

The 2013 third edition of the American College of Chest Physicians guideline on preoperative physiological assessment turns this into a route you can follow: start with cardiovascular assessment and spirometry, measure FEV1 and DLCO, then convert them into "predicted postoperative" (PPO) values according to how much lung is to be removed. If predicted postoperative FEV1 and DLCO are both above 60%, the patient is at low risk for anatomical lung resection and no further testing is needed; if either falls between 30% and 60%, a low-technology exercise test comes first (climbing more than 22 metres of stairs, or a shuttle walk over 400 metres, counts as low risk); if either is below 30%, or the low-technology test goes badly, cardiopulmonary exercise testing follows, where a peak oxygen uptake below 10 mL/kg/min or below 35% of predicted means high risk and above 20 mL/kg/min or 75% of predicted means low risk[2].

I write the numbers out so you can see this has an objective basis rather than being decided by impression. But please do not hold your own lung function report against those percentages — "predicted postoperative" is not a number printed on the report. It has to be calculated from how many segments are to be removed, and getting it wrong can go either way. This is arithmetic the medical team does for you.

A stage is not a label. It is four different aims of treatment

The stage distribution among cases diagnosed in Taiwan in 111年 (2022): stage 0, 6.2%; stage 1, 32.3%; stage 2, 3.7%; stage 3, 10.4%; stage 4, 47.7%[3]. In the 112年 (2023) cancer registry there were 19,986 new cases of lung, bronchus and trachea cancer, the largest number of new cases of any cancer for the third year running[4].

On survival I quote what the Ministry of Health and Welfare published, without adding a word: "at present the 5-year survival of stage 1 lung cancer is about 90%, by stage 2 it is close to 60% or above, by stage 3 it drops to nearly 30%, and if it is left until stage 4 before seeking care, 5-year survival is only 10%"[3]. I am not supplying decimal places or confidence intervals, because that is a plain-language statement in a press release, not a statistical table.

How to read it matters more. These are population averages, not your personal probability. What the stage decides is what this course of treatment is trying to achieve: whether cure can be the aim, whether systemic drugs are combined with it, whether symptoms have to be dealt with first. It is not a lottery ticket with your fate written on it.

One more thing makes reports look as though they contradict each other. The staging system is mid-revision: the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer used 76,518 analysable patients to propose the ninth edition stage groupings, subdividing N2 and M1c and moving several groupings[5]. That is a proposal, and not every hospital has switched to it. I am not printing any T, N or M definitions or a staging table here — the ninth edition's full definitions sit in a paid manual and I have no citable text for them, and taking the letters off your report and working down a table yourself is the quickest way to arrive at a stage nobody has confirmed for you. The question worth asking is: which edition was my report staged with?

If it can be removed, surgery is usually not the only step

How much to remove is itself a discussion. JCOG0802 randomised 1,106 patients from 70 Japanese institutions with clinical stage IA disease, tumour diameter 2 cm or less and a consolidation-to-tumour ratio above 0.5, to lobectomy or segmentectomy, with a median follow-up of 7.3 years: 5-year overall survival was 94.3% with segmentectomy against 91.1% with lobectomy (hazard ratio 0.663, 95% confidence interval 0.474–0.927), meeting non-inferiority — a hazard ratio compares the relative rate at which deaths occur over the same period, not a multiple of probability, and the two percentages beside it are the part you can actually get hold of; but local recurrence was 10.5% against 5.4% (p=0.0018)[6]. Taking less was not worse in small tumours that met the criteria, and local recurrence was higher. That is a trade-off, not a winner. And "met the criteria" is a narrow gate.

There may also be drugs before and after the operation. In CheckMate 816, in resectable stage IB to IIIA disease, nivolumab plus platinum chemotherapy before surgery was compared with chemotherapy alone: median event-free survival 31.6 against 20.8 months (hazard ratio 0.63, 97.38% confidence interval 0.43–0.91, P=0.005), pathological complete response 24.0% against 2.2%, and 83.2% against 75.4% actually went to surgery; but the interim survival analysis, hazard ratio 0.57, did not cross the significance threshold[7].

And after surgery. ADAURA randomised 682 patients with completely resected stage IB to IIIA disease carrying an EGFR exon 19 deletion or L858R to osimertinib or placebo for up to 3 years: 5-year overall survival in stage II to IIIA was 85% against 73% (hazard ratio 0.49, 95.03% confidence interval 0.33–0.73, P<0.001)[8]. There is an inference here that is easily missed: early-stage patients who have already had their operation may also need gene testing. What is tested and how long it takes is in "Those two weeks of waiting are not wasted".

All of these trials enrolled patients with good performance status and good organ function; a trial's survival figures are not a clinic's survival figures. And for what Taiwan's National Health Insurance (健保) covers in neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy and adjuvant targeted therapy, I have no citable current provision, so I cannot tell you whether either is covered — confirm with your case manager (個管師) or the hospital billing office (醫務課) (verification date 30 August 2026).

Unresectable stage III, where the aim is still cure

"Can't be operated on" lands in many ears as "nothing can be done", and it is one of the commonest misunderstandings in clinic. Among patients with unresectable stage III disease who had concurrent chemoradiotherapy followed by a year of consolidation therapy, 42.9% were still alive at five years — that is not a consoling turn of phrase, it is the five-year update of a randomised trial in 713 patients[9]. The whole of that section — how long the year runs, why it should not be interrupted, how to read the NHI conditions — is in "Stage III: the year after chemoradiotherapy".

There is also the situation where the tumour itself is early but the person is not fit for surgery. I do not make that comparison in this piece, because radiotherapy is what I do myself and a comparison from me needs its position declared more carefully; it is in "Inoperable does not mean incurable".

Stage IV: the aim changes, and the first thing is still the report

Almost half the people in Taiwan are already stage IV at diagnosis[3]. At this stage the aim shifts from removing everything to controlling the disease for a long time and living comfortably — and in Taiwan what decides the first-line treatment is not the stage, it is the gene report.

The NHI rules are written exactly that way, on two parallel tracks. First-line eligibility for checkpoint inhibitors (effective 1 June 2025, 114 年 6 月 1 日) requires non-squamous patients to be wild-type for all three driver genes and squamous patients for two[10]; first-line EGFR targeted therapy (effective 1 August 2026, 115 年 8 月 1 日) runs the other way, requiring an exon 19 deletion or L858R, with only one of several drugs permitted[11]. So that report does more than decide which drug you take. It decides which reimbursement pathway you walk into — the doors to both roads open on the same piece of paper. The exact wording and the exceptions to each clause are in "Those two weeks of waiting are not wasted". Verification date 30 August 2026; the rules are revised, so confirm with your case manager or the hospital billing office.

What I can and cannot say about waiting

I would like to write "a few weeks makes no difference to the outcome" to settle you, and I am not going to. I found no citable research on the relationship between the interval from diagnosis to the start of treatment and outcome in lung cancer. I have no numbers in that direction and none in the opposite direction, so I am giving you neither.

What I can talk about is order. A patient who may be resectable is waiting for a surgical assessment and lung function testing; a stage III patient is waiting for the multidisciplinary team to discuss the case; a stage IV patient is waiting for the gene report, and that report directly determines which first-line drug is used. These waits contain different things and cannot be weighed inside the same anxiety. Which symptoms cannot wait until the next appointment is a separate matter, and the thresholds are in "Breathlessness, blood, fluid: which one is today".

Four questions in this order beat asking everything at once

What stage am I, and which edition of the staging system was my report written with? Am I resectable at the moment, and if not, is it the position, the lung function or my general condition that is in the way? If surgery is not possible, what is the aim of what comes next? And — during this stretch of waiting, what should I watch for that means coming back early?

These four are in order; the answer to each one shapes how you ask the next. Copy them down and read them in sequence. Do not skip.

From the physician

Resectability is judged by thoracic surgery with the multidisciplinary team, using lung function figures the team calculates rather than ones you can read off a report. Ask the four questions in order at your next appointment; the answer to each shapes how you ask the next.
