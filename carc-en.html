CARCINOGENS

Carcinogens at work and at homeOne carcinogen per article: where it is, which cancers it causes, how hard the evidence is, and how Taiwan regulates it now.

Each article here is one thing: asbestos, silica, benzene, hexavalent chromium and arsenic, formaldehyde, cooking fumes and second-hand smoke, radon and sunlight, alcohol and betel quid. Each follows the same order — where it is, which cancers it causes, how hard the evidence is, how Taiwan regulates it, and what you can do today.

One thing gets misread more than any other: an IARC group describes how certain we are that something causes cancer, not how dangerous it is. Processed meat and asbestos are both Group 1, yet the cancer deaths attributable to each differ by orders of magnitude. Read the group column together with the last two.

1At Work

The ones with a legally defined operation category, a special health examination and a route to occupational disease recognition. These are also the ones Taiwan regulates most clearly.

Leave that asbestos roof alone
Undisturbed asbestos material is low risk and only turns dangerous once it is broken; mesothelioma mostly appears thirty to forty years after first exposure, at about 3 cases per million men a year in Taiwan.

Engineered stone silicosis is fibrosing the lungs of men in their thirties, and the risk sits with the workers who cut and grind, not with the benchtop already fitted in your kitchen.

2At Home

The ones nobody monitors for you and no health examination will catch. Here you have to judge the proportions yourself.

How much should the formaldehyde in a new home worry you?
Formaldehyde is Group 1, but the case that it causes nasopharyngeal cancer rests on about a dozen occupational deaths worldwide, and no study has ever looked at residential formaldehyde and nasopharyngeal cancer at all.

Why do mothers who never smoked get lung cancer?
Secondhand smoke is Group 1 and cooking fumes are Group 2A, yet Taiwan's largest prospective cohort saw neither; turning the multiples into absolute numbers changes the sense of proportion.

Radon is not Taiwan's problem. Sunlight is.
The Nuclear Safety Commission measured 293 homes at a mean of 19.33 Bq/m³ with not one above the WHO recommendation; the exposure nobody regulates for outdoor workers is the one they get every day.

Processed meat, alcohol, betel quid and asbestos all sit in Group 1, yet annual attributable cancer deaths run from about 34,000 to about 1 million; the grouping describes the strength of the evidence.

IF YOU WORK WITH THESE

If you are doing one of these jobs

Work listed as a specially hazardous operation carries an employer duty to arrange special health examinations, and the management grade on the report decides who does what next. That column usually goes unexplained — "Washing your hands in solvent" takes it apart.

AFTER YOU LEAVE

If you have already left the job

With long-latency carcinogens the problem often surfaces long after the work ended. The Labor Occupational Accident Insurance and Protection Act provides a health examination after withdrawal from insurance, and the worker does not pay for it; which operations qualify and how often it can be claimed are to be confirmed with the Bureau of Labor Insurance or an occupational medicine clinic. Write the work history down — the job, the years, the hours a day, whether there was protective equipment. Everything else starts there.

This is general patient education and does not replace a consultation. Reading an exposure history depends heavily on the work you actually did, for how long and with what protection; the same substance leads to different advice in different people. If you think your illness may be related to your work, take a full occupational history to an occupational medicine clinic.
