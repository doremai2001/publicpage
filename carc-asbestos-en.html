AT WORK

Leave that asbestos roof alone

Undisturbed asbestos material is low risk and only turns dangerous once it is broken; mesothelioma mostly appears thirty to forty years after first exposure, at about 3 cases per million men a year in Taiwan.

What Taiwan banned in 2018 was the manufacture and use of raw asbestos; import of asbestos-containing products stopped only on 1 May 2023, and the material already in roofs and partition walls is still there. This piece handles “intact and undisturbed” separately from “broken and taken down”, and finishes the route still open to those who have left the job.

"Is that sheet on my roof asbestos — should I get it off in a hurry?" and "I spent eight years in a shipyard wearing nothing at all, am I finished?" Asbestos brings only these two people into my clinic, and their next moves are opposite: the first should leave it alone; the second still has something to do today.

The sheet that is intact and the sheet you break are not the same object

The Ministry of Environment's asbestos information section gives the official position: while asbestos building material is "structurally intact and undamaged, asbestos fibres are not released; it can be regarded with confidence, and the health hazard to the human body is low risk"[1]; the common items are corrugated asbestos roof tiles and sheet, gypsum or magnesium oxide board, and roofing felt[1]. Danger starts the moment you go at it: cutting, drilling, grinding, striking, pulling it down.

Read this as a hard rule: do not remove it yourself, do not break it, do not water-blast an asbestos roof, do not cut suspected asbestos material with an angle grinder or a circular saw. The Guidelines on Hazard Prevention for Asbestos Building Material Demolition Work, from the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), Ministry of Labor, require double containment, HEPA-filtered local exhaust, negative pressure, a decontamination area, and wetting before cutting, drilling or grinding[2]. Removal goes to a licensed construction business[1]. A mask is not the answer: OSHA puts the dust mask in the back-up position, outside engineering control[3], and not one of the measures that actually holds fibres back can be set up on your own roof. Which grade of mask is not a homeowner's decision; why engineering control outranks protective equipment is in "The price of cutting one stone benchtop".

Why it takes thirty years to surface

The 2014 Helsinki Criteria are direct: attributing malignant mesothelioma to asbestos requires at least 10 years since first exposure, and most cases fall at 30 to 40 years[4]; Taiwan's OSHA guideline for recognising malignant mesothelioma adopts the same statement[5]. The Great Britain Asbestos Survey measures from another end: of 614 asbestos workers who died of mesothelioma between 1978 and 2005, median latency from first occupational exposure to death was 22.8 years (95% confidence interval 16.0 to 27.2 years — the bracket is the range in which the estimate plausibly sits), about 29% longer in women than men[6]. No conflict: the second counts as far as death, and that cohort began follow-up only in 1978[6].

That gap is the skeleton of the occupational disease system: the job you did twenty years ago still counts today. These year figures apply to asbestos and nothing else.

I was exposed. Is mesothelioma coming for me?

Magnitude first. The Taiwan Cancer Registry recorded 216 male malignant mesothelioma cases between 2008 and 2012, an age-standardised incidence of about 3 per million population per year, and 101 female cases, about 1[5]. About 3 in a million men a year: it is still a rare disease here, and the great majority of people who have been in contact with asbestos will not get cancer from it. That is so you do not panic, not so you skip the examination — the one thing in this article running on a clock is in the seventh section.

Hodgson and Darnton reviewed estimates across exposure cohorts: for crocidolite and amosite, each cumulative 1 fibre/ml-year of exposure adds about 5% excess lung cancer; for chrysotile the best estimate is 0.1%[7]. Those are occupational-cohort estimates and cannot be laid over someone living under an asbestos roof; for the Taiwanese general public there is no citable local figure, and I will not invent one.

Nor can I give you a level below which it is safe. Recital 7 of EU Directive 2023/2668 says it plainly: asbestos is a non-threshold carcinogen, and science cannot point to an exposure below which no harm arises; the directive lowers the occupational exposure limit to 0.01 f/cm³[8]. Taiwan's current 8-hour time-weighted average permissible concentration is 0.15 f/cc[3] — that is a management trigger, not a safety threshold.

Asbestos on top of tobacco: added, or multiplied?

Monograph 100C of the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), 2012, puts all six asbestos types in Group 1, with sufficient evidence in humans for malignant mesothelioma and cancer of the lung, larynx and ovary; pharynx, stomach and colorectum reach only "a positive association has been observed"[9]. Group 1 describes how certain the evidence is, not how dangerous the agent is — "Group 1 is not a danger ranking" and this topic's entry page, "Carcinogens at work and at home", cover that in full.

The literature diverges, so both sides go down. OSHA's recognition guideline for lung cancer says asbestos and smoking raise risk "multiplicatively", citing Selikoff's insulation workers: smoking about 11-fold, non-smokers with over 20 years of asbestos exposure about 5-fold, both about 55-fold[10]. A 2015 PLoS One meta-analysis of 17 observational studies came out otherwise: against "neither exposed nor smoking", the odds ratio was 1.70 for asbestos alone (95% confidence interval 1.31 to 2.21), 5.65 for smoking alone (3.38 to 9.42), 8.70 for both (5.80 to 13.10); the additive interaction index of 1.44 (1.26 to 1.77) reached statistical significance, the multiplicative index of 0.91 (0.63 to 1.30) did not[11]. That 8.70 is not "8.7 times the chance of cancer": an odds ratio compares two groups' ratio of happened against did-not-happen, and runs larger than the true multiple of risk. One statement holds up and no more: the combined risk is far higher than either factor alone, and whether it adds or multiplies, the literature still disagrees. These multiples multiply a baseline risk each person already carries; the absolute magnitude is that 5% and 0.1% excess lung cancer[7]. In clinic the disagreement changes nothing; in recognition of occupational disease it does, because practice follows OSHA's guideline, and that guideline takes the multiplicative position[10]. Only one of the two factors is yours to move.

Mesothelioma is separate. The Helsinki Criteria state that smoking does not affect the risk of malignant mesothelioma[4], and Taiwan's recognition criteria use that point to rule other causes out[5]. Tobacco and cooking fumes in their own right are in "Why do mothers who never smoked get lung cancer?".

So Taiwan has had no asbestos since 2018?

It has. The Ministry of Environment controls asbestos as a class 2 toxic chemical substance (control number 003-01, control concentration 1%), all six types prohibited from manufacture, import, sale and use except for research, testing and education[12], from 1 January 2018; asbestos-containing building boards had been banned earlier, in 2006[13].

The next sentence is the one that matters: what 2018 banned was the manufacture and use of raw asbestos, and the import of asbestos-containing products ran on a separate timeline — the Ministry of Environment announced the restriction only in October 2022, and import was prohibited only from 1 May 2023[14]. OSHA concedes in its guideline that use of asbestos-containing products "has not entirely ceased", with nearly 1,000 tonnes still imported in 2018[5]. What is already built in does not disappear, and the work that still meets asbestos is specific: pipework maintenance, maintenance of fireproofing and insulating material, construction, demolition of old buildings[3].

If I do fall ill, does it count as an occupational disease?

The route exists, but not on exposure alone. For malignant mesothelioma, OSHA's criteria require a latency of at least 10 years from first exposure[5]. The lung cancer volume takes the Helsinki quantitative threshold — a cumulative exposure of 25 fibres/ml-years is estimated to double lung cancer risk[4][10] — and requires at least 10 years between onset and the start of exposure[10]. That temporal requirement is the shared skeleton of every occupational cancer recognition.

Pleural plaques are misread more than anything else here. Helsinki calls them a marker of asbestos exposure[4]; the Taiwanese guideline lists them as a supporting criterion, to be paired with cumulative exposure or fibre burden analysis[10]. Seeing them means you really were in contact; it does not mean you have the disease being attributed.

What to prepare is exposure evidence: which years, which company, which process, whether a labour insurance enrolment record exists. Where to ask is an occupational medicine clinic. Reporting is short as it stands — OSHA's own guideline says confirmed malignant mesothelioma cases still fall short of the theoretical estimate[5]. Nobody raises it for you; it does not happen by itself.

You left the job and the insurance. One route is still open.

Article 4 of the Regulations for Preventive Occupational Disease Health Examinations and Health Follow-up Examinations under the Labor Occupational Accident Insurance provides that a worker who has performed an operation listed in Appendix 2 and has been continuously insured for a full year may, after changing operation, leaving the job or withdrawing from the insurance, apply to the insurer — through the insured unit or in person — for a "preventive occupational disease health follow-up examination"[15].

More than one version of the permitted number is in circulation, and I could not obtain the original Appendix 2 to check it against, so I write only what I am sure of: this route exists, you can apply after leaving the job or the insurance, and the worker does not pay for it[15]. For how many examinations and which categories of operation qualify, ask the Bureau of Labor Insurance or an occupational medicine clinic[16].

Anyone still in the job is on a different line: handling or use of asbestos is one of the categories of preventive occupational disease health examination announced by the Bureau of Labor Insurance[16]; how to read the management grading is in "Washing your hands in solvent". Low-dose computed tomography is not a recommendation for everyone who has touched asbestos: those meeting the publicly funded eligibility can have it, and that eligibility is set out in "Why do mothers who never smoked get lung cancer?"; paying for a scan when you do not qualify is exactly where overdiagnosis happens, which is what "When screening finds too much" is about.

Two phone calls before the drill comes out

The first goes to a licensed construction business. Before you make it, stand on the ground and look: broken, weathered, cracked? If it is intact, leave it there, and do not take a pressure washer to it for a tidy-up. If it truly has to come off, what you want is not a crew who will "take it away while they're here" — waste code C-0701, it goes to a clearance and disposal organisation approved by the Ministry of Environment[1].

The second is for anyone who has left the job or the insurance, to the Bureau of Labor Insurance: is the work I used to do listed in Appendix 2, and can I still apply for a health follow-up examination?[15] Write out that page of exposure history first; in clinic I will use it directly.

For anyone about to start work: before an old house is gutted, ask what the roof, the partition boards and the pipe lagging are made of. What you want is not panic, but knowing what that board is before the drill touches it. If the answer is asbestos, the next person holding the drill should not be you.

From the physician

Two reactions worry me most: seeing an asbestos sheet and wanting to climb up and pull it off yourself, or deciding twenty years have passed so it no longer matters. The first scatters fibres; the second loses the record you should be keeping. What you can actually do is small — leave it alone, write down your exposure history, and if you have left the job, make one phone call to the Bureau of Labor Insurance.
