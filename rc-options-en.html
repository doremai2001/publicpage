HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

How the treatment order is set, and why it is not a menu

Why one person goes straight to theatre and another does five and a half weeks of chemoradiation first — and why radiotherapy buys the pelvis, not extra years.

Rectal Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 16 references
#Decisions#Surgery#Chemotherapy#Immunotherapy#NHI Coverage

Nobody hands you a list and asks which treatment you would like. What you can ask for is the reasoning — which finding put you in this sequence, and what each step is buying.

Rectal cancer has three tools: an operation, radiotherapy, chemotherapy. What patients want to know is why they got this particular order — why one person goes to theatre the week they are diagnosed, another does five and a half weeks of chemoradiation first, and a third has five days of radiotherapy and then six months of chemotherapy before anyone opens them up.

This article lays the logic out, starting with the least popular sentence in it: radiotherapy has never been shown to lengthen life in rectal cancer. What it buys is the pelvis. That is easily misread as "so I can skip it", so I will explain it properly.

One thing to block first: this is not a menu. ASTRO's 2025 focused update says the choice of regimen, the sequencing, and whether non-operative management is considered at all "should be determined by multidisciplinary consensus", based on disease extent, tumour location, patient preference and quality of life[1]. Your preference is in that sentence, but as one variable among several. What sets the order is how many centimetres the tumour sits from the anus, whether the mesorectal fascia is threatened on MRI, and what the nodes look like — terms explained in "What T3, MRF and EMVI actually mean on your report".

On the surgical side, a 1982 paper rewrote the rules

In 1982 the British surgeon Bill Heald reported something nobody was looking for: inside the mesorectum, several centimetres below the visible edge of the tumour, microscopic cancer could still be found. He took the whole mesorectum out intact inside its envelope — total mesorectal excision (TME). Of more than a hundred consecutive anterior resections, fifty were classified as curative and followed more than two years, with no recurrence in the pelvis or at the anastomosis[2].

That paper is an uncontrolled case series and would not count as high-grade evidence today. Its weight comes from the forty years after it: rectal surgery has been built around taking the mesorectum out whole ever since. If your operation report says the mesorectum was complete, that is good news.

Adding radiotherapy buys the pelvis, not the years

The Dutch TME trial randomised 1,861 patients with resectable rectal cancer to five fractions of 5 Gy before TME, or to TME alone. Two-year local recurrence was 2.4% versus 8.2% (p<0.001), and two-year survival identical, 82.0% versus 81.8%[3]. At a median of twelve years' follow-up, ten-year local recurrence was 5% versus 11% (p<0.0001), with overall survival still no different[4].

In plain language: a pelvic recurrence is the hardest recurrence in rectal cancer to deal with. It grows in a small space walled in by bone, nerve, bladder and ureter; usually it cannot be cut out again or irradiated a second time, and what is left is persistent pelvic pain and the collapse of bowel and bladder function. Those six percentage points buy not ending up there. The same follow-up holds one other finding — in stage III patients with a negative circumferential resection margin, ten-year survival was 50% versus 40%[4] — but that is a subgroup analysis. I put it in print; I do not treat it as a promise.

Then why is the chemoradiation given before the operation?

The German CAO/ARO/AIO-94 trial answered exactly this. 823 patients with cT3/T4 or node-positive disease were randomised to chemoradiation before or after surgery — same dose, same drugs, only the order differed. At five years: local relapse 6% versus 13% (p=0.006), grade 3–4 acute toxicity 27% versus 40% (p=0.001), long-term toxicity 14% versus 24% (p=0.01), and five-year overall survival 76% versus 74% (p=0.80)[5]. At eleven years, ten-year local relapse was 7.1% versus 10.1% (p=0.048) and ten-year survival 59.6% versus 59.9% (p=0.85)[6].

The honest version: before rather than after buys better local control and fewer side effects, not a longer life. The first two are worth having. I will not use the third to package them.

Some go straight to surgery; some have only a small piece taken out

Not everyone is irradiated first. ASTRO 2025 strongly recommends preoperative radiotherapy in stage II and III, but the same document conditionally recommends omitting it in patients at lower risk of local recurrence, in favour of surgery after a good response to preoperative chemotherapy, or surgery straight away[1]. For that group the guideline is moving towards doing less.

Earlier tumours also have local excision. NCCN version 3.2024 lists endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) for early lesions[7]. Transanal local excision (TAMIS) has been tested too: ACOSOG Z6041 enrolled 79 patients with cT2N0 tumours under 4 cm and within 8 cm of the anal verge, giving chemoradiation and then local excision. Three-year disease-free survival was 88.2% (95% CI 81.3–95.8); the price was grade 3 gastrointestinal adverse events in 29% during the chemoradiation[8].

The authors' own wording is worth copying out: it "might be considered … in carefully selected patients … who refuse, or are not candidates for, transabdominal resection"[8]. It is not a parallel option to TME. A systematic review of robotic TAMIS covering 26 studies and 1,284 patients is just as guarded: R0 resection 89.1%, local recurrence 8.3% at a median of 28 months, gaps still open in the long-term survival data[9].

Chemotherapy after the operation too? This one really is unsettled

Plenty of people assume a few months of chemotherapy after preoperative chemoradiation and surgery goes without saying. The data do not hold up that well. An individual-patient-data meta-analysis of four European randomised trials, 1,196 patients with ypStage II/III disease and an R0 resection: overall survival HR 0.97 (95% CI 0.81–1.17, p=0.775), disease-free survival HR 0.91 (0.77–1.07). Only the subgroup 10–15 cm from the anal verge benefited, and the interaction p value there was 0.107 — that generates a hypothesis, it does not prove one[10].

Taiwanese data point the same way. Cancer registry linked to the National Health Insurance (健保) database, 720 rectal cancer patients treated between 2007 and 2017 who responded well to chemoradiation (ypT0-2N0), 51.1% of them given adjuvant chemotherapy: no difference in five-year overall survival (p=0.681), none in disease-free survival (p=0.942), adjusted hazard ratios 1.03 (0.88–1.21) and 1.05 (0.89–1.24)[11].

That does not make it wrong for a doctor to recommend it — node counts, how complete the mesorectum was, the margins and your response all feed the decision. But it is a fair question to put out loud: "given my pathology report, roughly how much benefit do you expect from chemotherapy after the operation?"

Every chemotherapy above shares one list of reasons to telephone the same day: a fever over 38°C, severe diarrhoea with dehydration (dizzy on standing, passing much less urine), mouth ulcers bad enough to stop you swallowing, or numbness in the hands and feet that is worse than before. On capecitabine, watch your palms and soles: redness, peeling and tingling is hand-foot syndrome, to be reported rather than treated with a cream and a stiff upper lip. On oxaliplatin you will get cold-induced nerve symptoms — fingers or throat feeling shocked by cold water or a cold drink. Avoid cold, and say so at every visit; dose adjustment runs on that feedback. Never stop or reduce a dose yourself.

dMMR/MSI-H: another road for a few people, on evidence still very young

A small minority of rectal cancers carry mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR) or high microsatellite instability (MSI-H), and those tumours behave completely differently under PD-1 immunotherapy. The Memorial Sloan Kettering single-arm phase 2 trial in 2022: 12 patients with dMMR stage II/III rectal cancer given dostarlimab alone for six months; all 12 (100%, 95% CI 74–100) reached a clinical complete response, none had chemoradiation or surgery, follow-up ran from 6 to 25 months, no grade 3 or worse adverse events[12]. The expanded 2025 report covered 117 patients: in the rectal group all 49 who completed treatment reached a clinical complete response and not one had the primary tumour resected, 37 still in complete response at 12 months; across the study, two-year recurrence-free survival was 92% (95% CI 86–99), and no patient lost the chance of curative surgery because of it[13].

Precisely because it looks this good, draw the boundaries: single-arm trials, no control group, follow-up still counted in months and a few years; a clinical complete response at 12 months is not a cure; it does not currently replace chemoradiation and surgery as a standard in any hospital; and it applies only to the dMMR/MSI-H minority.

One useful thing follows: ask your doctor whether your tumour has had MMR/MSI testing, and what it showed. This is a test result, not a treatment you can argue your way into. Delaying standard treatment in the hope of immunotherapy is the worst bargain I have watched anyone make.

In Taiwan, which parts the NHI pays for and which you must ask about

The radiotherapy end is reasonably clear. The National Health Insurance (健保) schedule of benefit payments lists item 36015B, complex computerised treatment planning (11,483 points), with a note covering plans for "conformal radiotherapy, intensity-modulated radiotherapy and stereotactic radiotherapy"; proton therapy is not in the payment standard at all, so it is self-pay[14].

The chemotherapy end needs care. In the current drug reimbursement regulations, Section 9, the colorectal indications for 9.10 oxaliplatin are only metastatic colorectal cancer and "adjuvant therapy after complete resection of the primary tumour in stage III colon cancer (Duke's C)"[15]; 9.17 capecitabine lists only first-line metastatic colorectal cancer and adjuvant treatment after surgery for stage III colon cancer[16]. Neither carries a reimbursed indication for preoperative treatment of rectal cancer. If either is in your plan, ask your case manager (個管師) or the hospital's billing office: covered, or self-pay? I will not quote a figure for any hospital.

The order is not yours to pick, but it can be explained to you

The order is not yours to pick, but every step has a reason and the reason can be said out loud. Ask first: am I in the group that needs preoperative treatment or the group that can go straight to surgery, and which test result decides that? Then: if I am having preoperative treatment, is the goal local control, shrinking the tumour to make the operation easier, or preserving an organ — because those three goals produce three different schedules.

Two shorter ones still need answers. Has my tumour had MMR/MSI testing? And is any part of this plan self-pay?

From the physician

Your sequence was set by a multidisciplinary team reading your MRI, your tumour's height and your nodes, none of which is visible from a waiting-room conversation. Bring two questions to your next appointment: which finding decided my order, and has my tumour had MMR/MSI testing? If oxaliplatin or capecitabine is in the plan, ask your case manager (個管師) about coverage before treatment starts.
