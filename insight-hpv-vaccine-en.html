VACCINE & PREVENTION

An entire age band, zero deaths

In England between 2020 and 2024, not one woman aged 20 to 24 died of cervical cancer. The first mortality data for HPV vaccination.

Insights · Vaccines & Prevention · 4 references
#Vaccine#Prevention#Cervical#Gynaecologic

That the vaccine reduces infection and pre-cancer was settled twenty years ago. The harder question has always been the next one: has it saved lives? It takes two to three decades from infection to death, and the first vaccinated cohort has only now reached an age where that becomes visible.

The HPV vaccine has been in use for close to twenty years, and its effect on infection and precancerous lesions was settled long ago. The harder question was whether it keeps women alive. Cervical cancer usually takes two to three decades to develop and kill, so the first vaccinated cohorts are only now reaching the ages where a mortality signal could appear. An American Cancer Society analysis published in JNCI in 2026 compared the pre-vaccine period (2000–2005) with the vaccine era (2016–2021) and found that cervical cancer incidence among US women aged 20 to 31 fell by 27% (RR 0.73, 95% CI 0.70–0.75), with the steepest declines in states that had achieved the highest vaccination coverage (rho = −0.42)[1].

That answer arrived in July 2026. Sasieni and Falcaro published an analysis of England's national mortality data from 2001 to 2024 in The Lancet[2]. England began vaccinating girls aged 12 to 13 in 2008 and has held coverage above 80% since. Between 2020 and 2024, not one woman aged 20 to 24 died of cervical cancer, against 23.1 deaths predicted from historical rates, a 100% reduction in mortality (95% CI 84–100). Women aged 25 to 29 saw a 69% reduction (55–79). By the end of 2024 the programme was associated with roughly 199.6 fewer cervical cancer deaths (125.0–274.2). The authors are direct about the design's limits: this is population-level observational data, not a randomised trial, and changes in screening and behaviour over the same years may account for part of the decline. The direction still matches the US incidence figures[1].

Taiwan's turning point came in September 2025, when publicly funded nine-valent HPV vaccination widened from junior-high girls to every junior-high student, boys included; those under 15 receive two doses at least six months apart[3]. Boys are in the programme because HPV causes oropharyngeal, anal and genital cancers as well as cervical cancer, and a cost-effectiveness model for adolescent males in Asia estimated that nine-valent vaccination at 70% coverage is cost-effective[4], though that is a modelled projection rather than an observed clinical result. If you have a child in junior high, the school schedule is the easiest way to finish the series. Women who completed the vaccine still need Pap and HPV testing on the usual schedule, since the vaccine does not cover every oncogenic type and cannot clear an infection already present. Nothing in these data is a reason to change ongoing cancer treatment; take specific questions to your oncologist.

From the physician

If you have a child in junior high, follow the schedule the school sets — since September 2025 Taiwan's publicly funded nine-valent vaccine covers all junior high students, boys included. Adult women who have completed vaccination still need routine cervical screening and HPV testing, because the vaccine does not cover every oncogenic type and cannot clear an existing infection.
