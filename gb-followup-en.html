How the follow-up scans are scheduled, and why bigger is not always worse

The baseline moved to the scan taken after radiotherapy, the twelve-week confirmation rule, and how the denominator behind "53 of the 166 whose scans looked worse" is worked out.

Glioblastoma Guide · Afterwards · 11 references
#Follow-up#Imaging#Recurrence#Evidence

Since 2023, the scan every later one is measured against is the one taken after radiotherapy, not the one taken after the operation. This article also takes apart a very tempting association: that people scanned more regularly live longer — which is most likely an artefact of the statistics.

"The scan looks bigger." The principle behind this question only holds up in hindsight — at the moment it is happening, there is no imaging feature and no symptom that separates a reaction to treatment from a tumour that is genuinely growing. So let me block the sentence I least want taken out of this article first: "it might be pseudoprogression" is not the same thing as "so I can wait a bit longer". Do not move your appointment.

Which scan is each of the later ones actually being measured against

The reading criteria updated in 2023 (RANO 2.0) changed something a lot of people do not know about: for a newly diagnosed patient, the baseline every later scan is compared against is the MRI taken after radiotherapy, not the one taken after the operation[1]. The same document also says that in IDH-wildtype glioblastoma, non-enhancing lesions are no longer included in the assessment, except when the response being assessed is to an anti-angiogenic drug[1] — so when the report says "T2/FLAIR slightly increased" and your doctor does not treat it as progression, that is not something being missed.

The same machine and the same set of sequences is not a ritual, it is the precondition for two scans being comparable at all — the standardised imaging protocol written for clinical trials pins down the minimum combination of sequences[2].

Of the 166 people whose scans looked worse, 53 were pseudoprogression and 113 were real

South-Eastern Norway enrolled 284 glioblastoma patients treated with radiotherapy between 2015 and 2018 who had follow-up imaging. On the scan taken about three months after radiotherapy, re-read afterwards, the result was: pseudoprogression in 53 (19.4%), true progression in 113 (41.4%), stable in 104 (38.1%)[3].

Change the denominator to the 166 people whose scans were not stable: about a third (53 people) were pseudoprogression, about two-thirds (113 people) were real progression. That proportion is my own conversion from the counts in the paper; what the paper itself reports is those three percentages. Hold on to the direction: when the imaging looks worse, the optimistic reading is less likely than the pessimistic one. By the six-month scan, pseudoprogression was down to 17 people (7.0%); taking out the 8 already scored at three months, the newly appearing figure is only 3.8%[3].

You may have seen higher numbers elsewhere. A single-centre study in Bologna in 2008 enrolled 103 people whose first MRI was booked one month after chemoradiotherapy finished: 50 lesions had grown, and were classified afterwards as 32 pseudoprogression and 18 early true progression; among the methylated, 21 of 23 (91%) were pseudoprogression, among the unmethylated 11 of 27 (41%), P=0.0002[4]. Please do not take that 91% home: the denominator is 23 people, from 2008, from one centre, from before the RANO criteria existed, and what it measured was the scan closest to the end of radiotherapy. The Norwegian data, using the current criteria and a much larger denominator, does not push pseudoprogression anywhere near that high. A report saying methylated does not make this particular change 90% likely to be nothing, and does not make "wait one more time" a safe option. The original RANO paper of 2010 puts it differently again: after concurrent chemoradiotherapy, 20% to 30% of newly diagnosed patients develop a transient increase in enhancement[5] — and there the denominator is all patients. The three numbers do not contradict each other, but any one of them taken alone will mislead you.

"He does not feel unwell" cannot be used as evidence

In the adjusted analysis of the Norwegian data, absence of neurological deterioration was indeed associated with pseudoprogression (P=0.029 at three months, P=0.034 at six months), and so was MGMT promoter methylation (P<0.001 at three months)[3]. Both of those are associations calculated at population level after the scans were re-read in hindsight, not tests that work on one person. This site drew that line already, in "The scan looks worse. The disease may not be."

Four to eight weeks, and twelve weeks: both are reading rules, not a waiting period you can help yourself to

Two official documents each give a window. The 2021 guideline from the European Association of Neuro-Oncology says that when progression is suspected, arranging a short-interval repeat MRI within four to eight weeks to confirm it is a reasonable approach; the same passage adds that pseudoprogression and pseudoresponse are most likely in the first three months, but can occur later[6]. RANO 2.0 is harder: the incidence of pseudoprogression is high within twelve weeks of radiotherapy, and during that period a call of "progression" has to be confirmed by repeat MRI or by pathology, with treatment continuing meanwhile; past that period, confirmatory imaging is no longer mandatory[1].

Read that with both layers attached. First, it is a rule for scoring a lesion, not an interval between clinic visits; the person who judges "clinically stable" is the doctor, not you deciding you feel all right. Second, through that period treatment continues and so does monitoring — it is not a stretch of buffer you can award yourself.

"Then I'll pay for a PET scan" — 90% accurate, and then what

Advanced imaging does not perform badly. A meta-analysis in high-grade glioma lines several approaches up side by side (sensitivity is "when it really is progressing, this picks it up"; specificity is "when it is not, this does not raise a false alarm"): conventional MRI 68% and 77% (5 studies, 166 patients); MR spectroscopy 91% and 95% (9 studies, 203 patients)[7]. That 95% needs discounting: its 95% confidence interval runs from 65 to 99% — a range like that is where the estimate plausibly sits, not your personal probability.

With PET you have to be clear which kind. In a meta-analysis of 39 studies and 11 tracers, the amino-acid tracers had higher sensitivity than fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG): FET 90% (7 studies, 172 lesions), MET 93% (8 studies, 151 lesions), FDG 84% (12 studies, 171 lesions)[8]. Where the article on this site says PET makes the distinction harder, it is talking about FDG in the setting of immunotherapy; do not transfer that onto amino-acid PET in brain tumours.

The more important sentence is the one the 2025 RANO and EANO update on PET imaging writes about itself: on one hand it stresses that amino-acid PET outperforms glucose PET, and on the other it states plainly that there is a lack of level 1 evidence that putting PET into the clinical pathway improves patient outcomes[9]. Ninety per cent accuracy and living longer for having done it are two different things.

Biopsy is not the end point either. The guideline's own wording is that a biopsy is often not informative, because viable tumour cells are frequently found and their presence does not rule out pseudoprogression[6]. It has happened in the other direction too: in the Norwegian cohort, the two patients whose treatment was changed on the strength of imaging both had a second operation, and the pathology showed only reactive tissue and necrosis[3]. On the Taiwanese side: MRI and PET are both existing items in the National Health Insurance fee schedule[10], but on whether there is a frequency limit for repeating the same region at short interval I could not find a provision I could cite, so how it is actually claimed is a question for your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office; whether amino-acid PET can be obtained in Taiwan at all is another one to put to your team.

How often to scan, and the word the guideline uses for its own answer is "practice"

The evidence behind the follow-up interval after treatment finishes is weaker than you would expect. The European guideline's wording is: "after the end of treatment, imaging every two to six months is common practice in most patients, depending on histology"[6] — common practice is the phrase used, not a grade of recommendation.

On the ground it is more uneven still. Data from 26 centres in Great Britain and Ireland covering 754 patients with WHO grade 4 tumours show two local recommendations in force, "within 72 hours of surgery, then every three to six months" and "within 48 hours of surgery, then every three months"; adherence was 52.8% and 24.9%, and the median number of subsequent follow-up MRI scans was one[11].

"Scan more often and you live longer" is an association that is very easy to fall for

The multivariable analysis in that same British and Irish dataset is tempting: the longer a patient spent inside the recommended follow-up interval, the longer their overall survival (hazard ratio 0.56, 95% confidence interval 0.46 to 0.66, P<0.001). A hazard ratio divides the rate at which deaths occur in one group by the rate in the other, so 0.56 means deaths were occurring more slowly. But there was no difference in progression-free survival[11].

If scanning more really did improve outcomes by catching things early, progression-free survival is what should have moved first. That it did not move while overall survival did has one very common explanation, and it runs the other way round: only people who live longer have the chance to accumulate more time that counts as "within the recommendation". The authors themselves write only that there is an association, and call for a prospective trial to answer whether regular follow-up affects outcome at all[11]. The same statistical trap turns up again over re-operation in "After recurrence, what roads remain".

On the scan that is uncertain, the worst thing to do is rewrite the plan yourself

The Norwegian data also records the treatment decisions: among the people suspected of progression at three months who turned out on re-reading to have real progression, only 17 (15.0%) had their treatment changed at the time; median survival was 12.1 months in those changed and 11.8 in those not, no difference to be seen (P=0.838)[3]. The recommendation the authors wrote down is this: where the imaging is inconclusive, complete the course of adjuvant temozolomide. How to read survival numbers is in "That number, and how I am going to tell you about it".

One set of situations does not take this route. One-sided weakness coming on over minutes to hours, speech suddenly slurred or gone, a change in consciousness, one pupil bigger than the other, a sudden severe headache with vomiting, a seizure — that tier means calling an ambulance, not ringing first; one-sided weakness or unsteadiness that has been getting more obvious over the past few days means contacting the team today. Neither tier waits for the date in your diary, and the list is in "When to come back the same day: three tiers".

I cannot give you a tool for reading this at home, because this is not a thing that can be read outside the clinic in the first place. What is left to do is quite plain: keep the appointment as booked, remember the date your treatment finished, and settle two things in the room — how this change was measured against the last one, and whether I am still inside those twelve weeks after radiotherapy. That second one decides whether this scan gets a confirmatory study or a change of treatment.

From the physician

There is no room in this one for reading your own scan, because the whole phenomenon only holds up in hindsight. Keep the appointment on the day it was booked, and settle two things in the room: how this change was measured against the last one, and whether you are still inside those twelve weeks after radiotherapy. Four to eight weeks is a rule for scoring a lesion, not a waiting period you can help yourself to — do not move your appointment.
