AFTER THE DIAGNOSIS

Three breast cancers, three different roads

Subtype decides the order of treatment and how recurrence risk is distributed over time; this also sets out which era of patients drew those much-quoted recurrence curves.

Breast Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 8 references
#Staging#Decisions#Biomarkers

Of the four boxes in SEER, the largest is hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative disease at 72.7%, with triple-negative at 12.2%. What this article is about is not which one is fiercer, but how differently the same "early breast cancer" behaves along the timeline — and why the most quoted curves cannot actually tell you the height.

"It is the same breast cancer, so why does the woman next to me need injections when I only take tablets?" Behind that question in clinic there are really three different roads. Breast cancer is not one disease; it is at least three that behave differently along the timeline: hormone-receptor-positive and HER2-negative, HER2-positive, and triple-negative. This article is about the roads themselves — who travels which one, what the rhythm of each one is, and one thing that is rarely spelled out: which era of patients drew those recurrence-risk curves we keep quoting.

First, count the people in the four boxes

The American SEER programme has recorded HER2 status since 2010. Among patients with known receptor status, hormone-receptor-positive and HER2-negative disease made up 72.7%, triple-negative 12.2%, hormone-receptor-positive and HER2-positive 10.3%, and hormone-receptor-negative and HER2-positive 4.6%; HER2-positive disease came to about 14.9% in total. The same analysis reports that the odds of a high-grade tumour in the other three groups were 6.4 to 20.0 times those in the hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative group[1]. Pause there. An odds ratio puts each group's ratio of happened against not-happened side by side and divides one by the other; it is not a multiple of probability — high grade is not a rare event in breast cancer, so the probabilities cannot really differ twentyfold. I could not obtain the absolute proportion of high-grade tumours in each group, so this figure can only be read as a direction: in the hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative group, the proportion at a lower grade is clearly higher than in the other three.

This is American data. For the distribution of subtypes in Taiwan and survival by subtype here, I could not find official statistics I could cite; if you want local numbers, that is one to confirm with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office, from your hospital's own records or the national registry. How the receptor and subtype terms themselves are read is in "What ER, PR and HER2 are telling you".

The survival figures really do differ — but look at how many years, and which deaths

In the same SEER data, four-year breast-cancer-specific survival for patients diagnosed between 2010 and 2013 and followed to the end of 2014 ran, highest to lowest: hormone-receptor-positive and HER2-negative 92.5%, hormone-receptor-positive and HER2-positive 90.3%, hormone-receptor-negative and HER2-positive 82.7%, triple-negative 77.0%[2]. Breast-cancer-specific survival counts only the proportion dying of breast cancer, not of other causes; and four years is not five, so the numbers are not interchangeable.

One box surprises people: among patients who were stage IV at first diagnosis, survival for hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-positive disease was 45.5%, higher than the 35.9% for hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative disease, which the authors attribute to advances in anti-HER2 treatment[2]. So "HER2-positive is the fierce one" no longer holds in an era where there are drugs for it. What the course of the disease and the goals of treatment look like after metastasis is in "Metastatic disease: what happens next".

What the order of events looks like on each of the three roads

ASCO's 2021 guideline on preoperative treatment sets the triage out concretely. Triple-negative: patients with clinically node-positive disease and/or at least T1c should be offered a regimen containing an anthracycline and a taxane; those with cT1a or cT1bN0 disease should not routinely receive preoperative treatment; carboplatin may be used to raise the pathological complete response rate. Hormone-receptor-positive and HER2-negative: preoperative chemotherapy may be used where a treatment decision can be made without the information surgery would give, and postmenopausal patients may be downstaged with endocrine treatment. HER2-positive: patients who are node-positive or high-risk node-negative should be offered preoperative treatment together with anti-HER2 therapy, and those with T1aN0 and T1bN0 disease should not receive it routinely[3]. The same guideline carries one sentence worth remembering: beyond histological type, grade, stage and ER/PR/HER2, the evidence is insufficient to support using other markers or gene expression profiles for clinical decision-making[3].

For HER2-positive patients with residual invasive disease after preoperative treatment, ASCO's 2021 adjuvant guideline has a recommendation of its own[4] — how that road is ordered is in "HER2-positive: how the drugs are ordered", and the trade-off between operating first and giving chemotherapy first is in "Surgery first, or chemotherapy first?". I am not opening either here.

Wherever an anthracycline, a taxane, carboplatin or trastuzumab appears on the route, a few situations mean phoning the treatment team the same day rather than waiting for the next appointment: a fever or a sudden bout of shivering after chemotherapy; flushing of the face, chest tightness, difficulty breathing or back pain during a taxane infusion; or new breathlessness after a few steps, breathlessness lying flat, or ankle swelling while on trastuzumab. The complete list and its thresholds are in "Which side effects mean phoning the same day".

At year five, two curves cross over

International Breast Cancer Study Group trials I to V followed 4,105 patients with operable breast cancer for a median of 24 years. For the whole group the annual hazard of recurrence was highest in the first five years at 10.4%, with a peak of 15.2% between the first and second years. In those first five years the annual hazard for ER-positive disease was lower than for ER-negative disease (9.9% against 11.5%, P value 0.01); after five years the direction reverses — 5.4% against 3.3% at 5 to 10 years, 2.9% against 1.3% at 10 to 15 years, 2.8% against 1.2% at 15 to 20 years, and only at 20 to 25 years do they level out at 1.3% against 1.4%[5]. An annual hazard of recurrence is simply "out of every hundred people who have not yet recurred, how many recur during this year".

What that changes is not the total quantity of fear but how it is distributed — and it is also why the anti-hormone tablets are taken for so many years. The decision about how long the course runs is in "Five years of endocrine therapy, or ten?".

In triple-negative disease the peak is pressed into the first three years

Among 1,601 patients diagnosed at a Toronto hospital between 1987 and 1997, median follow-up 8.1 years, 180 (11.2%) had triple-negative disease. Triple-negative patients were at higher risk of distant recurrence (hazard ratio 2.6, 95% confidence interval 2.0 to 3.5) and of death (hazard ratio 3.2, 2.3 to 4.5) within five years of diagnosis — a hazard ratio divides the speed at which events occur in one group by the speed in the other, so 2.6 says that over those five years they were happening a great deal faster, not that "the probability becomes 2.6 times"; after five years it was no longer significant. The risk of distant recurrence peaked at roughly the third year and then fell away quickly, while the recurrence risk in the "other" group stayed broadly constant across the whole follow-up[6]. The authors put it as: triple-negative disease follows a more aggressive course, but that adverse effect is temporary. Risk falling after five years does not mean it has gone to zero.

Another cohort of 1,249 patients drew the shapes of the four intrinsic subtypes in more detail: in luminal A the risk rises slowly, reaching its maximum at about three years and then holding steady; in luminal B most recurrences happen within the first five years; HER2-enriched disease peaks at about 20 months after surgery and shows a second peak at month 72; and in the triple-negative group with a high proliferation index there is a sharp peak at about 18 months[7]. That classification uses a Ki-67 threshold of 14% — and interlaboratory variation in Ki-67 is large enough to reclassify the same batch of tumours, for the reasons set out in "What ER, PR and HER2 are telling you".

Which era of patients drew these three curves

This is the point of the article and the sentence most easily skipped. The patients behind those three sets of data were, respectively, randomised between 1978 and 1985[5], diagnosed between 1987 and 1997[6], and a single series of 1,249 people[7]. In that era most had no anti-HER2 targeted treatment, and adjuvant chemotherapy and endocrine therapy did not contain what they contain today. I could not find corresponding data from the modern treatment era.

So what these curves can tell you is the shape — which subtype has its risk pressed to the front, which one stretches out. What they cannot tell you is the height, meaning your own probability of recurrence. Fitting a height from the 1980s onto yourself in 2026 is the self-sentencing I least want to see in clinic.

Hormone-receptor-positive disease: the fifteen years after year five

Here the data are more recent. The EBCTCG pooled 88 trials and 62,923 women who were ER-positive, had completed five years of endocrine therapy and were disease-free at year five: between years 5 and 20 recurrences went on happening at a steady rate, and each woman's absolute risk was separated by her original tumour size and node count — the lowest box (T1N0) 13%, the highest (T2 with 4 to 9 positive nodes) 41%[8]. The same analysis found that, once T and N were given, grade and Ki-67 had only moderate independent predictive value, and PR status and HER2 status had none[8]. The full six-box breakdown is in "Five years of endocrine therapy, or ten?", because that is precisely the table behind the five-or-ten decision.

13% and 41% differ threefold, and both women can be written up as "hormone-receptor-positive, early". Subtype decides the road; what decides the height of the risk is how big that tumour was and what the axilla looked like at the start.

Ask which road you are on, and which year of it you are in

At the next visit, these three can be said out loud:

  • "With my combination of ER, PR and HER2, which group do you put me in? What is the standard order of treatment for that group?"

  • "What are my T and N? In this combination, is my risk pressed into the early years, or is it the drawn-out kind?"

  • "On this road, what happens over the next three months, the next year, the next five years? After which stretch will the way you follow me up change?"

Write the answer to the third one down. Knowing which years your risk is distributed across is far more use than remembering a survival rate — it decides which year you tighten up the follow-up and which year you can put your life back in the diary.

From the physician

Subtype describes the road; what decides the height of the risk is how big that tumour was and what the axilla looked like at the start, and two people within the same subtype can differ threefold. At the next visit, get the question "are my risk years the early ones?" said out loud, and take home what is planned for the next three months, the next year and the next five years.
