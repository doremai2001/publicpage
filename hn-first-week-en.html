AFTER THE DIAGNOSIS

What happens in the month after diagnosis

What the weeks between the biopsy report and the first treatment are for, why starting tomorrow would cost you, and what you can decide yourself while you wait.

H&N Guide · After the Diagnosis · 10 references
#Getting Ready#Treatment Pathway#Tumour Board#Decisions#Quitting

I have laid this month out as a timetable so you can see where you are in it: which steps genuinely cannot be skipped, what the multidisciplinary meeting does with your case, and the one place where a phone call from you moves things along.

In the first month after a head and neck cancer diagnosis, the hard part is not the treatment. It is the waiting. An MRI, a PET, an endoscopy, a dental appointment, a team meeting — each a few days, while the tumour sits where you can touch it every morning. The sentence I hear most: "If we keep testing like this, will it be too late?"

Our patients are not old. In the 2023 national cancer registry, oral cavity cancer (including oropharynx and hypopharynx) ranked sixth overall and fourth by age-standardised incidence in men, with a median age at diagnosis of 60 — five years younger than the 65 for cancer overall[1]. The man opposite me is usually still working and supporting a household; every day costs him something.

These weeks are not a delay — they decide which treatment you get

Treatment depends almost entirely on the subsite, the depth, the nodes and whether it has travelled further. Two people both told they have "oral cavity cancer" may end up with surgery alone, surgery plus post-operative radiotherapy, or concurrent chemoradiation. Begin before those are answered and you lose not a few days but the whole course.

Does waiting carry a cost? It does, and Taiwan has its own data. Among 18,677 first-primary oral cavity squamous cell carcinoma patients in the Taiwan Cancer Registry, 2004 to 2010, the diagnosis-to-treatment interval was independently associated with five-year overall survival. Here, 57% started within 20 days, 34% at 21 to 45 days, 6% at 46 to 90 and 3% beyond 91; the authors concluded an interval beyond 30 days, and possibly beyond 20, may worsen survival[2].

The US National Cancer Data Base, 51,655 head and neck squamous cell carcinoma patients, points the same way: starting 61 to 90 days after diagnosis carried a hazard ratio for death of 1.13 (95% CI 1.08–1.19) against within 30 days. The two statistical breakpoints fell at 46 to 52 and 62 to 67 days, with median overall survival across the three bands 71.9, 61.0 and 46.6 months. In 2011 a quarter waited over 46 days to begin[3].

Now the honest part. Both are retrospective registry studies, and patients with more complicated disease take longer to work up and do worse anyway — confounding nobody has cleanly resolved. So the correct sentence is "a longer interval is associated with worse survival", not "waiting kills people". And 1.13 is a population hazard ratio, not "wait two months and you are 13% more likely to die."

In Taiwan the tumour board is not a courtesy, it is an accreditation requirement

Most patients do not know their case goes before a room full of doctors. The Ministry of Health and Welfare's (衛生福利部) cancer care quality accreditation standards make it a hard rule: under 50 new cancers a year, the multidisciplinary team must meet monthly; 50 to 499 cases, twice monthly; 500 or more, weekly. Per cancer type, at least 20% of new patients must reach the meeting and at least 15% must be discussed before treatment starts. Case manager (個管師) staffing is set too — one per 350 new patients, 50 hours of training in year one[4].

The meeting changes things. A study prospectively recording 120 consecutive head and neck tumour board cases found roughly 27% came out with a change in diagnosis, stage or plan; the plan changed in 24% of malignancies against 6% of benign tumours (p=0.0199), predominantly escalations to multimodality treatment (p=0.0084)[5]. One plan in four gets rewritten. That is why I ask patients not to resent its pace.

A 224-patient before-and-after series found five-year disease-specific survival rise from 52% to 75% (p=.003) after the tumour board began, matched hazard ratio for death 0.48[6]. Too good to take at face value, and the authors say why: the hospital hired a fellowship-trained head and neck surgeon over the same period. The design cannot separate the two. I include it because it points the same way, not because it proves anything.

Roughly the order it runs in

Details differ by hospital, the skeleton is similar. In the head and neck cancer guideline of the Taipei Medical University healthcare system (臺北醫學大學醫療體系), which includes Wan Fang Hospital (萬芳醫院), head and neck MRI or CT is mandatory, PET selective for stage III and IV, and dental consultation before radiotherapy mandatory[7].

  • Biopsy and pathology — cell type, differentiation. Oropharyngeal cancer adds p16.

  • Staging imaging: head and neck MRI or CT first, for local extent; PET for distant disease and a second primary.

  • Endoscopy, or examination under general anaesthesia, for mucosal surfaces imaging cannot show.

  • Dental consultation, nutrition, blood tests, cardiac and pulmonary function — these run alongside the imaging, not behind it.

  • The multidisciplinary team meeting, and only then the plan and the consent form.

Taiwanese centres generally build their protocols on the NCCN head and neck cancer guidelines, which handle oral cavity, oropharynx, hypopharynx, nasopharynx and larynx separately and are revised several times a year[8]. Which standard your hospital follows is a fair question for your case manager (個管師).

The one thing in these weeks you can genuinely do yourself

You cannot control the scheduling. You can control this. A meta-analysis of 24 studies and 6,332 patients found that people who smoked through radiotherapy had 1.85 times the risk of death of those who did not (95% CI 1.55–2.21), and 2.24 times the risk of locoregional failure (95% CI 1.42–3.52)[9]. This is one of the least contested numbers I have, and note what it measures — smoking during treatment, not a lifetime of it. Stopping now still counts.

For betel quid and alcohol I have no meta-analysis of the same quality, so I will not pretend otherwise. But it is the same mucosa, the same area about to take a high dose, and betel quid is the principal cause of this disease in Taiwan. These waiting weeks are the window in which levels fall to zero and the mucosa recovers a little, before side effects wear you down. Start two weeks later and it is harder.

Two opposite misreadings, and I have met both

The first: "the sooner the better — whichever hospital can operate next week is where I go." This one worries me more. That 27% rate of plan changes happened because full information was on the table[5]. Starting fast on incomplete staging can just mean starting fast down a road you have to walk back. Aim at the necessary work done efficiently, not begun early.

The second: "it has already been two weeks — is it too late?" No. The signal appears past 30 to 46 days, not at day 10[2][3]. These are population averages read backwards, not a verdict for one person. The time gone you cannot change; the scheduling from here we can work on together.

Once treatment starts, time starts meaning something else

Once the course begins, what needs protecting is continuity. The literature is consistent: stretch out the overall treatment time of radiotherapy and both local control and survival suffer[10]. Weddings, Lunar New Year, family obligations — move them if you can. If you truly must be away, tell the radiation oncology team so we can make the time up; never skip sessions on your own. I say this in the dental piece too, because the commonest reason patients postpone themselves is wanting the teeth sorted out first.

Three questions to ask out loud at your next visit, and the rest of this series

First: which staging investigations am I missing, and what day will they be in? Second: which multidisciplinary meeting will my case go to, and when will I know the outcome? Third: if I want to stop smoking and betel quid, does the hospital run a cessation clinic, and can you refer me today?

Several other pieces follow this one. One takes the tests: what each of MRI, CT, PET, endoscopy and biopsy answers, why a clean-looking PET does not mean the neck is clear, and what the National Health Insurance (健保) covers PET for. One is on why dental review before radiotherapy is not optional, and why a tooth pulled years later is high-risk. A third is on what HPV p16 and EBV DNA really tell you — in particular why, in Taiwan, p16-positive does not mean the good kind. Read whichever is on your mind; no need to go in order.

From the physician

This is a timetable, not a verdict. The days already spent waiting cannot be changed, but what is still ahead can usually be rearranged. If your tests look too spread out, say so to your case manager (個管師); chasing appointments is their job, not an imposition on them.
