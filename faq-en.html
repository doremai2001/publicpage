Before you start

I can look all this up online — why come to clinic?

What you can find online is the general case; what you cannot find is your case. For the same cancer, the stage, the location, the pathology, what treatment you have already had and your overall condition all change the recommendation — and those are exactly the differences a search engine cannot weigh for you.

In clinic I make a point of explaining things slowly: why I am recommending this approach, what the alternatives are, what each one costs you, and what happens if you do nothing. Patients often tell me that the biggest difference after a consultation is not the new information, but finally knowing which information does not apply to them — the dozens of hours of searching and hundreds of contradictory claims can be set down.

Write your questions on a piece of paper and bring them; we will go through them one by one.

How do I choose a doctor?

I cannot choose for you, but here are some criteria — and they apply to assessing me as well:

• Are they still seeing patients, still treating? Someone who has been away from the front line for a long time tends to describe the field as it was in the year they left.
• Does what they say track the evidence of the last few years? Guidelines in radiation oncology move quickly; a standard answer from five years ago may no longer be the answer.
• Will they tell you "this is not right for you"? An explanation that only recommends and never excludes deserves a second thought.
• Are the alternatives and their costs spelled out? An explanation that lists only benefits is incomplete.
• Can you follow it? An explanation you do not understand cannot be used to make a decision.

Not every item has to score full marks. But if none of them fit, it is worth getting another opinion — in cancer care a second opinion is normal, and appropriate.

Am I suitable for proton therapy or hyperthermia? How is that decided?

This cannot be answered on a web page, and I would not trust any answer given before someone has seen your records. The assessment looks at the cancer type and stage, the tumour location and which critical organs sit beside it, what treatment you have already had (particularly any previous radiotherapy), your general condition and comorbidities, the pathology and imaging detail, and whether the tumour can be heated effectively at its depth.

In practice, after reviewing your records in clinic the case is taken to a multidisciplinary team meeting, and we then come back to you with the workable options and their trade-offs. Reimbursement and out-of-pocket scope follow hospital announcements and the assessment at the time.

I have had radiotherapy before — can I have it again?

Possibly, but it needs very careful assessment. What matters is the cumulative dose to normal tissue, the interval since the last course, and how much the tissue has recovered — the tolerance of the spinal cord, optic nerves and carotid arteries sets the ceiling on whether re-treatment is possible.

Recurrent or residual tumour is often the population of cells that survived the last course: hypoxic, good at repair, relatively radioresistant. That is exactly where hyperthermia acts — it targets hypoxia and repair, the two causes of resistance, rather than simply adding more dose. Protons then help to spare normal tissue that has already received its share. In the re-treatment setting, both tools carry more weight than they do first time round.

Whether it is feasible depends on your previous treatment plan and dose records. Please bring the earlier radiotherapy records, dose reports and imaging — without them the assessment cannot be made.

What does a full course of radiotherapy involve?

Radiotherapy does not start the day after the decision is made. Between the clinic visit and your first session on the treatment couch there is usually another one to two weeks. That wait causes a lot of anxiety — people assume they are queuing. What is actually happening in that time is that a treatment plan is being built for you alone.

What happens during preparation. On the simulation day an immobilisation device is made — for head and neck treatment that is a thermoplastic mask moulded to your face, neck and shoulders, so that your position is identical every day. A planning CT scan follows, and set-up marks are placed on the skin. The physician then outlines the tumour and the organs to be avoided, slice by slice, the physicist and dosimetrist design the dose distribution, and the plan is physically measured and verified before it is used. The work done in those few days is what determines how precise the whole course will be.

What treatment is like. The first session takes longer because the position is verified with imaging. After that you are on the machine for roughly 10 to 20 minutes a day, of which only a few minutes is actual irradiation — the rest is positioning and image matching. The time spent at the hospital is therefore longer than the treatment time, so factor in travel and waiting. Monday to Friday with the weekend off is deliberate: it gives normal tissue time to repair.

There is a review once a week. This is not a formality. It is where skin, mucosa, weight and blood counts are checked and supportive treatment adjusted. If something is uncomfortable, do not wait for the next appointment — tell the radiation therapists or call us.

Finishing is not the same as recovering. Acute side effects usually continue for another one to two weeks after the course ends before they start to improve. That is expected and does not mean the treatment failed. After that you move into regular imaging and clinic follow-up, at intervals set by the cancer type and the aim of treatment.

The exact workflow and timelines vary by hospital, cancer type and technique; your own treatment team's explanation takes precedence.
What should I bring to my first visit?

The more complete it is, the more specific the first conversation can be:

• NHI card, identification, referral letter if you have one
• Pathology report (the formal report from biopsy or surgery)
• Imaging discs: CT, MRI, PET, with the reports
• Discharge or operative summaries
• A list of current medications, including supplements and herbal medicine
• If you have had radiotherapy before: the previous treatment plan and dose report
• Tell us if you have a pacemaker, metal implants or any electronic device — this bears directly on hyperthermia safety

Bringing a family member helps; two people between them remember more of the conversation.

During treatment

Does radiotherapy hurt?

There is no sensation during the treatment itself; it is like having an X-ray, lying still while the machine moves around you. A single session usually takes a little over ten minutes. What does need attention is the skin reaction or mucosal discomfort that can appear in the later part of the course, and we manage those together.

Do I need to be admitted? How long does it take?

Most courses are outpatient — you come in once a day, and go home afterwards, usually Monday to Friday over several weeks. The actual number of sessions depends on the cancer type and the aim of treatment.

Can I work during treatment? Can I drive myself?

Most people can. Radiotherapy itself does not take away your ability to drive or work; you can leave on your own afterwards and do not need someone to collect you (unless you are on sedation or strong analgesia). The more common practical problem is fatigue in the second half of the course, and oral mucosal discomfort towards the end for head and neck treatment, at which point energy levels and eating affect how well you can work.

A workable approach: fix the treatment at the same time each day (first thing in the morning, or before you leave work), so both the job and the course are easier to plan; and build in flexibility for reduced hours or leave later in the course. If your work is physically demanding or involves a lot of speaking, it is worth discussing adjustments with your employer early.

What should I eat during treatment? Any foods to avoid, or supplements?

The first priority is eating enough — calories and protein. Losing too much weight during head and neck treatment affects set-up accuracy and can force an interruption, which matters more than what you eat. The folk advice about avoiding chicken, duck or seafood has no evidence behind it, and there is no reason to cut out sources of nutrition because of it. What should genuinely be avoided during the course is tobacco and alcohol.

One thing does need care: high-dose antioxidant supplements. In a randomised trial in head and neck cancer patients, supplementation with high-dose vitamin E and beta-carotene during radiotherapy was associated with a higher rate of local recurrence (Bairati et al., J Clin Oncol 2005). Part of how radiotherapy kills cancer cells is through oxidative reactions, and extra high-dose antioxidants may interfere with that mechanism. The same applies to herbal medicines and health supplements — not all are prohibited, but bring the whole bag to clinic so we can look through it together.

Should I take L-glutamine?

Glutamine is an amino acid, and it circulates widely among patients on radiotherapy and chemotherapy — barely a week goes by without someone bringing a tub of it to clinic. It is not a drug but a self-funded nutritional supplement, not covered by Taiwan's NHI, and the cost over a full course is not trivial — which is a good reason to look at the evidence before deciding.

The most complete evidence comes from a 2021 meta-analysis in Head & Neck covering 11 randomised controlled trials and 922 head and neck cancer patients. The result has two halves, and both matter.

In other words: it does not prevent mucositis, but it can make it less severe. And less opioid analgesia, fewer feeding tubes and fewer treatment interruptions are differences patients actually feel — not just numbers on paper.

Two boundaries worth stating. First, this evidence is about oral mucositis, not skin. Taking it as a skin-care supplement has no basis. Second, oral and intravenous are not the same thing — the MASCC/ISOO mucositis guidelines explicitly recommend against intravenous glutamine for preventing mucositis in stem cell transplant patients, and the two are frequently conflated.

In practice. The dose used in trials is around 30 g a day in three divided doses, swished and swallowed, usually started around the beginning of the course. Oral use is generally well tolerated, but impaired liver or kidney function needs assessment first. Whether to take it, when to start and how long to continue should be discussed with me or a dietitian in clinic — bring the whole bag of whatever else you are taking, it is the quickest way.

One last point: this is about reducing severity, not avoiding the problem. Taking it does not replace oral hygiene, adequate nutrition and calories, or coming back so we can look at the mucosa.

Based on: Alsubaie et al., meta-analysis of glutamine for radiation-induced oral mucositis (Head & Neck, 2021), MASCC/ISOO clinical practice guidelines for mucositis.
How should I look after the skin in the treatment area?

First, the thing most people get wrong: you can wash it. "Keep the treated area away from water" is a long-standing myth. A meta-analysis of randomised trials found that patients who washed normally during treatment had significantly less severe dermatitis (odds ratio 0.32) and less moist desquamation (0.25). Use warm water and a mild, fragrance-free cleanser, wash gently, and pat dry with a towel — no rubbing, no exfoliating.

Moisturise daily. Use a fragrance-free lotion or cream, once or twice a day. No single product has been shown to be clearly better than the others; the best one is the one that feels comfortable, does not sting, and that you will actually use every day. You do not need to skip it before treatment — but do not apply a thick layer just before walking into the treatment room either.

Deodorant is allowed. Breast cancer patients are often told to avoid antiperspirant or deodorant in the axilla during treatment. In a randomised trial, ordinary deodorant use did not increase skin toxicity.

What to avoid: scratching, rubbing, exfoliating; products containing alcohol or fragrance; adhesive tape on the treated area; temperature extremes (heat packs, ice packs, electric blankets, saunas, hot springs); tight or rough clothing — loose cotton is best. Protect the treated area from the sun, during the course and after it ends: irradiated skin is more sensitive to ultraviolet light, and that does not stop when treatment does.

For head and neck treatment: switch to an electric shaver rather than a blade; avoid a collar rubbing repeatedly against the neck; the skin behind the ears and in the neck folds breaks down most easily, so dry those areas properly. Do not deliberately scrub off the set-up marks drawn by the team — if they fade, tell us so they can be redrawn.

A cool compress helps with burning and itching. Soak a clean towel in room-temperature or slightly cool water (no ice cubes, no ice packs), wring it out so it does not drip, and rest it gently on the treated area for 10 to 15 minutes; this can be repeated several times a day. Pat dry afterwards and do not leave the skin damp for long. It relieves symptoms; it does not change the course of the dermatitis and does not replace moisturising.

There are evidence-based preventive measures, but every one of them should be discussed with your own physician or treatment team before use. In the 2023 MASCC guideline, the preventive interventions that reached consensus included Mepitel film, Hydrofilm dressing, topical corticosteroids (mometasone, betamethasone) and photobiomodulation therapy.

Why the discussion matters: these are interventions, not skincare products. A dressing placed in the beam path changes the surface dose distribution; topical corticosteroids have limits on timing, site and duration of use, and broken skin is managed differently; and whether any of them suits you depends on your treatment site, dose and current skin condition. Please do not buy and apply them yourself, and do not copy someone else's regimen. If they are appropriate, we will prescribe them after assessment in clinic, or the treatment team will apply them for you.

If the skin breaks down and weeps (moist desquamation), let the treatment team see it promptly; it is usually manageable with dressings. Do not apply over-the-counter remedies, folk treatments or ointments of unknown origin.

Based on: systematic review and meta-analysis of skin hygiene and washing (Supportive Care in Cancer, 2023), MASCC clinical practice guidelines for acute radiation dermatitis (2023), randomised non-inferiority trial of axillary deodorant use (IJROBP, 2009).
Will I lose my hair?

Radiotherapy affects only the area being irradiated; it is not systemic the way chemotherapy is. So unless the scalp is in the treatment field, you will not lose hair all over.

After treatment, will I be radioactive around my family and children?

No. Standard radiotherapy — including proton therapy — is external beam: the radiation is produced by the machine, and when the machine is off there is none. Nothing radioactive remains in your body. During the course you can live normally with your family, eat at the same table, hold children and share a bed. No isolation is needed, and there is no need for separate utensils.

A different category of treatment does require care: radioactive iodine for thyroid cancer, for example, and certain brachytherapy procedures where a source is placed inside the body. Those have their own precautions, and if your course includes them the team will explain them to you individually.

Keloids & skin lesions (SRT)

Will my skin change colour after SRT?

Pigment change is the most common reaction. In a study following 96 keloid lesions, around six in ten developed some degree of pigment change (mostly darkening), around two in ten developed radiation dermatitis, and a small number had temporary itching or pain.

Most pigment change fades over time, but in darker skin it tends to be more noticeable and more persistent. This is a trade-off to weigh before treatment, particularly when the lesion is somewhere visible.

Is there a radiation risk with SRT? Could it cause cancer?

SRT uses low energy, the field is confined to the lesion itself, and the depth is only a few millimetres. The risk of a second malignancy in adults treated this way is considered very low — at the level of isolated case reports in the literature.

That does not make it the same for everyone. Children and adolescents, pregnancy, and lesions near the thyroid or breast require more careful individual assessment, and other approaches may be recommended instead. This is always discussed before treatment.

Which department should I see about a keloid?

You can come directly to my clinic. Bring the lesion (or a clear photograph) and any records of previous treatment; there is no need to make a round of appointments first.

If excision plus post-operative irradiation is appropriate, I will arrange the surgical referral and align the irradiation slot with the operating date — that coordination is what determines the outcome, and it is not something you should have to run between two departments to arrange.

If you already have surgery scheduled, let me know the date as early as possible, so that irradiation within 24 hours of surgery can be arranged. Once the operation is done, that window has usually closed.

This is general patient education and cannot replace a face-to-face consultation. Every diagnosis, stage and general condition differs; discuss your actual treatment with your own physician.
