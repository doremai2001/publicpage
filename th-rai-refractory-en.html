FOLLOW-UP AND BEYOND

After radioiodine stops working

"Refractory" is a set of criteria that carries no grade of evidence anywhere in the guideline; and those two words do not mean short of time — for many people the next step is to watch.

Thyroid Cancer Guide · Follow-up and beyond · 14 references
#Radioiodine#Targeted Therapy#Clinical Trials#NHI Coverage

What these drugs buy is time with the disease held down, not a cure — no trial has shown a survival benefit, and none was designed to look for one. When to start is a decision you are entitled to take part in.

"Radioiodine doesn't work for you any more." It lands like a sentence being passed. It is actually a set of criteria, written down — and the awkward part is that those criteria carry no grade of evidence anywhere in the guideline.

This article covers differentiated thyroid cancer only: papillary, follicular and oncocytic (Hürthle cell) carcinoma. Medullary thyroid carcinoma is a different disease with a different body of trials, in 〈Medullary cancer is a different disease〉; anaplastic thyroid carcinoma is in 〈Anaplastic cancer is an emergency〉; a number that moves while imaging finds nothing is in 〈The number rises and nothing shows〉.

How "refractory" gets decided

The 2025 American Thyroid Association guideline starts by laying down two thresholds: nobody who has never had an ablative or treatment dose of radioiodine (I-131) can be diagnosed as refractory, and anyone who meets the criteria for radioiodine should have it in order to find out; and anyone already judged refractory should not be given further empiric radioiodine, but considered for other treatment. Both of those are listed as good practice, which the guideline does not grade[1].

The criteria themselves sit in the discussion beneath those two clauses, likewise with no strength of recommendation attached. Two strong criteria: disease visible on structural imaging or PET with no iodine uptake on the post-therapy scan; or progression less than six months after an appropriately dosed treatment that did show uptake on the post-therapy scan. Then two supplemental ones: no uptake on a diagnostic scan while disease is otherwise detectable (which predicts a poorer response, though some of these patients still have a positive post-therapy scan); and uptake in some but not all tumour foci, which does not rule radioiodine out but does mean it is not sufficient on its own[1]. The guideline says of itself that these criteria will keep changing.

Refractory does not mean short of time

This is where the misunderstanding runs deepest. In the same section the guideline writes, word for word, that while some of these patients die within three to five years, there are also long-term survivors with stable or very slowly progressive disease[1].

Which is why the first move after "refractory" is often not a drug at all. The guideline is explicit: patients who are asymptomatic, stable or minimally progressive, or who carry clinically significant comorbidities, can be monitored on TSH-suppressive thyroid hormone therapy with serial imaging every three to twelve months (Conditional recommendation, low certainty evidence)[1].

The threshold for starting a drug is imaging, not a blood test

What the guideline requires before systemic therapy is disease visible on imaging that is progressing, not a blood curve climbing. A rising thyroglobulin on its own, it says in as many words, does not constitute a reason to start systemic treatment[1]. This is not caution for its own sake. The trials below enrolled people whose imaging was worsening; put a different patient inside that conclusion and you get none of the result and all of the side effects.

The two first-line drugs

One term first: progression-free survival is the time from randomisation until the disease worsens or the patient dies. It is not lifespan.

The lenvatinib phase 3 enrolled 392 patients with radioiodine-refractory, progressive differentiated thyroid cancer, 261 on drug and 131 on placebo. Median progression-free survival was 18.3 against 3.6 months, hazard ratio 0.21 — and that interval is a 99% confidence interval, 0.14 to 0.31, not a 95% one; response rate 64.8% (4 of them complete responses) against 1.5%. The cost: the abstract records discontinuation for adverse events in 14.2% against 2.3%, and among 20 deaths during treatment 6 were judged drug-related; hypertension, diarrhoea, fatigue, loss of appetite and weight loss all ran above four in ten[2].

The sorafenib phase 3 enrolled 417 people (207 on drug, 210 on placebo) with the same kind of disease: radioiodine-refractory, locally advanced or metastatic, progressing differentiated thyroid cancer. Median progression-free survival 10.8 against 5.8 months, hazard ratio 0.59 (95% confidence interval 0.45 to 0.76). Response rate 12.2% (24/196), all partial. In the treated arm, doses were interrupted in 66.2%, reduced in 64.3%, and stopped for adverse events in 18.8%; serious adverse events 37.2% against 26.3%[3]. One practical thing as well: in a third of patients (69/207) TSH rose by more than 0.5 mIU/L while on the drug, so the levothyroxine dose has to be chased[3].

The guideline's choice between them: for progressive patients with no matching target, one of these two, and in most situations lenvatinib is preferred (Strong recommendation, high certainty evidence) — while conceding, in its own words, that no randomised trial has ever compared the two head to head[1]. There are clauses about timing too: in symptomatic patients for whom surgery or radiotherapy is not appropriate, do not delay (Strong recommendation, moderate certainty evidence); in asymptomatic patients who have progressed over the previous twelve to fourteen months, it depends on whether you put efficacy or quality of life first — which the guideline does not grade, listing it as good practice.

Second line

Cabozantinib is for people who have progressed on the drugs above, and the guideline gives it a Strong recommendation with high certainty evidence[1]. Its phase 3 exists in two versions of the numbers. In the interim analysis (187 patients) the response rate among the first 100 randomised was 10/67 (15%, 99% confidence interval 5.8 to 29.3) against 0/33, short of the prespecified threshold[4]. The 2022 extended analysis is the one to use now: 258 randomised, median follow-up 10.1 months, median progression-free survival 11.0 months (96% confidence interval 7.4 to 13.8) against 1.9 months, hazard ratio 0.22 (0.15 to 0.32, 96% again); response rate 11.0% (95% confidence interval 6.9 to 16.9) against 0; grade 3 or 4 adverse events 62% against 28%[5].

The drugs chosen by gene work well, on very few people

The guideline asks for tissue-based biomarker testing before systemic therapy is started, to look for an alteration a drug can be aimed at (Strong recommendation, moderate certainty evidence)[1]. Three directions exist:

RET fusions. Strong recommendation, moderate certainty evidence — resting on 19 previously treated patients with RET fusion-positive thyroid cancer, response rate 79% (95% confidence interval 54 to 94)[6]; the matching group for a second drug of the same class had just 9 patients with measurable disease, 8 of whom responded (89%, 95% confidence interval 52 to 100)[7]. Both single-arm, neither randomised.

NTRK fusions. Strong recommendation, moderate certainty evidence again. The pooled thyroid analysis of larotrectinib covered 29 patients (20 papillary, 2 follicular, 7 anaplastic); among 28 evaluable, the overall response rate was 71% (95% confidence interval 51 to 87), and 86% (64 to 97) for papillary plus follicular[8].

BRAF V600E mutations. Three clauses here: first line only in people who are poor candidates for lenvatinib (Conditional recommendation, moderate certainty evidence); for those who have progressed on one or more prior multikinase inhibitors, Strong recommendation with moderate certainty evidence; non-V600 alterations, not recommended[1]. The phase 3 was still running when the guideline was written, and has since read out: 153 previously treated patients with BRAF V600E-positive, radioiodine-refractory differentiated thyroid cancer, randomised 101 against 52, median progression-free survival 12.8 months (95% confidence interval 10.2 to 21.2) against 3.7 months, stratified hazard ratio 0.38 (0.25 to 0.57); response rate 57% against 4%[9]. The authors' conclusion is written for second-line use. 86% of participants were Asian; that is the ethnic composition of the trial, not a statement about who it suits.

Redifferentiation is still at the trial stage

The idea behind redifferentiation is to use a drug to call the cancer cells back into taking up iodine, then give radioiodine again. The International Thyroid Oncology Group's 2025 statement is candid about where this stands: restoration of iodine uptake runs from 33% to 95% across trials and tumour response rates from 11% to 80%. The sentence that matters most: "Randomised studies are missing"[10].

The only completed phase 3 was not even asking about salvage. It enrolled high-risk postoperative differentiated thyroid cancer, not selected by gene, and asked whether adding a drug to adjuvant radioiodine helped: 233 randomised, complete remission at eighteen months 40% against 38%, odds ratio 1.07 (95% confidence interval 0.61 to 1.87) — negative[11]. So the guideline writes that redifferentiation in adjuvant radioiodine for high-risk, non-gene-selected patients is not recommended (Strong recommendation, moderate certainty evidence).

In the salvage setting, this is the scale of things. A phase 2 aimed at BRAF mutations took 24 patients, 21 assessable: partial response 38%[12]. A British phase 2 closed early because iodine uptake was only restored in about four in ten and accrual was too slow; the denominator of its primary endpoint was 9 people[13]. There is a safety item too: in the earliest 20-patient study, one person was diagnosed with myelodysplastic syndrome fifty-one weeks after the radioiodine, which later progressed to acute leukaemia[14]. The guideline's position: it may be considered in progressive patients carrying a targetable mutation, and trial participation is encouraged (Conditional recommendation, low certainty evidence).

What to ask before starting a drug

What these drugs buy is time with the disease held down. Whether they lengthen life is a question none of the four trials above was built to answer: overall survival was a secondary or exploratory endpoint in every one, and every one let people on placebo move to the active drug once their disease progressed — 71.4% of them did in the sorafenib trial, whose authors write that the survival result may be confounded by exactly that. One at a time, then: sorafenib, no significant difference, hazard ratio 0.80 (0.54 to 1.19)[3]; cabozantinib, a hazard ratio of 0.76 (0.45 to 1.31), also not significant, with the authors writing that crossover will confound future analyses of overall survival[5]; dabrafenib plus trametinib, an interim analysis short of significance, hazard ratio 0.66 (0.36 to 1.19), in a trial still running[9]; lenvatinib, no overall-survival hazard ratio or test published at all — only that median survival had not been reached in either arm[2]. No survival benefit has been shown. That is not the same sentence as "there is none", and I am not going to write the second one. Holding that in mind, work through these five:

  • "I have been judged radioiodine-refractory — under which criterion?"
  • "Is my disease actually growing? How much has it grown since last time?"
  • "Has my tumour tissue been tested? Is there an alteration a drug can be aimed at?"
  • "Can we still just watch? How often would we image, and what would move us to a drug?"
  • "If I start, what are we expecting to get? How often do we assess? How bad do side effects have to be before we reduce the dose?"

And get this last one said out loud: "If three months on the drug has not held the disease down, what is our next step? I would rather have that agreed now — it makes the decision to start an easier one to make."

From the physician

Read these out at your next appointment: "Under which criterion was I judged radioiodine-refractory?" and "Has my tumour tissue been tested, and is there an alteration a drug can be aimed at?" and "Can we still just watch — how often would we image, and what would move us to a drug?"
