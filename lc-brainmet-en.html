SIDE EFFECTS, EMERGENCIES AND FOLLOW-UP

Brain metastases are not the end

What radiosurgery and whole-brain radiotherapy each cost you, and why that scan changes which reimbursed first-line drug you qualify for.

Lung Cancer Guide · Side Effects, Emergencies and Follow-up · 14 references
#Lung#Brain Metastasis#SBRT / SRS#Targeted Therapy#Quality of Life

Two wrong readings are about equally common in my clinic, and this piece is built to block both. The cognitive cost of whole-brain radiotherapy stays in, with its numbers. So does the fact that a brain scan can change which drug the NHI will pay for.

"It has gone to the brain" stops the conversation for three seconds, every time. This article has to block two readings at once: "that means the end, there is no point treating" and "there are new drugs now, the brain is nothing to worry about". I will put three sets of numbers side by side — how common it is, what treatment can do, and what whole-brain radiotherapy really costs. I will not skip that last one to make you feel better.

Common enough that it should not be read as a sentence

A meta-analysis searched to February 2024 included 120 studies, of which 94 (17,458 patients) carried prevalence data: pooled prevalence of brain metastases at diagnosis in non-small cell lung cancer was 28.8% (95% confidence interval 26.3–31.3%), with 31.6% in ALK-positive and 29.4% in EGFR-positive disease[1]. A high proportion of adenocarcinoma in Taiwan carries an EGFR mutation — that belongs to "I never smoked. Why me?" — so this is not rare here. But I will not multiply Taiwan's mutation rate by that figure to produce a "local percentage"; that number would be invented. In the same analysis, the lower bounds of the annual incidence intervals for most driver gene subgroups touch zero, so those subgroup figures cannot be ranked against each other[1].

One more thing now: the review literature states the brain is often the first site of progression in patients on tyrosine kinase inhibitors[2]. A targeted drug working well does not mean the brain needs no surveillance.

Adding whole-brain radiotherapy buys control inside the skull and is paid for in cognition

These numbers have to be written down. A randomised trial across 34 North American institutions enrolled 213 patients with 1 to 3 brain metastases (111 radiosurgery alone, 102 with whole-brain radiotherapy added), primary endpoint cognitive deterioration at 3 months: 40 of 63 (63.5%) with radiosurgery alone, 44 of 48 (91.7%) with whole-brain radiotherapy added, a difference of −28.2 percentage points (90% confidence interval −41.9 to −14.4), P<.001; quality of life also favoured radiosurgery alone; overall survival did not differ, median 10.4 against 7.4 months (hazard ratio 1.02, 0.75–1.38)[3].

What is bought is control inside the skull: without whole-brain radiotherapy, the hazard ratio for new intracranial lesions appearing was 3.6 (2.2–5.9)[3]. A hazard ratio compares how fast events occur in two groups over the same period; it is not "the probability becomes 3.6 times larger". It says new lesions arrive sooner, not that 3.6 in ten people will grow one. Note the denominators, 63 of 111 and 48 of 102: the primary endpoint counted only those who completed testing at baseline and three months. An earlier, smaller randomised trial stopped at interim analysis pointed the same way.

Where whole-brain radiotherapy cannot be avoided, the harm can be reduced. NRG Oncology CC001 (518 patients) compared whole-brain radiotherapy plus memantine with and without hippocampal avoidance: the risk of cognitive failure was significantly lower with avoidance (adjusted hazard ratio 0.74, 0.58–0.95), with no difference in survival or toxicity[4]; the final report agreed, with a lighter patient-reported symptom burden too[5]. But what it compared was hippocampal avoidance against no avoidance, not whole-brain radiotherapy against none, so it cannot erase 63.5% against 91.7%. On the Taiwan side I have to be straight: I did not find the reimbursement clauses for memantine in this setting, nor a separate payment item for hippocampal-avoidance whole-brain radiotherapy, so I will not write "it is covered" and will not write "you have to pay". Ask the radiation oncologist directly: can it be done here, and how is it costed.

The other end needs writing too. QUARTZ enrolled 538 patients suitable for neither surgery nor stereotactic radiotherapy and compared optimal supportive care with and without whole-brain radiotherapy: quality-adjusted life years 46.4 days against 41.7, no difference in overall survival, and the authors concluded whole-brain radiotherapy gave this group "little additional clinically significant benefit"[6]. That population's eligibility travels with that sentence; it does not say whole-brain radiotherapy is useless for everyone.

The 2022 ASCO–SNO–ASTRO guideline is clear on sorting: symptomatic brain metastases should receive local therapy whatever the systemic treatment; those with no symptoms, no systemic therapy option and 1 to 4 unresected lesions (small cell carcinoma excepted) should be offered radiosurgery alone; and anyone having whole-brain radiotherapy should be offered memantine and hippocampal avoidance[7]. The guideline sets surgery alongside radiosurgery, and as a radiation oncologist I should say this first: a large lesion with mass effect is more likely to benefit from surgery, and that is not the thing I do.

The cognitive side effects are not you overthinking it

What those trials measured has names: verbal learning and memory, verbal fluency, executive function and attention[3]. In daily life that is learning new things more slowly, not retrieving the name of someone you know well, losing the thread between the steps of a task. It is a measurable change, not your family being critical and not you dwelling on it. Say it out loud, because only then can the team plan around it.

Newer targeted drugs do reach the brain. That is not a reason to stop watching it

ALEX (303 patients) compared alectinib with crizotinib in first-line ALK-positive disease: a central nervous system progression event in 12% against 45%, cause-specific hazard ratio 0.16 (0.10–0.28)[8]. In the seven-year update of CROWN (296 patients), seven-year progression-free survival was 55% with lorlatinib against 3% with crizotinib, and no further intracranial progression events occurred in the lorlatinib arm after the first 30 months; the same paper states overall survival follow-up is continuing and has not yet reached the protocol-specified number of events, and the word the authors use about a chronic-disease trajectory is "may"[9]. Reading those figures, remember crizotinib is no longer a first-line choice.

On the EGFR side, FLAURA's pre-specified brain subgroup analysis: central nervous system progression-free survival was not reached with osimertinib against 13.9 months on the standard comparator (hazard ratio 0.48, 0.26–0.86); as for the widely quoted "intracranial response rate 91% against 68%", the denominators are only 22 and 19 patients, and the odds ratio of 4.6 has a confidence interval of 0.9 to 34.9 with P=.066, not statistically significant, and that trial did not require brain imaging in everyone[10]. The accurate statement: the drugs get in, but the strength of the evidence differs from one to the next. What happens after resistance is in "Preparing for the day the targeted drug stops working"; prophylactic cranial irradiation belongs to "Small cell lung cancer: speed is its only rule".

Whether there is anything in your brain is written into the reimbursement rules

Here is something a reader in Taiwan will struggle to learn elsewhere, and it matters: the National Health Insurance (健保) drug reimbursement rules (藥品給付規定) themselves sort patients by whether there are brain metastases. In section 9, first-line dacomitinib is limited in writing to patients "with an EGFR-TK Exon 19 deletion or Exon 21 L858R point mutation and no brain metastases (non-CNS)"; first-line bevacizumab with erlotinib is written specifically for "non-squamous non-small cell lung cancer with an activating EGFR Exon 21 L858R mutation and brain metastases". The clauses for osimertinib, alectinib and lorlatinib carry no brain-metastasis condition[11]. So the extra thing on that scan does not only change the treatment plan; it changes which clause on this page applies to you. This is also the fastest-expiring content here — the last amendment date in the chain is 115/8/1, verified 2026-08-30, so please go through the current version clause by clause with your case manager (個管師) or the hospital billing office (醫務課). The gene report is in "Those two weeks of waiting are not wasted".

The treatment side has rules too. The note to gamma knife stereotactic radiosurgery, item 37029B in the NHI payment schedule (支付標準) at 153,229 points, effective 2023-03-01, limits it for metastatic brain tumours to no more than five intracranial lesions, each no larger than 3.5 cm in diameter or 20 cm³ in volume, and requires one of: high craniotomy risk, unsuitability for invasive surgery, or "KPS ≥ 70 or ECOG 0-1 with limited progression"; CyberKnife is claimed under the same item; and every case must be submitted to the insurer for prior approval. The threshold for X-knife, item 37028B, is stricter still. Whole-brain radiotherapy is claimed on the ordinary linear accelerator per field (36011B, 1,231 points per simple field) and needs no prior application[12]. That explains two things: why the doctor asks how you are walking and managing at home (those are the performance status conditions), and why there is a wait — not the hospital dragging its feet, but the prior approval process. The above is the 114.03.13 version, verified 2026-08-30, and please confirm with your case manager or the hospital billing office; whether an application succeeds is judged case by case.

Which neurological symptoms mean today

First, to be clear: what follows is a list, not a ranking. I found no citable data on the frequency of each symptom, so no percentages. A headache unlike your usual ones; weakness or numbness down one side; speech that will not come out fluently, or a word you cannot find; a missing piece in your field of vision, or double vision; unsteadiness on your feet; a personality or reaction that does not seem like you; an early-morning headache with vomiting — new, and different from you last week, means the same day. The guideline principle is that symptomatic brain metastases should receive local therapy[7]: neurological symptoms are not the moment to watch and wait, they are the moment the treatment plan changes.

A first seizure is an emergency, not a case of "let's see whether it happens again". Current review writing states that once a brain tumour patient has had one confirmed seizure, the consensus is that brain tumour-related epilepsy has developed and treatment should start; while prophylactic medication is not recommended in those who have never had one[13]. The answer to "should I take something just in case" is no. What family can do during a seizure is short: nothing in the mouth, turn the person on their side, note the time it started, call an ambulance.

Steroids are also misread as "the doctor is being stingy". A 1994 randomised trial (89 evaluable patients, none with signs of herniation) compared three daily doses of dexamethasone: improvement at one week was comparable, while toxicity rose with dose[14]. That is a 30-year-old trial in 89 people, cited for one reason: there is a reason the dose is kept down. Not meanness, and certainly not something for you to adjust yourself.

Turn "brain metastases" into four concrete questions

One: "how many lesions, how big? Under the NHI conditions, am I going the radiosurgery route or the whole-brain route?" Two: "if I need whole-brain radiotherapy, can hippocampal avoidance be done here, and how is it costed?" Three: "my driver gene plus my brain metastasis status — which first-line reimbursement clause do I qualify under? Could we go through the text together?" Four: "when is the next brain scan, and what change in between brings me back early?" Write down the answer to the fourth.

A last sentence, for anyone still shaking: this whole article has been about how it is treated, what treating it costs, and how the NHI counts it — all another way of writing "there are still things that can be done". A lesion in the brain changes the plan. It is not a full stop.

From the physician

Nothing here decides your case — the number and size of the lesions, your performance status and your driver gene all move the answer, and the reimbursement text changes faster than this page. Take the four questions to your radiation oncologist and go through the current clauses with your case manager. Anything neurological that is new this week is a same-day visit, not a question for the next appointment.
