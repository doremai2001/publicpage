WHAT COMES AFTER

If it comes back, what can be done

When each of the three tumours recurs, what a second operation costs and what it buys, the discipline re-irradiation is held to, and how to read the scorecard of systemic drugs in meningioma.

Benign Brain Tumour Guide · What comes after · 19 references
#Recurrence#SBRT / SRS#Clinical Trials#Decisions

Every number in this article comes with its denominator and its study design attached. How many people have a complication at a second operation and how many live a long time afterwards are both in here; the drug section is a whole row of single-arm trials, plus the two that were put into a randomised comparison and came out badly.

"If it has come back, does that mean there is nothing left to give?" There are two mistakes packed into that sentence: treating recurrence in the three diseases as one thing, and turning "there is no standard drug" into "there is no drug". More drugs have been tried in meningioma than most people assume, and the problem is not the number — the two that were put into a randomised comparison both came back with bad news.

"Recurrence" does not run on the same clock in the three diseases

In meningioma what is watched is the grade and the extent of resection. The 2024 international consensus is direct about it: the higher the grade, the higher the recurrence rate; and the same document says the Simpson resection grade has become "somewhat controversial" in modern surgery[1]. Those two belong to "How a meningioma gets called grade 1, 2 or 3" and "How much came out, and whether that still counts". For the time scale, look at the interval between operations: in one skull base meningioma series, the median time from first resection to first reoperation was 4.4 years, and from the first reoperation to the second 4.1 years[2].

The clock runs slower in vestibular schwannoma (also called acoustic neuroma): after failed stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), the median interval to salvage surgery was 48 months, range 24 to 120 months (28 patients, six European centres)[3]. In the non-functioning pituitary adenoma the clock is barely a clock at all: of 148 people taken to gross total resection, 12 recurred (8.1%), the median time to recurrence was 80 months, and the odds of recurring rose linearly year on year[4]. What that does to the follow-up schedule is written in "How follow-up is scheduled, and when it stops".

Operating again: two cohorts, with the gain and the price both inside them

Can you be operated on again after a recurrence? Yes — but what you pay and what you get back have to be read together. Start with non-skull-base meningioma. One medical centre's 67 patients underwent 111 reoperations, with a median follow-up of 9.8 years: in 22% of cases the grade had gone up at reoperation. Those 111 operations produced 48 complications, spread across 32 patients (48%), 26 of which needed another operation to deal with them; neurological deficit 14% (permanent in 8%), wound dehiscence or infection 14%, cerebrospinal fluid leak or hydrocephalus 9%[5].

The other half has to be written too: in the same dataset there were no perioperative deaths, median survival after the first reoperation was 11.5 years, and survival at two, five and ten years was 91.0%, 68.8% and 50.0%[5]. The skull base series has a similar shape: 78 patients, 100 reoperations, 60 complications occurring in 30 cases, 20 of them needing surgical management; again no perioperative deaths, and posterior fossa location was significantly associated with complications[2]. Both are single-centre retrospective data, and the people who could be reoperated on had already been selected; these are not the answer to "what would happen if I were opened up again".

What a second operation costs in schwannoma and in pituitary adenoma

The meta-analysis of repeat microsurgery for vestibular schwannoma gathered 10 studies and 359 patients: pooled gross total resection rate 0.71 (95% confidence interval 0.46 to 0.87) — the range in the bracket is where that estimate plausibly sits. The price was worsening of facial nerve function after surgery in 43% (0.29 to 0.58) and cerebrospinal fluid leak in 11%. The authors' concluding sentence is that repeat microsurgery gives reasonable tumour control, but the risk of facial nerve deterioration and other complications is higher than at the first operation[6].

The numbers for the group who failed radiosurgery are harder. In 28 patients, the indication in 27 was radiological regrowth; gross total resection was achieved in 13/28 (46.4%), complications in 3/28 (10.7%). The hearing column: 10/20 (50.0%) still had serviceable hearing before radiosurgery, 4/24 (16.7%) still had it immediately before surgery, and all 14 with postoperative follow-up ended in the bottom hearing class. What the authors wrote down is: in this situation, preserving hearing is not a realistic goal. In the facial nerve column, 73.3% were back in the top two grades at twelve months, and 3/15 (20.0%) stayed in the bottom two[3].

For a second endoscopic endonasal operation on a non-functioning pituitary adenoma, a comparison of 211 first operations with 57 repeat operations found comparable complication rates, no deaths, and comparable two-year radiological progression-free survival (95.4% against 97.9%). The difference sat in two places: new panhypopituitarism 7.1% against 0.5%, and gross total resection 26.3% against 59.2%[7].

On re-irradiation I am deliberately printing no numbers

Whether somewhere already irradiated can be treated again is written out on this site in "Can somewhere already irradiated be treated again?": there is no general rule here, it has to be assessed case by case, and the European consensus document says of itself that prospective evidence on re-irradiation is scarce. That page prints no dose and no interval in months, and neither will this one. There is one thing to take away: the smaller the treated volume, the higher the cumulative dose that can be given, and "after a few months it is safe again" does not stand up.

What about meningioma's own re-irradiation data? I found several series reports, and the direction leans towards feasible, but I could not obtain figures I could quote word for word, so I am printing no values here. Boron neutron capture therapy for high-grade recurrent meningioma is already written out on this site in "Meningioma: the big trial that is never coming".

"There is no drug" is wrong; "there is no standard drug" is right

Lay out the citable signals first. Every one of them is a single-arm phase II trial, and the primary endpoint is the six-month progression-free survival proportion (PFS-6) — "what share of people have not progressed at six months", which is neither a response rate nor a survival rate.

  • Sunitinib: 36 people with recurrent grade 2 to 3 meningioma, PFS-6 42%, meeting the prespecified threshold. Look at the toxicity at the same time: one fatal intratumoural haemorrhage, several grade 3 to 4 haemorrhages and thrombotic microangiopathies, one gastrointestinal perforation. The authors themselves wrote that a randomised trial should be done[8].

  • Bevacizumab: 50 people enrolled (42 of them with progressive meningioma), PFS-6 87% for grade 1, 77% for grade 2 and 46% for grade 3; radiological response was mostly stable disease, with hypertension in 42.2% and proteinuria in 35.6%[9].

  • The focal adhesion kinase inhibitor in Alliance A071401: the first meningioma phase II trial to assign treatment by the tumour's genotype, 322 screened and 36 enrolled, PFS-6 83% in grade 1 and 33% in grades 2 to 3, both meeting their thresholds. The authors' conclusion is that it warrants further evaluation, not that it can be used[10].

Below those, they get smaller still. Everolimus plus octreotide had only 20 people, with 55% free of progression at six months (95% confidence interval 31.3 to 73.5), and the authors' own words are that it merits further study[11]; octreotide on its own had 9 people and not one partial response[12]; pembrolizumab had 18 people, with only 11.1% free of progression at six months and at twelve, and it closed early for lack of efficacy together with poor accrual[13]. The thing to read alongside those figures is the denominator — nine people, eighteen people, twenty people.

The two trials that made a randomised comparison both came back bad news

The antiprogestin mifepristone has a double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled phase III trial behind it (SWOG S9005, 164 patients with unresectable meningioma). The proportion completing two years without progression was 30% (24/80) on the drug and 33% (28/84) on placebo, and neither failure-free survival nor overall survival differed statistically; the authors' concluding sentence is that long-term mifepristone was well tolerated but had no effect in these patients[14].

Trabectedin has a randomised phase II trial (EORTC-1320, 90 people with recurrent grade 2 to 3 meningioma, against local standard of care as the control arm). Median progression-free survival was 4.17 months with standard care and 2.43 months with trabectedin — the direction is the wrong way round; overall survival did not differ, and grade 3 or higher adverse events were 59.0% against 44.4%. What the authors wrote is that trabectedin did not improve progression-free or overall survival and was more toxic than standard care[15].

These two have to be read together with the ones above. What those single-arm trials "met" was a historical-control threshold, not another group of real people; and the international consensus writes for itself that because historical thresholds of the PFS-6 kind are of poor reliability, randomised trials should be done wherever they are feasible[1]. The 2021 European meningioma guideline sets the tone: all systemic drug treatment is still experimental, and no standard systemic therapy has been defined[16]. The schwannoma guideline is shorter still: apart from bevacizumab used in NF2-related patients, drug treatment has no role[17] — the detail is in "More than one, NF2, and what is inherited", and it cannot be extrapolated to people with the sporadic kind. Several trials are still recruiting; how to find one and how to read it is set out on this site in "Clinical trials: how to find one, how to read it".

In the Taiwanese clause, "recurrence" is written in explicitly

On the Taiwan side there is a clause I can quote here, and it has written "recurrence" into itself explicitly. In the current version of the NHI fee schedule (effective 1 April 2026, ROC year 115), 37028B, three-dimensional X-knife stereotactic radiosurgery, 82,000 points, and 37029B, image-guided intensity-modulated X-ray stereotactic radiosurgery, 153,229 points, cover vestibular schwannoma, meningioma and pituitary tumour word for word, and require one of the following conditions to be met — the clause lists six of them, A to F, and meeting any one is enough; you do not have to meet them all. A is "A. has previously undergone craniotomy but has residual tumour or tumour recurrence", B is where craniotomy might cause neurological injury or carries a high risk, and C is where severe cardiopulmonary or other medical disease makes the patient unsuitable for surgery or general anaesthesia; F is worth pointing out separately, because it names a unilateral intracranial cerebellopontine angle acoustic neuroma less than 2.5 cm in width (excluding the internal auditory canal) — which puts someone who has never had an operation inside the provision on size alone. The clause also states that every case requires prior case-by-case application[18]. "Recurrence after an operation" is not an exception in the clause; it is a listed indication — but the prior case-by-case application is reviewed by the insurer, and whether it passes is not for me to say.

Two things in the opposite direction. Re-irradiation: I could not find a listed item for it anywhere in the fee schedule — and not finding a listed item does not mean there is no coverage; how to file a claim is a question for the medical affairs office. Bevacizumab: I have read clause 9.37 of the drug reimbursement regulations word for word, through all seven indication groups, and meningioma, schwannoma and NF2 return not a single hit[19] — that only means the clause does not list this indication, and no conclusion about whether an application could be made follows from it. The catastrophic illness certificate and other subsidies are covered more fully on this site in "Beyond the Catastrophic Illness Certificate: what else you can claim".

After a recurrence the first thing is not to choose a drug; it is to get the case into a multidisciplinary discussion — how to confirm that is written in "Has my case been discussed by a team?". Three questions to take into that discussion: whether the grade and location of this recurrence make reoperation, re-irradiation and a clinical trial easier or harder, each in turn; if another operation, which column is the price paid in; and if none of them is chosen, what is watched next and how often.

From the physician

What decides how many roads you have is a handful of documents, not how many drug names you can find: the last two scans, the pathology report from the previous operation, and, if you have had radiotherapy, that treatment summary as well. If those are not in the room, the discussion can only be held from memory.
