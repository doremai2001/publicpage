AFTER TREATMENT

The days on the waiting list

Why you still get treated while waiting, SBRT's rightful place in bridging, and both sides of downstaging — every treatment in these days has one purpose: protecting your eligibility for transplant.

Liver Cancer Guide · After Treatment · 8 references
#Liver Transplant#SBRT#TACE & Y-90#Treatment Pathway

Bridging therapy has no randomised trials, and the guideline still lists it as routine — because dropping off the list is an irreversible price. The AASLD in black and white: no one local treatment is superior to another, and external-beam radiotherapy is on the list. This article lays out every square of the wait for you.

From the day your name goes onto the waiting list, time runs on two tracks: the ranking is moving on one, and the tumour is growing on the other. This article is about those days — why you still get treated while waiting, with what, and whether there is a road for people whose disease has grown beyond the criteria. Let me nail my position down at the start: every local treatment in this article exists to protect your eligibility for transplant, not to replace the transplant. Any reading that leaves you thinking "do the treatment first, sort out the transplant later" is a misreading.

My position first: stereotactic body radiotherapy and proton therapy for liver cancer are treatments I deliver myself — and much of it is self-paid. The other side of every comparison in this topic is my colleagues' surgery and ablation. That is why every comparison comes with its primary source, so you can take it to the surgical and hepatology clinics for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

Why treat at all while you wait

Waiting is not standing still. Under the American allocation system, a liver cancer patient must wait six months after listing before receiving tumour exception points — and the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) 2023 guidance accordingly lists bridging therapy as routine practice: local treatment during the wait, to control the tumour and lower the risk of dropping off the list through progression[1]. The floor of the evidence has to be declared honestly first: the systematic review the AASLD commissioned (Kulik 2018) found no randomised trials at all; for within-criteria waiting patients, the effect of bridging on "dropping off the list through progression" pointed in a favourable direction but was not statistically significant, and the quality of the evidence was rated very low[2]. Even on evidence this thin, the guidance still treats bridging as routine — because dropping off the list is an irreversible price, and a trial that randomises one group to no treatment and watches the tumours grow will probably never be run. This is a field where consensus fills the evidence gap — the same predicament as "Follow-up and AFP: how often to scan".

Which bridge? The guideline says none is superior to another

AASLD 2023 in black and white: for bridging therapy, no one form of local treatment is currently recommended over another; the available options include transarterial chemoembolisation (TACE), Y-90, ablation, and external-beam radiotherapy[1]. Let me point out what that line means for me: radiotherapy's place in bridging is given by the guideline's own text — it does not have to be fought for with comparative studies. Actual comparative data exist too, labels attached: a ten-year intention-to-treat analysis from Toronto (Sapisochin 2017; retrospective, single centre) covered 379 waiting patients — 36 on SBRT, 99 on TACE, 244 on RFA — with dropout from the list at 16.7%, 20.2% and 16.8%, statistically no different; survival measured from listing and from transplant likewise showed no difference that could be seen[3]. The SBRT group held only 36 people, and this is retrospective data — what this study can carry is "SBRT can serve safely as a bridge"; it cannot carry "SBRT is the better bridge". Which one suits you is put together by the multidisciplinary meeting from tumour position, size and liver function — this article gives no algorithm.

The bridge is paid for in liver function too

This red line has to be drawn once more, in the context of waiting. Local treatment can itself decompensate the liver — for a waiting patient that is a double blow: the body gets worse, and eligibility for transplant may be lost with it. The AASLD advises attempting downstaging treatment only in patients with adequate liver function (for example Child-Pugh A to B, bilirubin no higher than 3)[1]. The purpose of a bridge is to protect eligibility; a bridge that crushes the liver function is the opposite of the point. The Child-Pugh vocabulary is in "You have two diseases: the cancer and the cirrhosis". If, while waiting, ascites builds up, consciousness changes, or you vomit blood or pass black stools, do not wait for your appointment — the list is in "When to come back to hospital the same day" — and notify the transplant team at the same time, because a change in your condition changes your status on the list.

Beyond the criteria: downstaging is a real road — both sides of it

A tumour beyond the criteria is not necessarily the end. Downstaging means using treatment to shrink the disease back inside the criteria: a pooled analysis of 13 studies and 950 patients put the rate of successful return to within criteria at about 48%, higher in the prospective studies (68%); patients transplanted after successful downstaging had post-transplant recurrence of about 16%, higher than those who started inside the criteria[4]. The system's side: after coming back within criteria, there is still a three-to-six-month observation period before exception points are granted; and a patient whose AFP exceeded 1,000 must bring it below 500 to count as successfully downstaged[1]. What successful downstaging is worth has been measured by the Italian XXL randomised trial: 74 patients beyond the Milan criteria entered downstaging, of whom 29 (39%) were already out — through progression and other causes — before randomisation; the 45 who downstaged successfully and stayed stable for three months were randomised between transplant and continued treatment, with five-year overall survival of 77.5% against 31.2%[5]. Hold on to both sentences: for those who downstage successfully and get through the observation period, the gap the transplant opens up is enormous; but nearly four in ten never reached the randomisation step, and the trial closed early and was small — the authors themselves say the results should be interpreted with caution[5]. Downstaging is part of the transplant plan from beginning to end; every step is taken in sync with the transplant team — not treatment first, transplant questions later.

Taiwan's reality: over 80% living donors

Waiting in Taiwan has its own shape. A transplantation study built on Taiwan's NHI database cites that over 80% of liver transplants in Taiwan are living-donor transplants[6] — that is the figure cited by that study; I have not obtained the official breakdown. It rewrites the logic of "waiting": with a suitable living donor, the timetable is driven mainly by the assessment process; without one, it is a race between the list and the disease — and those are two entirely different calendars. The living donor's safety numbers and their price, I have placed in "Life after a liver transplant"; the assessment and listing system is in "Transplant: the one option that treats both diseases". The number waiting: the Organ Donation, Transplant Registry and Patient Autonomy Promotion Center (at tosrpapc.org.tw) publishes monthly statistics, and at the end of July of ROC year 115 (2026) the combined waiting count across all organs was 11,786 people[7]; the liver-only figure is published as a graphic in the official release, and I could not extract a precise value I could safely cite — please go by the registry's published figure on the day[8].

Twelve months on the list, with something to do every month

The most grinding part of waiting is not knowing whether you are moving. Lay the next twelve months out as visible squares: every three months, confirm with your transplant case manager that your listing status and ranking conditions have not changed; keep every post-bridging imaging appointment — that is the report card on whether the tumour is being held down; keep the liver function and the antivirals to the discipline of "The liver needs care for life after treatment ends" — decompensation loses twice, damaging the body and the eligibility at once; if someone in the family is willing to be assessed as a living donor, start that assessment as early as possible — it takes time in itself; and pin the emergency warning list to the fridge — if something on it happens, go to hospital first, then notify the transplant team. The days on the list are not empty waiting — every one of those squares is about delivering a better version of you to transplant day.

From the physician

A change in your condition while waiting changes your status on the list: for an emergency, go to hospital first and notify the transplant team at the same time. Confirm your listing status with the transplant case manager every three months; if family are willing to be assessed for living donation, start early — the assessment itself takes time.
