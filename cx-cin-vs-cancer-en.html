Before it is cancer: CIN and carcinoma in situ

Putting cytology, CIN grades, carcinoma in situ and invasive cancer back on one road: which grade can wait, which cannot, and when conisation is itself the treatment.

Cervical Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 11 references
#Screening#Staging#Decisions

The same diagnosis, treated or not treated, ends thirty years later at 0.7% versus 31.3% — "carcinoma in situ is nothing to fear" is a sentence that is only half true. This article writes out the condition that makes it hold, and maps every term on the report to its next move.

My position first: definitive chemoradiation and brachytherapy are the treatments I deliver every day. So every sentence about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it — not more warmly.

The smear report says HSIL, or "carcinoma in situ", and by that evening many people are searching survival rates. Pause first. "Abnormal smear", "precancerous change", "carcinoma in situ" and "invasive cancer" are not different names for the same thing — they are points on one road, set far apart. Put them back in the right order first, then be clear about what each point calls for. Most people holding an abnormal report are standing at a distance from "cancer" that still leaves time to act.

An abnormal smear is not a diagnosis — it is a signal to look closer

A smear is screening, not a verdict. The cytology classification on the report leads to colposcopy and biopsy, and the last word belongs to pathology. The pathology terms were unified in 2012 by the LAST project of the College of American Pathologists and the American Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology into two grades: low-grade (LSIL) and high-grade (HSIL) squamous intraepithelial lesions, with HSIL further annotated as CIN 2 or CIN 3[1]. So the same report can carry both "HSIL" and "CIN 3" — they point to the same box, not to a lesion that has upgraded.

"Carcinoma in situ is nothing to fear" holds on one condition: it gets treated

Carcinoma in situ is the old name for CIN 3: the lesion is still confined within the epithelium and has not broken through the basement membrane, so "it is not yet cancer" is correct as a statement of pathology[2]. But the other half of that sentence is written in a dark chapter of New Zealand's history. From 1955 to 1976, a group of CIN 3 patients at New Zealand's National Women's Hospital were kept under observation without their consent and left largely untreated; after a judicial inquiry, the complete follow-up was reconstructed. Among those who had only small biopsies and whose lesion went essentially unmanaged, 31.3% had developed invasive cancer by thirty years — 50.3% among those whose lesion was still present at 24 months — while among those whose initial treatment was adequate, the thirty-year figure was 0.7%[2]. The same diagnosis, differing only in whether it was treated, and the outcomes were 0.7% against 31.3%. "Carcinoma in situ is nothing to fear" is true — but the condition for it to hold is that the action on the treated side of that comparison actually happens. Which is why the 2019 guideline of the American Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology leaves CIN 3 no room to manoeuvre: treatment is recommended, "observation is unacceptable", and apart from pregnancy there is no second option[3].

CIN 1 can be left to retreat on its own, CIN 2 depends on conditions, CIN 3 does not wait

The classic review pooling natural-history studies going back to the 1950s estimates that about 60% of CIN 1 regresses by itself and about 1% progresses to invasive cancer — and its author says plainly that these are composite values across studies, and that morphology cannot predict the individual patient[4]. So for CIN 1 the 2019 guideline writes observation as preferred over treatment, with treatment becoming acceptable only after persistence for two years or more[3]. CIN 2 stands in the middle: a meta-analysis of 36 studies and 3,160 women who chose observation showed that about half regress within 24 months, and about 60% in women under 30[5]; the guideline recommends treatment, but when the fertility concern outweighs the cancer concern, the transformation zone is fully visible and endocervical sampling is clear, you can discuss observation with your doctor at 6-month intervals, capped at two years[3]. CIN 3 is the previous section — treatment, with no observation option. Glandular disease, adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), runs on separate rules: a diagnostic conisation first to exclude invasive adenocarcinoma, with the specimen taken out intact; once conisation confirms it with negative margins, simple hysterectomy is the preferred choice for those not preserving fertility[3].

Conisation is not the prelude to treatment — it often is the treatment

What LEEP or cone excision does is remove, in one piece, the small block of tissue containing the lesion. For CIN 2 and CIN 3 the guideline specifies excision over ablative destruction[3]; one box further up, for stage IA1 invasive cancer invading no deeper than 3 mm, the 2023 European gynaecological oncology societies' guideline (ESGO/ESTRO/ESP) is more direct still: conisation with negative margins can be considered definitive treatment, because "hysterectomy does not improve the outcome" — a bigger operation is overtreatment; and for stage IA2 (3 to 5 mm) with negative margins, conisation or simple hysterectomy is sufficient[6]. Taiwan's NHI fee schedule lists "wedge resection of the cervix" among its covered items[7]; whether LEEP carries a self-paid difference is a question I could not find one nationwide answer to — it varies by hospital, so confirm with the medical affairs office or your case manager.

"The margins are not clear" sounds like failure, but the next step is not a big operation

A meta-analysis of 97 studies and 44,446 women treated by excision: the lesion reached the margin in about 23%; residual or recurrent high-grade disease after treatment was 6.6% overall; and women with involved margins carried 4.8 times the risk of those with clear margins — a ratio compares the event rates of two groups, it is not a prediction about you as one person[8]. The numbers look frightening, and in clinic I ask patients to read the next sentence first: the same analysis found that the better watchdog is the HPV test after treatment — its sensitivity for catching residual disease is 91%, against 55.8% for margin status, and in women who test HPV-negative after treatment the residual risk falls to 0.8%[8]. The European guideline's handling: when high-grade disease is still present at the margin, the usual step is a repeat conisation to exclude deeper invasion — not going straight to removing the uterus[6].

If the cone specimen turns out to hold invasive cancer, the road changes

Stage IA is defined as invasion visible only under the microscope, no deeper than 5 mm — and its diagnosis, by design, requires a cone specimen that encompasses the whole lesion[9]. So this situation happens: you thought you were dealing with a precancerous lesion, and the pathology report comes back reading invasive cancer. That does not mean everything before was wasted — that very specimen is the basis for the staging that follows. Once the depth exceeds 5 mm or there is a visible lesion, the questions become "how is the stage set" and "surgery or radiotherapy", written in full in "Staged by examination and imaging, not surgery" and "Surgery or radiotherapy: not operating is not giving up"; I will not expand on them here.

There is a piece of administrative evidence drawing the same line from the side: Taiwan's NHI catastrophic illness list defines cervical cancer by the disease codes of invasive cancer, and carcinoma in situ is not on the list[10]. The system draws its line in the same place — carcinoma in situ is a lesion still within reach of a small operation. But that sentence must not be read backwards as "not serious": what happens when it is left untreated is in the numbers two sections up.

Follow-up after treatment is counted in years, not in visits

Having been treated does not mean you step off this road. A meta-analysis of 27 registry-linkage studies: after treatment for CIN, the risk of cervical cancer is 3.3 times that of the general population, and the elevation persists for at least 20 years[11]. The guideline stretches follow-up accordingly: an HPV test 6 months after treatment (whatever the margin status), then every 3 years, continuing for at least 25 years[3]. Taiwan's NHI keeps this door open too: for women with previous cervical precancer, or an abnormal last smear, a repeat smear within 6 months is a covered indication[7].

Report in hand, four questions to ask straight out at the next visit

  • "Which grade is my pathology report — CIN 1, 2, 3, or already invasive? If the report carries two different terms, do they mean the same thing?"

  • "At my age and with my fertility plans, is the option at this grade observation or excision? If observation, how often do I come back, and what is the time limit?"

  • "If I have a conisation, what is the plan if the margins are clear — and what if they are not?"

  • "Has the HPV test at 6 months after treatment been booked for me? How far out does my follow-up schedule run?"

Copy the answers into the blank space on the report and take it home. What this article can give you is the map; the road out of your own square has to be talked out with your doctor, through those four questions.

From the physician

Every grade's management carries caveats about age and fertility plans — the same report can mean different answers in two different people. Before the visit, dig out the pathology report and ask item by item: which grade, are the margins clear, is the next step observation or excision. Margin status and the HPV test at 6 months after treatment are the two boxes most often missed.
