WHY, AND HOW IT IS DECIDED

The machine, and the breathing

IMRT, TOMO and protons are about how the machine delivers the dose; deep inspiration breath-hold is about how you breathe. Separate the two axes and the choice of technique goes back from being a price list to being an indication.

Breast Radiotherapy Guide · Why, and How It Is Decided · 9 references
#IMRT#TomoTherapy#Proton Therapy#Breath-Hold#Decisions

The ordinary IMRT the NHI pays for has 3 randomised trials behind it and is not a superseded budget model; TOMO and protons each get a sentence placing them, with the depth left to the articles that follow. Once you have this map, the next time you hear a technical term you will know which box to put it in and which question to press.

Anyone coming to a radiation oncology clinic for the first time has usually already been washed over by the vocabulary: IMRT, arc therapy, TOMO, protons, deep inspiration breath-hold — it sounds like a menu running from cheap to expensive. Let me take that menu apart first. The first four are about how the machine delivers the dose into you; the last one is about how you breathe during the time the beam is on. They are not on the same axis, and both can be true at once. This article gives you a map that puts each of those words back in its own place. The depth in each box — cardiac dose, the situations where TOMO differs, the proton evidence — has an article of its own in this topic, and I am not opening them up here.

My position first: every technique compared in this topic — ordinary intensity-modulated radiotherapy, TOMO, deep inspiration breath-hold — has a self-paid component among the more expensive options, and all of them are delivered in my own department; protons mean a referral elsewhere. So every "better" here comes with its primary source and the level of evidence behind it, and on cost I cite the official schedule where one exists and tell you to ask the medical affairs office where it does not. Concluding that the basic option is enough for you is a completely reasonable conclusion.

Two axes: how the dose goes in, and where the heart is

The horizontal axis is how finely the dose is shaped: from three-dimensional conformal treatment (3D), to intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), to rotational techniques and protons — one direction, with the dose wrapped ever more closely around the target. The rotational box holds two ways of giving the same thing: volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) and helical tomotherapy (TOMO). Inside that box there is no ranking — the two win and lose against each other, and which produces the better dose distribution depends on your field arrangement and on the planning; the group B article has Taiwan's own data on that. The vertical axis has only one thing on it: deep inspiration breath-hold (DIBH). It is not a machine, it is the breathing posture you hold while the beam is on, and it can be laid on top of any photon technique above. So "TOMO or ordinary IMRT" and "breath-hold or not" are two independent questions. Which box you land in is decided by the indication — where the tumour sits, how large the treated volume is, what your own anatomy is like — not by the budget.

The ordinary IMRT the NHI pays for is not the budget model

The advantage IMRT has over the way treatment was given in earlier decades is at the level of randomised trials, and there are three of them. A Canadian multicentre double-blind trial randomised 358 breast radiotherapy patients between IMRT and wedged fields (331 were included in the analysis): acute moist desquamation 31.2% against 47.8%, and moist desquamation was itself associated with pain and with a drop in quality of life[1]. The Royal Marsden's randomised trial in the UK (306 patients) followed photographs for 5 years: 58% of the old-style 2D group had a change in the appearance of the breast, against 40% in the IMRT group[2]. The Cambridge trial (1,145 analysed, randomising the patients whose dose was uneven) found a 5-year odds ratio of 0.68 for poor overall cosmesis and 0.58 for skin telangiectasia — an odds ratio below 1 means the event was less likely to happen in the IMRT group — and the authors wrote "practice changing" into their conclusion outright[3]. All three point at the same mechanism: IMRT evens the dose out and takes the hot spots away, and that is where the difference in skin and appearance comes from. Two honest labels go with them: the comparator arms were the 2D and wedge-era technique of the years those trials enrolled, 2003 to 2007, not today's 3D conformal treatment; and this evenness is what the ordinary IMRT covered by the NHI is already doing — it is the current standard, not a superseded budget model.

DIBH is not a machine — it is taking a deep breath in

The principle of deep inspiration breath-hold takes one sentence: when you breathe in deeply the diaphragm drops and the lungs expand, which pulls the heart away from the chest wall; at the planning scan and during treatment you take a deep breath in and hold it, and the beam is on during the held breath[4]. Because it is a posture, it can be laid on top of any photon technique — 2D, 3D, IMRT, arc therapy, TOMO. Its evidence has to be labelled in two layers. The first layer is dosimetric: a meta-analysis of left-sided breast cancer treated with arc therapy shows that DIBH, compared with free breathing, lowers the mean heart dose, the dose to the left anterior descending coronary artery and the dose to the ipsilateral lung[5] — the reduction is measurable, but this is dose, not clinical events. The second layer is the relationship between dose and events: cohort-level data on women who had radiotherapy for breast cancer show that for every additional 1 Gy of mean heart dose the rate of major coronary events rises by about 7.4%, with no safe lower threshold in sight[6]. Joining the two layers gives a reasonable chain, but the thing itself — that DIBH reduces cardiac events — has no randomised clinical evidence behind it at present, and I write that down as it is. Why the left side is different, how big the reduction is, who can hold the breath, and what it costs you are all covered in depth in "Heart dose: the core question on the left side".

TOMO: another way of giving rotational treatment, not an upgrade across the board

TOMO delivers intensity-modulated treatment in a helical, slice-by-slice fashion; it sits in the same rotational box as arc therapy, and it is a way of taking the dose distribution one step further. One step further does not mean winning in every box: a retrospective cohort (315 patients with early breast cancer, single centre, followed for only 3 months) saw less radiation pneumonitis in the TOMO group (0% against 4.3%) and, in the other direction, more grade 3 to 4 skin reactions (16.2% against 7.6%)[7]. That dataset is both short and retrospective, and it cannot be turned round and used to rule that "TOMO damages skin" — but it is enough to make one point: the differences turn up in particular situations, they are not a blanket superiority. Which situations differ in a way that matters, which situations differ hardly at all, and how the cost is worked out are in "TOMO and ordinary IMRT: where the difference is".

Protons: the dosimetry is clear, the randomised answer has not read out

The physics of protons concentrates the dose more tightly, and there is not much argument about that. What is argued about is what the concentration buys clinically. RADCOMP, the pragmatic randomised trial comparing protons directly against photons, is under way, and its primary endpoint is major cardiovascular events; as of the end of August 2026, all I can find is the trial protocol — the main results have not been published[8]. The estimated primary completion date on the trial registry is 2036, so this box is going to stay empty for many years; it is not a matter of waiting one or two[9]. Until it reads out, "protons are safer for breast cancer" has no citable evidence behind it — protons may need a referral elsewhere, but radiotherapy is my own field, so I draw this line more tightly rather than more loosely. Who might consider protons for breast cancer, and where referral and cost stand at the moment, are in "Protons for the breast: where the evidence stands"; the physics of protons and their regulatory status have two articles of their own on this site, "Protons: precise. Then what?" and "Is proton therapy actually better?", and I am not repeating them.

Take the map into the clinic, not the price list

On cost I will say only one thing here: the NHI and self-paid status of each technique has its own section in the group B articles of this topic, and for any item where I could find no official announcement, what I write is "ask the medical affairs office". What this map is really meant to leave you with is a single sentence: work out first which box the word you have just heard belongs in — is it about how the machine gives the dose, or about how you breathe — then ask whether it has an indication given your treated volume and your anatomy, and put the price after those two questions every time. If your tumour is on the left, hold on to the breath-hold box; that is the one square on this map you can use today. How the number of treatments gets set is a separate question, in "Why am I not on the shorter course?".

From the physician

When you hear a new term, ask first whether it is about the machine or about the breathing, then ask about the indication, and put the price last. If your tumour is on the left, put "do I need to practise the deep inspiration breath-hold?" on the question list for your next appointment — that is the one thing on this map you can take away today.
