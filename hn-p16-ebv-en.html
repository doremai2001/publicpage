p16-positive does not mean good news in Taiwan

What p16 and EBV DNA are each used for, what they mean, why the Western line about better prognosis has to be recalculated here, and why you should not read your own number.

H&N Guide · After the Diagnosis · 11 references
#Nasopharyngeal#Biomarkers#Decisions#Quitting

Two terms on the pathology report get looked up more than anything else on it, and misread more often than anything else too. Here is which cancer each one belongs to, and why a sentence lifted from a Western paper needs recalculating before it applies to you.

There is a conversation I have almost every month in clinic. The patient comes in holding the report and the first thing he says is: "Doctor, they say p16-positive is the good kind. That means I have the good kind, right?" I usually answer with a question of my own: do you chew betel quid, do you smoke? Because in Taiwan, the answer to those two questions turns the meaning of "p16-positive" completely over.

Taiwan's own numbers: among HPV-positive patients the gap runs to forty percentage points

A study collecting 425 incident oropharyngeal cancers from five Taiwanese medical centres between 1999 and 2014 is the data closest to us on this question. Overall, HPV-positive patients had five-year overall survival of 67.8% against 49.0% for HPV-negative, hazard ratio 0.52 (95% CI 0.35–0.76, p=0.0005) — so far, exactly what the Western literature says[1].

Split the HPV-positive group open and the story changes. In the same study, HPV-positive patients with no betel quid or cigarette exposure reached five-year overall survival of 88.2%; HPV-positive patients who did have betel quid or cigarette exposure reached only 48.3%[1]. That 48.3% is barely distinguishable from the 49.0% of HPV-negative disease.

So for a Taiwanese patient with a history of betel quid, tobacco or alcohol, "p16-positive means the good kind" is close to wrong. That handsome prognosis belongs to the people without those exposures. Of everything in this head and neck series, this is what I think Taiwanese readers most need to know. Read the other way round, it is also where the good news is: the one variable in that sentence you can still change is not touching them again from today.

First, one thing to settle: p16 is only used in oropharyngeal cancer

In the head and neck cancer guideline of the Taipei Medical University healthcare system (臺北醫學大學醫療體系), which includes Wan Fang Hospital (萬芳醫院), adding p16 to the biopsy is a mandatory item for oropharyngeal cancer, while EBV DNA is a selective test for nasopharyngeal cancer[2]. If there is no p16 on your report for an oral cavity, hypopharyngeal or laryngeal cancer, nothing was left out — that field carries no meaning in your disease, and it would not change your stage either.

This matters in Taiwan because our disease mix is dominated by oral cavity cancer. Among Taiwanese men between 1980 and 2019, age-standardised incidence rose for oral cavity cancer from 4.19 to 27.19 per 100,000 (average annual change 5.1%), for oropharyngeal cancer from 0.97 to 8.46 (5.7%), for hypopharyngeal cancer from 0.92 to 6.46 (5.1%), while laryngeal cancer stayed nearly flat (1.2%); the authors attributed the strongest period effect over that span to national per-capita betel quid consumption, and noted that the curves began to flatten after 2010 as tobacco and betel quid policy took hold[3]. Most of the patients who sit in my clinic were never going to have a p16 line on their report, and they do not need to spend money to get one.

So why does oropharyngeal cancer get its own staging system

Because the old staging stopped working in this group. The International Collaboration on Oropharyngeal cancer Network for Staging (ICON-S) analysed 1,907 HPV-positive oropharyngeal cancer patients from 7 centres across Europe and North America and found that under TNM 7th edition, five-year overall survival for stages I, II, III and IVA was 88%, 82%, 84% and 81%, with a global p value of 0.25 — four stages that could not be told apart, with only stage IVB separating at 60%[4]. A staging system that cannot discriminate prognosis has no use, and that is the direct reason the AJCC/UICC 8th edition pulled HPV-related oropharyngeal cancer out and gave it a separate scale.

What that means in practice: "stage I" in HPV-positive oropharyngeal cancer and "stage I" in HPV-negative oropharyngeal cancer are measurements taken with two different rulers. Comparing your stage with the person in the next bed is mostly meaningless.

p16 is a stand-in, not the virus — and Taiwan is where the stand-in is least reliable

p16 is an immunohistochemical stain. It measures a protein, not a virus. In that five-centre Taiwanese study, p16 staining had 92% sensitivity and 91% specificity for HPV infection[1] — good, but not perfect.

An individual patient data analysis spanning 9 countries, 13 cohorts and 7,654 oropharyngeal cancer patients puts it more finely: of 3,805 p16-positive patients, 415 (10.9%) were in fact HPV-negative. That discordance rate was higher in regions with lower HPV-attributable fractions, and higher in subsites other than the tonsil and base of tongue (29.7% against 9.0%). Patients whose p16 and HPV results disagreed had a significantly worse prognosis than those positive for both, and a significantly better one than those negative for both; the authors recommend adding HPV testing to p16 in regions with a low HPV-attributable fraction[5].

Taiwan's HPV-positive share of oropharyngeal cancer is 29%, and it barely moved between 1999 and 2014 (25%, 30%, 30% and 29% across the four four-year blocks)[1]. We are exactly the kind of low HPV-attributable-fraction region they are describing. So I do not treat p16-positive as a reason to give anyone less treatment. De-escalation is still a question clinical trials are asking, not standard practice, and Taiwan is the last place to apply it to yourself.

EBV DNA in nasopharyngeal carcinoma: it predicts well, but it is not a verdict

Plasma EBV DNA in nasopharyngeal carcinoma is a different story, and Taiwan has its own data here too. A study from Taichung Veterans General Hospital (臺中榮民總醫院) measured pre-treatment plasma EBV DNA in 99 patients with stage III or IV non-metastatic nasopharyngeal carcinoma: it was detectable in 94, while all 40 healthy controls and all 20 cured patients were undetectable. Median levels were 681 copies/mL in stage III and 1,703 copies/mL in stage IV, against 291,940 copies/mL in the patients with distant metastases used for comparison[6]. The value broadly moves with tumour burden.

The post-treatment signal is stronger. The same group followed 931 newly diagnosed nasopharyngeal carcinoma patients who completed radiotherapy given with curative intent, of whom 125 (13.4%) still had detectable plasma EBV DNA one week after radiotherapy ended — and at genuinely low levels (median 21 copies/mL). Among those 125 the relapse rate was 64.8%, median time to progression 20 months, five-year overall survival 49.6%; of the 39 with levels of 100 copies/mL or above, 32 (82.1%) relapsed, against 49 of the 86 (57.0%) below 100[7].

Note the other side of that 64.8%: thirty-five per cent of them did not relapse. Detectable is not the same as relapsed. It is a signal to follow you more closely, not a diagnosis.

It also works for screening in endemic populations. A prospective screening study in Hong Kong covering 20,174 asymptomatic men found EBV DNA on first test in 1,112 (5.5%), but only 309 were still positive on a repeat about four weeks later — 1.5% of everyone screened, and only 27.8% of those initially positive. Thirty-four nasopharyngeal cancers were found, 71% of them stage I or II against 20% in a historical comparison group[8]. The number worth remembering from that study is actually the 27.8%: more than seven in ten of the initially positive results turned negative on retesting.

Why you should not read your own number, and should not compare it with anyone else's

This is the section of the whole series where I am least willing to soften my tone.

An international collaboration sent the same 40 nasopharyngeal carcinoma plasma samples to four laboratories, each of them expert-level. Before harmonisation, the intraclass correlation coefficients of three of them against the index centre were only 0.62, 0.70 and 0.59; once calibrators and PCR reagents were standardised, they rose to 0.83, 0.95 and 0.96[9]. (The intraclass correlation coefficient scores how closely two sets of measurements agree; 1.0 is perfect agreement. Around 0.6 means that the same tube of blood sent to two laboratories can come back with very different numbers.)

In plain words: numbers from different laboratories cannot be compared directly. Your 800 from Hospital A and your friend's 300 from Hospital B tell you nothing about who is worse off; comparing yours with a figure posted in a patient support group is more meaningless still. Even moving your follow-up to a different hospital is something to tell your doctor about, so they know where the previous test was run. What carries meaning is the trend across several measurements on you, from the same laboratory using the same method — and that trend is for the doctor looking after you to read.

Two things that get misunderstood, said plainly

First, EBV DNA is not part of staging. The 9th edition of the AJCC and UICC nasopharyngeal cancer TNM classification, revised on the basis of 4,914 patients, changed things on imaging — bringing radiologically evident extranodal extension into N3, regrouping non-metastatic disease into stages I to III, and subdividing M1 into IVA (three or fewer metastatic lesions) and IVB (more than three)[10]. EBV DNA in the blood was not written into the anatomic staging. It is a prognostic and surveillance tool, not a stage.

Second, do not use a clinical trial as grounds for taking less treatment. NRG-HN001 is the flagship trial of using EBV DNA to set adjuvant treatment intensity in nasopharyngeal carcinoma, designed so that patients with undetectable EBV DNA after treatment were randomised to observation or to adjuvant chemotherapy. That trial closed to accrual in July 2023, the official page is marked "Closed to Accrual & Treatment", and no results have been posted there[11]. It is not a basis for saying today "my EBV DNA is undetectable, so I can skip adjuvant treatment."

This whole article, condensed into three questions you can ask out loud

If you have oropharyngeal cancer: is my p16 positive or negative, and was HPV testing added? Given my betel quid or smoking history, does this result mean for me what the textbook says it means?

If you have nasopharyngeal cancer: which laboratory ran my EBV DNA, and will my follow-up tests all be run there? When is the next one due, and what kind of change would make you investigate further?

If you have oral cavity, hypopharyngeal or laryngeal cancer: my report does not need p16, so which fields are the ones that actually affect my prognosis? Getting those straight is worth far more than chasing a test that does not belong to you.

From the physician

These figures are population averages; what they mean for you depends on subsite, stage, your exposure history and how the tumour responds to treatment. With EBV DNA in particular, do not track your own numbers or compare them with anyone else's. Bring the report to the doctor who follows you and read the trend together.
