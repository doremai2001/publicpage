AFTER THE DIAGNOSIS

The report says IDH-mutant: you are on a different road

There are two diseases under IDH-mutant. This article separates astrocytoma from oligodendroglioma, and sets out the population vorasidenib was tested in, criterion by criterion.

Glioblastoma Guide · After the Diagnosis · 11 references
#Molecular Testing#Decisions#Targeted Therapy#Evidence

The phrase "lower-grade" gets read as benign in my clinic every day of the week. It is not. It grows, it infiltrates, and it eventually becomes an aggressive tumour — and one line on the report in your hand decides which road you take from here.

If what pushed you into this topic is the words IDH-mutant on your report, let me be clear straight away: the main line of this topic is glioblastoma, and that is not your disease. I wrote this article anyway, because until the report came out you were among the same group of people — the same operation, the same stretch of waiting. After the report, you are on a different road, and almost every rule on it differs from the main line.

First sort out which of the two diseases you are holding

Underneath IDH-mutant there are two diseases. The INDIGO trial paper defines them cleanly: a glioma carrying an IDH1 or IDH2 mutation together with an unbalanced translocation between chromosomes 1 and 19 (that is, 1p/19q codeletion) is an oligodendroglioma; one with an IDH mutation but without 1p/19q codeletion is defined as an astrocytoma[1]. This is not a naming detail: the treatment recommendations for the two differ, and which of the sentences below applies to you turns on that one line of your report. How that line is arrived at, and why FISH produces false positives, is in "Two weeks for the report: what is it waiting for".

There is one more term to learn: homozygous deletion of CDKN2A/B. Once an IDH-mutant astrocytoma is found to carry it, the tumour is CNS WHO grade 4 outright, even with no necrosis and no microvascular proliferation[2]. And IDH-mutant grade 4 astrocytoma is a grey zone — the ASCO and SNO guideline says so itself: this group may be managed according to the recommendations for grade 3 astrocytoma, or according to those for IDH-wildtype glioblastoma[3].

"Lower-grade" is the phrase that gets misread most

There are two sentences in INDIGO's background section that I read to patients word for word in clinic. The first: IDH-mutant grade 2 oligodendroglioma and astrocytoma keep growing (slowly, but they keep growing), infiltrate normal brain, and eventually become aggressive tumours with accelerated growth and new blood vessels, which on MRI shows up as enhancement appearing[1]. The second is blunter: IDH-mutant grade 2 gliomas are malignant brain tumours that cause considerable disability and early death[1]. So "lower-grade" describes what it looks like now, not how it ends. It does not mean benign, and it does not mean no treatment is needed.

Two roads, and the guideline recommends different things for each

In the ASCO-SNO recommendations as updated in 2025, the two roads are written separately[4]:

  • Grade 2 oligodendroglioma: radiotherapy with PCV (a combination of three drugs — procarbazine, lomustine and vincristine) should be offered, quality of evidence moderate, strength of recommendation strong; where toxicity is a concern, temozolomide is a reasonable alternative, but that one carries low quality of evidence and a conditional strength.

  • IDH-mutant, non-1p/19q-codeleted grade 2 astrocytoma: radiotherapy plus adjuvant chemotherapy (temozolomide or PCV) should be offered, quality of evidence moderate, strength of recommendation strong.

The long-term numbers for radiotherapy plus PCV come from RTOG 9802: it enrolled grade 2 astrocytoma, oligoastrocytoma or oligodendroglioma, in patients under 40 whose resection was incomplete or who had a biopsy only, or in patients over 40 regardless of extent of resection; 251 evaluable patients, median follow-up 11.9 years; radiotherapy followed by six cycles of PCV against radiotherapy alone gave median overall survival of 13.3 against 7.8 years (hazard ratio for death 0.59, P=0.003) and 10-year overall survival of 60% against 40%[5]. A hazard ratio compares how fast events happen in the two groups: 0.59 means deaths occurred more slowly in the arm that had PCV added. It does not mean "the probability becomes 0.59 times as big". This trial was published in 2016 and enrolled under the 2007 histological classification, when the diagnostic names still included "oligoastrocytoma", a word no longer in use[2]. How to read numbers of years like these, and why they are not your deadline, is in "That number, and how I am going to tell you about it".

Two more things follow on here, because they are often misheard as "chemotherapy can be swapped in for radiotherapy". EORTC 22033 randomised 477 patients with high-risk grade 2 glioma to radiotherapy alone or temozolomide alone: the primary endpoint of progression-free survival showed no difference (39 against 46 months, hazard ratio 1.16, P=0.22); and in an exploratory analysis of the IDH-mutant, non-codeleted group, progression-free survival with radiotherapy was in fact better than with temozolomide (hazard ratio 1.86)[6]. CATNON demonstrated something else: in IDH-mutant patients, adjuvant temozolomide after radiotherapy improved survival (hazard ratio 0.53), while concurrent temozolomide during radiotherapy did not — that arm was declared futile by the trial itself[7]. A trial ruling out one of its own arms is a thing you should know can happen.

Why people want to watch first, and why watching is not the same as not coming back

INDIGO's background sets out the reasoning plainly: adjuvant chemoradiotherapy can produce long periods of disease control, but it cannot clear the disease, and it comes with radiation-induced neurocognitive impairment, chemotherapy-associated hypermutation of DNA and other toxicities; to push those long-term toxicities further out, many such patients have no immediate adjuvant chemoradiotherapy after diagnosis and are followed with serial brain MRI instead[1]. The cognitive side of this is in "Memory, concentration, and changes in who he is".

"Watch first" has randomised evidence behind it, but that evidence is a different shape from what many people imagine. EORTC 22845 randomised patients with lower-grade glioma to early postoperative radiotherapy (157 patients) or radiotherapy only at progression (157 patients): progression-free survival was 5.3 against 3.4 years (hazard ratio 0.59), but overall survival was 7.4 against 7.2 years, with no difference (hazard ratio 0.97, P=0.872); 65% of the control group went on to have radiotherapy later; and seizure control at one year was better in the early radiotherapy group[8]. The authors attached two conditions to their own conclusion: radiotherapy can be deferred in patients in good condition — provided they are carefully monitored; and the trial did not measure quality of life, so "a longer time without progression" is not the same as "living better"[8]. This trial opened in 1986 and has no molecular typing.

The current guideline's position carries conditions too: for people with favourable prognostic factors (complete resection, younger age) or with concerns about toxicity, initial radiotherapy and chemotherapy may be deferred until radiographic or symptomatic progression — quality of evidence low, strength conditional[4]. "Watching" is a plan with a schedule, imaging and clinic appointments in it. It is not leaving it alone.

vorasidenib: which kind of person that trial actually enrolled

INDIGO is a double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled phase 3 trial that enrolled 331 patients at 77 sites in 10 countries[1]. I am setting out the inclusion criteria item by item, because this is the most important passage in the article[1]:

  • Aged 12 or over, with residual or recurrent, histologically confirmed grade 2 oligodendroglioma or astrocytoma, IDH1 or IDH2 mutation confirmed centrally.

  • Karnofsky performance score of at least 80; at least one previous operation, with the most recent surgery between 1 and 5 years before randomisation.

  • No anticancer treatment of any other kind apart from surgery; no need for steroids for signs or symptoms of the tumour; the physician's judgement that immediate chemotherapy or radiotherapy was not required.

  • Measurable non-enhancing disease was required (at least one target lesion of 1 cm by 1 cm or more), confirmed by central blinded review.

The result: progression-free survival as read blind by the independent review committee was 27.7 against 11.1 months (hazard ratio 0.39, P<0.001), and the hazard ratio for time to next anticancer intervention was 0.26[1]. The limits belong in the same paragraph as the result: this trial has no overall survival result, the paper stating that follow-up is ongoing; the quality-of-life, cognitive and seizure results are not reported yet either; median follow-up was only 14.2 months; the subgroup analyses had no pre-specified statistical testing; the primary endpoint is radiographic progression, not symptoms or function; and the comparator was placebo, not radiotherapy plus chemotherapy[1]. What it demonstrates is that taking this drug during a period of watchful waiting buys longer than taking nothing — not that it is better than radiotherapy plus chemotherapy.

The cost side: grade 3 or worse adverse events in 22.8% against 13.5%, most commonly raised liver enzymes (ALT increase 9.6% against 0%), and discontinuation for a treatment-related adverse event in 3.6% against 1.2%[1]. The guideline writes the monitoring in as a requirement: liver function every two weeks for the first two months of treatment and monthly thereafter; and people planning a pregnancy (either parent), pregnant or breastfeeding should not use it[4].

One drug, written differently in three places

The American FDA approved it on 6 August 2024, with the indication "grade 2 astrocytoma or oligodendroglioma with a susceptible IDH1 or IDH2 mutation, following surgery"[9]; ASCO-SNO adds that surgery here includes biopsy, subtotal resection or complete resection[4]. The European Union approved it on 17 September 2025, with three conditions written into the body of the indication — predominantly non-enhancing disease, surgery as the only prior treatment, and no immediate need for radiotherapy or chemotherapy — in patients aged 12 or over weighing at least 40 kg[10]. The European indication is narrower than the American one, and the group actually proven in the trial is narrower than either label.

The guideline's wording has to be read as written too. ASCO-SNO 2025 says it "may be offered", quality of evidence high, strength of recommendation conditional[4]. The same update points out item by item where the FDA label is wider than the trial, and says explicitly that for people who have already had radiotherapy or chemotherapy, the panel cannot make a recommendation[4].

In Taiwan: vorasidenib (VORANIGO film-coated tablets, 10 mg and 40 mg) is licensed, licence numbers 衛部藥輸字第 029063 號 and 029064 號 (Ministry of Health and Welfare imported-drug licences 029063 and 029064)[11]. Whether the NHI reimburses it, I could not find an official provision I could cite — that is "I could not find it", which is not the same as "there is none". Ask your neuro-oncologist, or ask your case manager to check with the hospital's medical affairs office.

Three things to get said out loud at your next appointment

First: is mine an oligodendroglioma or an astrocytoma? What does the 1p/19q line say, and has CDKN2A/B been tested? Second: if we watch for now, how far apart are the scans, and what change would make us alter the plan? Third: do I meet those INDIGO criteria — how many years since my last operation, is there measurable non-enhancing disease, have I had radiotherapy or chemotherapy?

None of those three needs you to understand statistics first, but they will force out specific answers. And one last time: "lower-grade" does not mean you can stop coming to clinic. Which situations cannot wait and mean coming back to hospital the same day is in "When to come back the same day: three tiers".

From the physician

You are not on this topic's main line, but "lower-grade" does not mean nothing is wrong. Take the report to clinic and settle the 1p/19q and CDKN2A/B lines first; if your doctor raises vorasidenib, go through the inclusion criteria together. That trial has no overall survival result to this day, and for its reimbursement status in Taiwan I could not find a provision I could cite.
