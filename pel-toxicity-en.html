HOW THE TECHNIQUE IS CHOSEN

What you buy is side effects, not survival

Modulated radiotherapy took 3-year late bowel toxicity after surgery for cervical cancer from 42.4% down to 21.1%; but on survival four separate datasets all say no difference, and acute gastrointestinal toxicity in rectal cancer did not improve either.

For the bladder box I will not give you a dose number — the QUANTEC bladder chapter, which collates dose-volume relationships, says itself that those partial-organ data are suspect. In the same trial, patients reported high-grade diarrhoea a great deal more often than the doctors recorded it (the figure in the paper is 38.5%); if you do not say it, the improvement cannot be seen.

The question I get asked most is not about side effects. It is "if I change to this technique, will I live longer?" The answer goes at the top: no, and that has been checked many times over. What modulated radiotherapy buys you is side effects, not survival — and not every side effect, and not in every cancer. This article writes the column that improved and the column that did not at the same length. Write only one of them and people get hurt, whichever one you pick.

My position first: pelvic radiotherapy is what I do every day, so every good word about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it. Implants such as the rectal spacer are not something my department provides; I write about them as an option you can go and ask about, and for cost and for who performs them, please ask that department and the medical affairs office.

Closing the survival box before anything else

Four independent sets of data point the same way. The randomised trial in cervical cancer after surgery (300 patients): 3-year pelvic relapse-free survival 81.8% against 84% (hazard ratio 1.17, p=.55 — a hazard ratio is the relative rate of events over time, and a large p value means the difference is easily explained by chance alone), disease-free survival 76.9% against 81.2% (p=.89)[1]. The updated report of the randomised trial in endometrial and cervical cancer after surgery: overall survival p=.53, disease-free survival p=.21, locoregional failure p=.81[2]. A meta-analysis in definitive treatment of cervical cancer (6 studies, 1,008 patients): the odds ratio for 3-year overall survival was 2.41, with a confidence interval of 0.62 to 9.39 — an interval that wide says nothing at all[3]. An Indian phase III trial in 173 patients likewise showed comparable local and distant control[4]. Anyone describing a technique as "treating the cancer better" gets stopped there in my clinic.

The column that did improve

The randomised trial in cervical cancer after surgery, 50 Gy in 25 fractions, median follow-up 46 months: grade 2 or worse late gastrointestinal toxicity at 3 years was 21.1% against 42.4%, hazard ratio 0.46 (95% confidence interval 0.29 to 0.73); any grade 2 or worse late toxicity 28.1% against 48.9%; and the patients themselves reported less diarrhoea and better appetite[1]. The label has to be exact: what it compares is image-guided intensity-modulated radiotherapy against three-dimensional conformal, and the experimental arm is recorded in the registry as being delivered on a helical tomotherapy unit[5]. So it cannot hold up "helical tomotherapy is better than ordinary modulation" — there is no such control arm — and it equally cannot be turned round into helical tomotherapy being a machine with no clinical evidence, because the one randomised trial in the pelvis that halved late toxicity was done on it.

The acute box is filled in by the patients themselves. The randomised trial in endometrial and cervical cancer after surgery (278 eligible): at the end of treatment, 33.7% against 51.9% reported "frequent or almost constant diarrhoea", and 7.8% against 20.4% were taking antidiarrhoeal medication four or more times a day[6]; a year later, high-level diarrhoea frequency was 5.8% against 15.1%, and at three years the urinary scores were still improving in the modulated group while the conventional group was going backwards[2]. Dose-painted modulated radiotherapy with chemotherapy in anal cancer (52 evaluable): acute grade 3 or worse gastrointestinal toxicity 21% against 36% in the historical control, skin 23% against 49%, grade 2 or worse haematologic toxicity 73% against 85%[7]. The within-trial comparison in prostate cancer at 79.2 Gy (748 patients): the bladder and rectal volumes receiving 65, 70 and 75 Gy were all significantly lower with modulated radiotherapy, and combined acute gastrointestinal and urinary toxicity fell significantly as well[8]. The marrow-sparing box has a meta-analysis of 17 randomised trials and 1,297 patients: the odds ratio for grade 3 or worse haematologic toxicity was 0.39 (0.28 to 0.55) — an odds ratio is not a multiple of the probability, and below 1 means the event is less likely; the odds ratio for chemotherapy being interrupted because of blood counts was 0.44; and the authors say plainly that the data are insufficient to assess survival and that most of the trials included were single-centre[9]. This column is what the technique the National Health Insurance already pays for has actually delivered — real numbers with their cancer and their endpoint attached, not adjectives.

The column that did not improve, at the same length

Rectal cancer is the clearest counterexample. In the multicentre trial of preoperative chemoradiotherapy using modulated radiotherapy (68 evaluable) the primary endpoint was not met, 51.5% still had grade 2 or worse gastrointestinal toxicity, and the conclusion reads, in its own words, that using intensity-modulated radiotherapy in preoperative chemoradiotherapy for rectal cancer "did not reduce the rate of GI toxicity"[10]; the Irish phase II trial that randomly assigned the two techniques enrolled 94 people and was stopped early because its primary endpoint, acute gastrointestinal toxicity, showed futility at interim analysis, the authors concluding that the quality-of-life benefit from modulated radiotherapy was limited[11]. That much-quoted anal cancer trial also failed its primary endpoint: it was designed to reduce combined grade 2 or worse acute gastrointestinal and urinary toxicity by at least 15 percentage points against a historical control, and the result was 77% against 77%; what it won were the secondary endpoints[7].

Several other boxes are empty. Sexual function shows no difference: a secondary analysis of the same randomised gynaecological trial (236 patients) found no significant difference in patient-rated sexual function between the arms, and it was instead the people with abdominal pain after treatment whose sexual function was worse[12]. In prostate cancer the difference in late gastrointestinal toxicity is visible on univariable analysis (p=.039), but once put into a multivariable model only a 26% relative reduction is left, at p=.099, which is not significant; and for acute and late urinary toxicity the two techniques do not differ at all[8]. There is even one signal pointing the other way: in the American SEER-Medicare prostate cancer data, the modulated radiotherapy group was diagnosed with erectile dysfunction slightly more often (5.9 against 5.3 per 100 person-years, relative risk 1.12) — those are insurance diagnosis codes and subject to detection bias, but if I am going to write "did not improve" then it has to be listed[13].

And there is one fact that is easily overlooked. In the same gynaecological trial, 234 patients completed the patient-rated questionnaires, and the proportion of patients reporting high-grade diarrhoea was far higher than what the doctors recorded (the figure in the paper is 38.5%, with no statement of whether that is an absolute or a relative difference), with abdominal pain higher by 19.1%; and the difference between the two arms was visible only in the patients' own ratings, while the adverse events recorded by doctors showed no difference between the arms[14]. That sentence cuts both ways: if you do not say it, the improvement cannot be seen — and the boxes that did not improve do not become improvements just because nobody wrote them down.

Why it can be done: the numbers we watch while planning

The mechanism behind the bowel boxes is the dose-volume relationship. The threshold for small bowel comes with a proviso that has to be said in the same breath: the same organ gives completely different numbers depending on how it is contoured — contouring individual bowel loops gives V15 of 120 cc, while treating the whole peritoneal cavity as a bowel bag gives V45 of 195 cc[15]. The rectal box is the most complete: the rectal volume receiving 60 Gy or more is consistently associated with grade 2 or worse rectal toxicity, and the model parameters derived from four clinical series agree closely with each other[16]. The modern planning goals: the EMBRACE-II protocol sets bowel V40 within 250 cc and V30 within 500 cc[17]. Hospitals in Taiwan have done this too: a single-centre retrospective analysis of 93 patients having concurrent chemoradiotherapy for cervical cancer showed that the higher the rectal V30, the higher the odds of grade 2 or worse acute diarrhoea, at an odds ratio of 1.15[18].

These are targets to watch while planning, not promises. The prediction model grown out of that randomised trial (283 patients) shows bowel V30 and V40 both associated with acute and with late diarrhoea, but the model's own ability to tell one patient from another sits only between 0.588 and 0.761, and the authors say it needs external validation[19]. In plain words: a model like that can help a planner choose between two plans; it cannot foretell whether you will get diarrhoea.

The bladder box: we know less about it than about the bowel

That is not modesty; it is what the literature says about itself. The bladder chapter of QUANTEC, which collates dose-volume relationships, says in its own words that the data on partial-organ, non-uniform irradiation "are suspect", because they do not account for movement of the bladder itself and because many of the studies were not followed up for long enough[20]. So I am not going to give you a number for "at this bladder dose, this happens". If you hear one given in clinic, remember to ask where that number came from.

The cost side: the low-dose bath and second cancers

Tightening the high dose has a price: more normal tissue receives a low dose. In the pelvis it is measurable — intensity-modulated protons leave only about half as much tissue volume receiving low dose (within 10 CGE) as photon modulation does[21], which put the other way means the photon low-dose region is the larger one. Does that turn into a second cancer? The data that answer this are not protons against photons, but modulated radiotherapy against the older three-dimensional conformal — the low-dose region is larger in that comparison too, and there is long-term follow-up in a large population. The answer is no: the SEER-Medicare analysis in prostate cancer (65,235 two-year survivors) showed second haematologic malignancy unrelated to technique, and a hazard ratio of 0.91 for second primary solid cancers — and that difference appeared only in the earlier years of diagnosis, with 1.14 for the 2006 to 2010 period, its confidence interval crossing 1[22]. Modelling work points out that the choice of energy is more relevant than the name of the technique: pelvic modulated radiotherapy at 6 MV adds 6% to the cumulative second cancer risk relative to 3D, and 18 MV adds 26% — those are model estimates, not observed events[23].

Take this sentence to every promise you are offered

Back to the core of it: what you buy is side effects, not survival; and not every side effect, and not in every cancer. Next time someone introduces a technique to you, ask them to finish the sentence — which endpoint improved (survival? acute diarrhoea? late toxicity three years on?), in which cancer, and in a study of what design. Cervical cancer after surgery has a randomised trial behind it, and the acute gastrointestinal box in rectal cancer is empty; both of those are true at once. How the technical names sort into layers is in "Not three machines — three layers", and how far the proton layer has got is in "Protons in the pelvis: where the evidence stands"; whether the NHI pays and whether there is a self-paid difference is written up in the former as a shape, and the amount is always a question for the medical affairs office. At your next appointment you only have to say those two words out loud — which cancer, which endpoint — and we will take it from there.

From the physician

When you hear about the benefit of any technique, ask the person to finish the sentence: which endpoint improved, in which cancer, and in a study of what design. Cervical cancer after surgery has a randomised trial behind it, and the acute gastrointestinal box in rectal cancer is empty; both of those are true at the same time.
