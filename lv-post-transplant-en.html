Life after a liver transplant

Missed anti-rejection doses as everyone's default risk, follow-up tools switching to CT, the bill of a second cancer, and the living donor's own follow-up — the homework after transplant, laid out in one place.

This article also keeps a section for the person who gave you part of their liver: their follow-up is follow-up too.

After a liver transplant, the clinic questions change to a new set: how long do I take the drugs? How often do I get scanned? Can it still come back? This article lays out the days after transplant. Who is suitable for a transplant, what the criteria look like, how listing and ranking work — all of that is in "Transplant: the one option that treats both diseases" and is not repeated here. Here we only talk about life after the operation.

My position first: stereotactic body radiotherapy and proton therapy for liver cancer are treatments I deliver myself — and much of it is self-paid. The other side of every comparison in this topic is my colleagues' surgery and ablation. That is why every comparison comes with its primary source, so you can take it to the surgical and hepatology clinics for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

Anti-rejection drugs: not "will I forget?" but "everyone forgets"

Immunosuppressants are the foundation of life after transplant. A meta-analysis of 147 studies (Dew 2007, covering transplants of every organ): non-adherence to immunosuppressants runs at 19 to 25 cases per 100 people per year; liver recipients score lower than kidney recipients, but not zero; and the research could find almost no demographic feature that predicts who will miss doses[1]. The meaning: missing doses is not something that happens only to the weak-willed — it is everyone's default risk, to be defended against with systems (a pill box, alarms, a second layer of reminders from family), not gambled on personality. Which drugs go in the mix is the transplant team's professional judgement: the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) 2023 guidance notes that higher exposure to calcineurin inhibitors is associated with increased liver cancer recurrence, and that mTOR inhibitors appear to have anti-tumour properties — but the phase III SiLVER trial did not show, overall, that sirolimus improves long-term recurrence-free survival; only the within-criteria subgroup showed a signal[2] — so this is not a topic for a patient to take an article in and demand a switch; it is one the team weighs on trial evidence. On the Taiwan side: tacrolimus is explicitly listed in the NHI drug reimbursement regulations as first-line for liver transplantation, and cyclosporin is likewise listed as an anti-rejection drug for organ transplants[3]; for other immunosuppressants used in liver transplantation I could not find a dedicated reimbursement clause — please confirm your own combination with your transplant team and case manager.

A new liver does not end the cancer follow-up

First, a Taiwanese baseline: national statistics released by the Ministry of Health and Welfare in 2013 counted 2,623 liver transplants from 2001 to 2011, with post-operative survival of roughly 80% at three years, 76% at five and 70% at ten — an earlier era, not adjusted for pre-operative severity, so read the direction rather than the digits[4]. That baseline is not bad — but it assumes the homework afterwards gets done. The AASLD 2023 summary sentence: even within the Milan criteria, liver cancer still recurs after transplant in 10% to 15% of patients, and it is the most common cause of death in this population[2]. The earliest Milan report (1996, 48 patients, mainly within criteria) had a recurrence of only 8%[5]; the larger experience that followed settled into that 10%-to-15% band. The risk can be stratified: the RETREAT score, built on AFP, vascular invasion and tumour burden, estimates five-year recurrence risk from under 3% at the lowest score to 75% at the highest[2] — your transplant team can calculate yours, and it is worth asking for. The situation after a recurrence has to be told honestly: fewer than one in five are operable, immune checkpoint inhibitors are essentially unusable after transplant because of the rejection risk, and median survival after recurrence is about a year[2]. That is the reason to do the follow-up schedule in full.

The tools of follow-up change: CT takes over from ultrasound

The most common sites of recurrence after transplant are the lungs (about 40%) and the liver (about a third)[2], so the surveillance tools are multiphase abdominal CT or MRI plus chest CT — ultrasound cannot see the lungs, and in this square it leaves the stage. As for how often and for how long, the guidance once again admits "uncertain"[2] — as with "Follow-up and AFP: how often to scan", the schedule is a consensus, not a randomised-trial victory. One multicentre study found that scanning more often was "associated" with catching recurrences still amenable to curative treatment, and with better survival after recurrence[2] — an observational association, which I label as such and will not write up as cause and effect.

The bill for immunosuppression: a second cancer

Keeping immunity suppressed for years costs more than infections. A US study linking the transplant registry to cancer registries (Engels 2011; 175,732 solid-organ transplants, about 20% of them liver): overall cancer risk in transplant recipients was about 2.1 times that of the general population; non-Hodgkin lymphoma about 7.5 times; among liver recipients, lung cancer about 1.95 times and kidney cancer about 1.80 times; melanoma and lip cancer were also elevated[6]. Lip cancer and melanoma are tied to ultraviolet light — sun protection, and having a doctor look over your skin at intervals, are concrete actions that hang directly on this evidence; as for how often that skin check should be, I could not find a citable recommendation, and I will not invent a frequency for you — settle that one with your transplant team. As for smoking: put it next to that lung cancer line yourself.

Hepatitis B: indefinite after transplant

For hepatitis B patients, the antivirals after transplant are indefinite — the AASLD 2018 hepatitis B guidance in its own words: treatment after transplant should continue indefinitely, regardless of e-antigen or viral load status[7]; the NHI drug reimbursement regulations carry a corresponding provision, allowing preventive use for liver transplant recipients[8]. Why "there every day" matters so much, and what stopping on your own costs, the full numbers are in "The liver needs care for life after treatment ends". On the warning-signs side: for a transplant patient with fever, jaundice or abdominal pain, the first call is the transplant team — the list they give you outranks any general checklist; the general emergency list is in "When to come back to hospital the same day".

If your liver came from a living donor

Most liver transplants in Taiwan come from living donors (the number behind that is in "The days on the waiting list"), so many people reading this carry a liver that came from family. The donor's price is real: in the US multicentre A2ALL cohort, of 740 donors who completed donation, 40% had complications — the great majority mild, with 95% overall resolving within a year, but hernias (7%) and psychological complications (3%) can surface more than a year after donation[9]; a global survey covering 71 transplant programmes and 11,553 living liver donations found a donor mortality of 0.2% and an average complication rate of 24%[10]. These numbers are not there to make you feel guilty — donors have their own medical team and independent assessment, and the decision was made informed. What they mean is: the donor's follow-up is follow-up too. Their post-operative visits, their awareness of hernia symptoms, the rise and fall of their mood — these deserve a place in the same family calendar as your own appointments.

The first year's calendar after transplant is fuller than you'd think

Lay out the next twelve months. Drugs: fix the collection days and the drug-level blood draws, with two layers of reminders; report every missed dose honestly at clinic — the team wants information, not confession. Imaging: ask the transplant team for a schedule in black and white — which month, which scan, chest CT included or not. Skin: agree a frequency for skin checks with the transplant team, put them in the calendar, and make sun protection routine. The donor: their clinic dates go into the same calendar. Three questions to ask straight out at the next visit: what is my RETREAT score? What tools does my surveillance schedule use, and until when? My antivirals and my anti-rejection drugs — who is watching each?

From the physician

Take three questions to your next transplant clinic: what is my RETREAT score? What tools does my imaging schedule use, and until when? Who is watching my antivirals and my anti-rejection drugs, respectively? Report missed doses as they happened — the team wants information, not confession.
