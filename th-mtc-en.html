TWO OTHER DISEASES, AND TAIWAN

Medullary cancer is a different disease

It comes from another cell, so radioiodine does nothing, TSH suppression is pointless, and follow-up watches a different number entirely; and its guideline has gone eleven years without a revision.

Thyroid Cancer Guide · Two other diseases, and Taiwan · 13 references
#Genetics & Family#Biomarkers#Surgery#Targeted Therapy

Most people handed the report are preparing by somebody else's papillary experience. But medullary carcinoma is followed on calcitonin and carcinoembryonic antigen, and the adrenal gland has to be cleared before the thyroid comes out; as for the inherited part, that decision is yours, and it is not in any article.

The hardest sentence to get out in clinic: what you have is not the same disease as the thyroid cancer you have been reading about. Most people handed a medullary report have already worked through somebody else's account — radioiodine (I-131), thyroglobulin, a tablet dose kept high to hold recurrence down. None of those three applies to you.

This article: which cell it grows from, which two numbers follow it, why other blood goes off before the operation is booked, the inherited part, and the drugs. What the National Health Insurance pays for, and what has no entry at all, is in 〈How this goes in Taiwan〉. Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma is a third disease again, in 〈Anaplastic cancer is an emergency〉.

A different cell, so a different disease

Papillary thyroid carcinoma and follicular thyroid carcinoma grow from the gland's follicular cells, which take up iodine, make thyroid hormone and secrete thyroglobulin. Medullary carcinoma does not. It grows from the parafollicular C cells wedged between the follicles, and those secrete calcitonin and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA).

One sentence, three consequences. Radioiodine does nothing for medullary carcinoma, because C cells do not take up iodine. Pushing TSH down is pointless too; this tumour does not grow to that signal, and the tablet afterwards only puts back what your body needs. Follow-up measures calcitonin and CEA, not thyroglobulin. The pages here on radioiodine and TSH suppression, you can set down.

It is rare. In Taiwan's cancer registry report for 2023, medullary carcinoma accounts for 19 men and 29 women, 48 in the year: 1.26% of the male and 0.64% of the female thyroid malignancies recorded[1]. The official statistics stop at that count. Survival for medullary carcinoma in Taiwan, and what proportion is inherited, are no entry found — not that I failed to look, but that the report carries no such item.

Why other blood goes off before the operation

A medullary diagnosis often does not go straight onto the operating list, and people read that as delay. The reason is in the guideline; the 2015 edition's own words are "a PHEO should be excluded prior to thyroidectomy" — a phaeochromocytoma has to be ruled out before the thyroid comes out[2].

A phaeochromocytoma sits on the adrenal gland and releases, in bursts, hormones that drive blood pressure up. If one is still there, the stimulus of anaesthesia and surgery can push that pressure to a dangerous height. Those tubes of blood and that scan of the abdomen are not extras. They are the order being put right.

It turns up alongside medullary carcinoma because of an inherited make-up called multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (MEN2). MEN2A is medullary carcinoma plus a phaeochromocytoma, or primary hyperparathyroidism, or both; MEN2B begins earlier, behaves more aggressively, and nearly everyone carrying it develops medullary carcinoma[3].

The inherited part: keep two kinds of mutation apart

About three-quarters of medullary carcinoma is sporadic, about a quarter inherited. The inherited quarter comes almost entirely from a germline mutation in a gene called RET, passed on in an autosomal dominant pattern with close to complete penetrance[3]. Germline means every cell in your body carries it, a blood test can find it, and there is a question about passing it on.

The next sentence is the one people read backwards. Among sporadic medullary carcinomas, roughly half — one review writes 50%, another writes 50% to 80% — have a RET mutation that can be found in the tumour cells[4][5]. That is a somatic mutation inside the tumour. It exists only in that lump, and it does not go to your children. "Half of medullary cancers carry a RET mutation" and "half of medullary cancers are inherited" are two different sentences.

What genetic counselling asks, and the word "offered"

Two of the guideline's own sentences show where it stands on germline testing. One is "Patients with presumed sporadic MTC should have genetic testing to detect a germline RET mutation" — testing is on the table even for cases that look sporadic, because looking sporadic is not being sporadic. The other begins "Genetic counseling and genetic testing for RET germline mutations should be offered to"[2]. I want to stop on that word, offered. Offered and explained, not required. The decision is not in this article, and it is not with me.

What counselling asks runs about like this. Who in the family had what, at what age, thyroid and adrenal and parathyroid above all. Whether anyone has episodes of high blood pressure, palpitations, sweating. Whether anyone had thyroid surgery very young. Whether you have children, and whether you intend to have them. The guideline also lists pre-implantation or prenatal diagnostic testing, in those words, among the options to be talked through[2].

On whether family should be told, what the guideline addresses is the doctor's duty to the patient in front of him, phrased as "The duty to warn a competent and capacitated patient"[2]. The direction of that is me putting it plainly to you, not me contacting anybody. Whether to say it, when, and to whom is yours, and you can take your time.

Who carries the consequence of not testing, I will say plainly too. By where the RET mutation sits, the guideline sorts carriers into three levels — a scheme I can only take second-hand from a review — highest risk is M918T, high risk is codon 634, and the other pathogenic variants are moderate[4]. For the high-risk box its own sentence is "Children in the ATA-H category should have a thyroidectomy performed at age 5 years or earlier"; for the moderate box, physical examination, neck ultrasound and calcitonin from age five[2]. The highest-risk box is earlier still, and there I could not obtain the guideline's own sentence, only a review's account: surgery within the first year of life[3]. I set it down so you know what is behind that door. It is not an instruction to knock on anyone's.

Why nobody calls it a rise off one blood test

Follow-up after surgery watches calcitonin and CEA. What predicts is not a single high or low reading. It is the doubling time.

A French database followed 65 people who had had a total thyroidectomy with bilateral node dissection and whose calcitonin was still abnormal afterwards. Of the 12 whose doubling time was under 6 months, 3 were alive at five years (25%) and 1 at ten (8%); of those between 6 months and 2 years, 11 of 12 at five years (92%) and 3 of 8 at ten (37%); all 41 with a doubling time longer than 2 years were alive when the study closed. In the multivariable analysis doubling time was the only independent predictor; TNM stage and the EORTC score were significant on univariable analysis and stopped being significant once they went into the multivariable model[6]. That set of figures has an edge: it belongs to people left with biochemical disease after surgery, and does not carry across to someone whose calcitonin is undetectable once the operation is over.

A meta-analysis of ten studies adds a line: the CEA doubling time predicts better than the calcitonin one, which is why both get drawn[7]. I will not call something a rise off one draw. It is tied to the grade on the pathology report too: in the high-grade group the calcitonin doubling time ran 8.51 plus or minus 3.22 months, in the low-grade group 38.42 plus or minus 11.19; 66.7% of the high-grade group doubled in under a year, against 1.0% of the low-grade[8].

The drugs: four of them, and what "selective" buys

One term first: the response rate is the proportion of people whose tumour shrinks measurably.

The first generation hits many targets at once. The phase III trial of vandetanib randomised 331 people against placebo (90% sporadic, 95% metastatic): hazard ratio for progression-free survival 0.46 (95% confidence interval 0.31 to 0.69), with the side effects alongside — diarrhoea 56% against 26%, hypertension 32% against 5%. That paper reported no median, and I will not supply one[9]. The phase III trial of cabozantinib randomised 330 people with metastatic medullary carcinoma and radiologically documented progression: median progression-free survival 11.2 against 4.0 months, hazard ratio 0.28 (0.19 to 0.40), response rate 28% against 0%; the cost, dose reduction in 79% and interruption in 65%[10].

The second generation is the selective RET inhibitors, which mostly hit RET alone. The phase III trial of selpercatinib is the only one in this disease to put a new drug head to head against the old: 291 people with RET-mutant, progressive medullary carcinoma, the comparator not placebo but cabozantinib or vandetanib by physician's choice. At a median 12 months the new drug's median progression-free survival had not been reached; the comparator's was 16.8 months (95% confidence interval 12.2 to 25.1), hazard ratio 0.28 (0.16 to 0.48); response rate 69.4% against 38.8%. Discontinuation for adverse events was 4.7% against 26.8%, and dose reduction 38.9% against 77.3%[11]. That pair of discontinuation figures is what the word selective means. One thing to hold: the comparator arm could cross over to the new drug on progression, so the overall survival numbers have to be read with that in view.

Pralsetinib ran single-arm, no comparator, reporting response rate alone: in previously untreated RET-mutant medullary carcinoma 15 of 21 responded (71%, 95% confidence interval 48 to 89), and among those already given cabozantinib or vandetanib 33 of 55 (60%, 46 to 73)[12]. An updated analysis moved the cut-off date and the denominators, giving 77.4% untreated and 55.7% previously treated — two sets that cannot go in one sentence[13].

Which drug's reimbursement text in Taiwan names which histological type, I deliberately leave out here. Those clauses are easy to get the wrong way round, and they are transcribed word for word in 〈How this goes in Taiwan〉. Before any of them starts, hold that text against what you are being offered.

A guideline eleven years without a revision

The American Thyroid Association guideline still citable for medullary carcinoma is the 2015 one: sixty-seven recommendations, graded A to F on a scheme adapted from the United States Preventive Services Task Force[2]. The differentiated thyroid cancer book was rewritten in 2025, the anaplastic one in 2021. Three histological types, three books, three years, three grading schemes.

Eleven years is not a small gap. Two selective RET inhibitors and a head-to-head phase III trial arrived inside it. So you will meet places where the guideline says nothing and your doctors are discussing something anyway; that is usually not improvisation, it is the book not having caught up. It runs the other way too — some of what the guideline describes may be older than what is done now.

One more thing straight. I could not get the full text of that guideline; the publisher's end blocked it. Every sentence marked above as its own words was checked phrase by phrase against a full-text index to confirm it is there. I have written no recommendation numbers and no grades, because I could not verify them.

The one item to ask about before surgery

The one that must not be missed is the phaeochromocytoma. Put it first; take the other four in with it:

  • "Before surgery, has the phaeochromocytoma been ruled out? What did it show?"
  • "My calcitonin and CEA — what were they last time and this time? How far apart were the draws? Is there enough to work out a doubling time?"
  • "Do I need germline RET testing? If I do, who talks it through with me, and where?"
  • "Does my report say high grade or low grade? Does that change how often I am seen?"
  • "If drugs are needed later, in what order would they come?"

And one I would rather you asked early: "The numbers my follow-up watches are not the ones other thyroid cancer patients watch — so which of what I find online does not apply to me?"

From the physician

Read this out at your next appointment: "My calcitonin and CEA — what were they last time and this time, how far apart were the draws, and is there enough to work out a doubling time?" Then two more: "Before surgery, has the phaeochromocytoma been ruled out?" and "Do I need germline RET testing, and if so, who talks it through with me and where?" Before deciding, ask one more: "If I do not test today, can I still test later? Is there a time limit?"
