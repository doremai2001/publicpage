HYPERTHERMIA: THE EVIDENCE, SITE BY SITE

Sarcoma, melanoma, and the thin end of the evidence

The soft tissue sarcoma phase 3 trial is the fulcrum of this field. The other sites have been tried, and the data will not carry a conclusion.

Next-Generation Therapy Guide · Hyperthermia: the evidence, site by site · 9 references
#Hyperthermia#Evidence

This article comes in two halves. The first is the strongest piece of evidence hyperthermia has — a phase 3 randomised trial that recruited for nine years and followed patients for eleven. The second is a run of cancer sites often produced in support of hyperthermia whose data will not actually carry a conclusion. Read both halves.

Warning 1 (what this displaces): Most of the treatments in this topic are paid out of pocket, and most sit outside standard treatment. Before you decide, you need to know that what they displace is usually not time but three things of real value: standard treatment you have not yet used up, money you will need for later lines, and your eligibility for clinical trials (many trials exclude people who have recently received other experimental treatments). And there is one more, harder to see — physical reserve. The strength burnt through in a course that does not work may no longer be there when a genuinely useful option appears. So the question is not whether this is worth trying — it is what you give up by doing it.

Having hyperthermia at a cancer site where the evidence is thin means trading a certain cost — money, time, physical reserve — for an uncertain gain. That trade is not necessarily a bad one, but you should know what you are trading.

Warning 2 (finding the right doctor): Do not make this kind of decision alone, and do not make it on the word of the person offering the treatment. The doctor you are looking for is one willing to tell you "this is not right for you" — if they never once say who this is NOT for, that is not a consultation, it is a sale.

My position first: proton therapy and hyperthermia are both technologies within my own field, and the hospital I work at has the equipment for them. So I draw every line in this topic tighter, not looser.

This article gives the strongest single piece first, and then thins out honestly.

Soft tissue sarcoma: the one large positive phase 3 trial in this field

EORTC 62961-ESHO 95: from July 1997 to November 2006, nine centres across Europe and North America recruited patients with localised high-risk soft tissue sarcoma (tumour 5 cm or larger, grade 2 or 3, sitting deep to the fascia). They were randomised to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (doxorubicin, ifosfamide, etoposide) alone or with regional hyperthermia added. The primary endpoint was local progression-free survival, analysed by intention to treat[1].

The long-term results: 341 patients randomised in all, 329 included in the intention-to-treat analysis, median age 51, median follow-up 11.3 years. By December 2014, 220 patients (67%) had relapsed and 188 (57%) had died. Adding regional hyperthermia improved local progression-free survival[2].

Why does this one matter so much? Because it has all three of the things every other study in this field is missing: enough size (over three hundred patients), long enough follow-up (eleven years), and a phase 3 randomised design analysed by intention to treat. This is the fulcrum on which whether hyperthermia gets taken seriously rests.

But the scope has to be stated too. What it did was neoadjuvant chemotherapy plus hyperthermia, not radiotherapy plus hyperthermia; and the patients had deep, larger than 5 cm, high-grade soft tissue sarcoma — not any kind of sarcoma, and certainly not any other cancer site.

Melanoma: a randomised trial from the 1990s

The European Society for Hyperthermic Oncology's trial: 134 recurrent or metastatic melanoma lesions in 70 patients, randomised to radiotherapy alone (three fractions within eight days, 8 to 9 Gy each) or radiotherapy followed by hyperthermia (43 °C for 60 minutes). Two-year local control was 37% overall; 46% in the heated group against 28% with radiotherapy alone (p = 0.008). In multivariable analysis hyperthermia remained an important prognostic factor (odds ratio 1.73). Heating did not appreciably increase acute or late radiation reactions[3].

What gets quoted from this trial is that local control gap, but it recorded something else at the same time: because of equipment difficulties, only 14% of treatments reached the prescribed thermal dose. The two things have to be said together.

Brain tumours: two sets of data pointing different ways

Glioblastoma has a prospective randomised trial: a brachytherapy boost with or without interstitial hyperthermia. 79 patients were randomised (39 without heat, 40 with). On intention-to-treat analysis, both time to progression and survival were longer in the heated group (p = 0.04 for each); among the 68 patients who actually received the boost, median survival was 85 weeks against 76 weeks and two-year survival 31% against 15%, with a hazard ratio of 0.51 after multivariable adjustment for age and performance status (p = 0.008). Grade 3 toxicity occurred in six patients in the heated group and one in the control group[4]. Look carefully at the denominators: 112 patients were registered at the time, 33 of whom never reached randomisation at all, and that two-year survival figure is calculated from the 68 who actually received the boost. This is a positive result, but the standard treatment of glioblastoma in that era is nothing like today's — a brachytherapy boost is no longer routine.

The other route is magnetic nanoparticles: iron oxide particles are injected into the tumour and heated with an alternating magnetic field, combined with fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy (median 30 Gy). In 59 patients with recurrent glioblastoma, median overall survival from the diagnosis of recurrence was 13.4 months[5]. This is a single-arm study, with no control group.

Oesophagus, lung, ovary: it has been tried, and the data will not hold a conclusion

Oesophageal cancer has a prospective randomised study: with hyperthermia added, the proportion in whom no viable cancer cells could be found in the resected specimen was 25% against 5.9% (p < 0.05), and three-year survival 50.4% against 24.2%[6]. The gap looks large, but the two arms together come to only sixty-odd people (32 heated, 34 control), and at that size one or two deaths move the percentage by several points. This was also published in 1995, and the treatment of oesophageal cancer has changed enormously across those thirty years.

Non-small cell lung cancer has a retrospective study: 93 patients with stage IIIB to IV disease, 48 heated and 45 on chemotherapy alone; among those with a malignant pleural effusion, the overall response rate of the effusion was 81.2% against 40% (p = 0.046)[7]. Retrospective, single centre, and the endpoint is the response of the effusion rather than survival.

Recurrent ovarian cancer has a pilot study: 36 patients had regional abdominal hyperthermia plus systemic chemotherapy, more than eight in ten of them platinum-resistant. Complete response 2.8%, partial response 33.3%, progression 44.4%; 30.5% stopped treatment because of toxicity; median overall survival 12 months[8]. The authors' own conclusion is that the combination is feasible and that a phase 3 trial is needed to assess the benefit. That sentence is worth copying out in full — “feasible” is not “effective”.

Why the evidence is distributed the way it is

Having read the above you may ask: why do some cancer sites get randomised trials and others only retrospective data? Part of the answer has nothing to do with efficacy. The sarcoma trial got done because there was an international collaborative structure and the funding to go with it at the time; superficial lesions — melanoma, the chest wall — are easy to heat and easy to measure, which makes research on them easier[9]; deep and visceral tumours are hard to heat, hard to measure, and far more expensive to study.

So “this cancer site has less evidence” sometimes means “this cancer site is harder to study”, and not necessarily “this cancer site responds less”. But that cannot be run in reverse — it does not amount to a reason for any cancer site to skip the evidence. It is only a reminder that which cancer site has more research is not the same as which cancer site it works better in.

How to read the article as a whole

The sarcoma piece is real, and it carries real weight. But it does not spread by itself to other cancer sites — at every site above, hyperthermia has been tried and has left data behind, and most of that data has only answered whether it is safe and whether it can be done, not what you get in exchange for adding it.

If your cancer site is in the second half of this article, the reasonable way to have the discussion is not “does it work” but three questions: in this situation, does standard treatment still have options left? Is there a clinical trial recruiting? And if the answer to both is no, how much time, money and physical reserve am I willing to trade for an uncertain chance? Only you can answer the third one, but the first two should be answered by your treating team.
