DIAGNOSIS AND STAGING

Bleeding after the menopause is not something to wait out

Of the women assessed in clinic for postmenopausal bleeding, about one in ten is eventually diagnosed with endometrial cancer; "most people do not have cancer" and "this symptom should not be left" are two sentences that can both be true at once.

I am not going to talk you into it by saying that leaving it will make it advanced — the evidence for that causal link is weak and inconsistent. What holds the timing up is three harder things: this group's denominator, the way endometrial cancer shows itself, and the fact that one outpatient test gives an answer. Along the way, the misunderstanding that a normal thickness on ultrasound means you can relax gets taken apart.

Bleeding again, several years after the menopause. The first thing I hear in clinic is almost always the same sentence: "It was hardly anything, one wipe and it was gone, it's probably nothing." Most people who get it checked do turn out not to have endometrial cancer. I will still ask you to have it settled this month. The reason has nothing to do with being afraid it will turn into advanced disease — this symptom gives you an answer from a single test, and waiting buys you nothing.

Nine in ten endometrial cancers are picked up because of bleeding

The denominator first. A meta-analysis of 129 studies covering 34,432 women with postmenopausal bleeding and 6,358 women with endometrial cancer, 40,790 women in total, produced two figures pointing in opposite directions: among those 6,358 women with endometrial cancer, 91% (95% confidence interval 87% to 93%) had postmenopausal bleeding as a presenting feature; the other way round, among those 34,432 women with postmenopausal bleeding, 9% (8% to 11%) were eventually diagnosed with endometrial cancer[1]. A confidence interval, in plain language, is the range the true answer would probably sit in if the same study were run many times over.

9% is not a fixed number. Depending on whether hormone therapy was being used it splits in two: 7% (6% to 9%) in women who were using hormone therapy, 12% (9% to 15%) in those who were not; by region, 5% in North America and 13% in Western Europe[1]. And this is the denominator for women who have already come in because of bleeding, not a whole-population risk. The authors' own conclusion is also two sentences placed side by side: this symptom offers a chance to catch as many as nine in ten endometrial cancers, and most women who have it do not turn out to have cancer[1]. Both are true, and dropping either one bends the reading.

"Will leaving it make it advanced?" — the evidence here is weaker than you would think

I don't use that argument on you, because it does not stand up. FIGO's 2025 report on cancer of the uterine corpus says it outright: the effect of waiting time for surgical staging on survival outcome "is controversial"; and looking only at low-grade endometrial cancer, waiting time "was not associated" with worse survival[2]. Some studies have seen delay go with worse disease-free survival and a greater need for radiotherapy; others have seen no difference in overall survival — weak and inconsistent, and I am not going to use it to frighten anyone.

"Not something to wait out" rests on three other pillars: about one in ten of this group has cancer to begin with[1]; nine in ten women with endometrial cancer present with postmenopausal bleeding[1]; and settling the question takes one outpatient test. Not one of those three needs a scare attached to it.

Ultrasound tells us whether to take tissue today; it is not a gate that lets you leave

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists put it plainly in its 2018 committee opinion: transvaginal ultrasonography usually is sufficient for an initial evaluation of postmenopausal bleeding if the endometrial echo is thin (less than or equal to 4 mm), given that an endometrial thickness of 4 mm or less has a greater than 99% negative predictive value for endometrial cancer[3]. Negative predictive value, in plain language: of the people whose test says "this does not look like it", the proportion who really do not have it. That sentence can be quoted, but it has to be read together with what follows.

A meta-analysis of 44 studies, 17,339 women with postmenopausal bleeding and 1,341 endometrial cancers (7.7%): at a 5 mm threshold, sensitivity was 96.2% (the proportion of people with the disease flagged as abnormal) and specificity only 51.5% (the proportion of people without it flagged as normal)[4]. An earlier meta-analysis of 35 studies and 5,892 women showed that specificity is dragged down by hormones: 92% in women not on hormone therapy, falling to 77% in those on it[5].

What matters more are two sets of figures pointing the other way. A prospective Mayo Clinic cohort of 1,163 women found that in women with a first episode of postmenopausal bleeding, the group the algorithm sends for ultrasound first, endometrial thickness barely separated high from low risk: the risk of cancer or endometrial intraepithelial neoplasia was 5.8% (1.3 to 10.3) above 4 mm and 3.6% (0.9 to 8.6) at 4 mm or less[6]. A separate study in Black women, 1,494 of them including 210 endometrial cancers, measured the miss rate directly: the false-negative probability at a 5 mm threshold was 11.4%, and 12.4% in the postmenopausal-bleeding subgroup[7]. This is a study in one population, and it must not be read as "everyone misses one in ten"; what it does say is that the miss rate of a threshold varies between populations, and that it has already been measured.

So here is how I use it in the room: ultrasound helps decide whether to take tissue the same day, or to go straight to booking a hysteroscopy. It is not a pass that reads "thickness normal, go home and watch". The same committee opinion also states that transvaginal ultrasonography is not an appropriate screening tool for endometrial cancer in postmenopausal women without bleeding[3].

If all you have had is an ultrasound, the report says the lining is thin, and the bleeding is still there or has come back, that is not a closed case; it is a reason to go back. Persistent or recurrent postmenopausal bleeding needs tissue: office sampling or hysteroscopy, with the gynaecologist deciding which.

Age and "it has happened again" separate risk better than thickness does

In the same Mayo cohort, among 593 women with postmenopausal bleeding there were 18 cases of endometrial intraepithelial neoplasia (3.0%) and 47 endometrial cancers (7.9%); the two highest-risk boxes were not the ones ultrasound draws, they were age 60 or over (17.7%, 13.0 to 22.3) and recurrent bleeding (14.7%, 11.0 to 18.3)[6].

"It was checked this time and it was fine" does not mean it can be left alone from now on either. A follow-up study of 222 women enrolled with a lining thicker than 4 mm, a benign sampling result, and cancer already excluded by hysteroscopy or curettage found that 21% (16 to 27) bled again during follow-up, at a median of 49 weeks, and two of those who bled again were later diagnosed with atypical hyperplasia[8]. Bleeding a second time does not put the risk back where it started — that 14.7% in the paragraph above is exactly how it is worked out; it is a reason to take tissue again, not a reason to wait again.

Two ways of taking a biopsy, and what each of them misses

FIGO's 2025 report can be quoted directly here: office-based endometrial sampling (for instance with a Pipelle-type fine cannula) is highly sensitive (90% to 100%) and specific (98% to 100%) for detecting endometrial cancer, but inadequate or failed sampling occurs at rates of up to 42%, caused by scanty samples, cervical stenosis or intolerable pain; hysteroscopy allows the focal lesions that blind sampling misses to be seen and biopsied under direct vision[2].

Change the comparator and the numbers change with it. A meta-analysis of 12 studies and 1,029 women with postmenopausal bleeding: against dilatation and curettage, office sampling detected endometrial cancer with 100% sensitivity; against hysteroscopy plus pathology, sensitivity fell to 90% (50 to 100), atypical hyperplasia to 82%, and if benign polyps are counted as "endometrial disease" it was only 39%. In the same analysis, sampling failed in 11% and was inadequate in 31%, and 7% of those women were eventually found to have endometrial cancer or a precancer[9]. That is why ACOG writes that if blind sampling does not reveal hyperplasia or malignancy, further testing such as hysteroscopy with dilatation and curettage is warranted in women with persistent or recurrent bleeding[3]. Between "the biopsy was benign" and "it has been ruled out" there is still some distance.

Abnormal bleeding before the menopause does not look the same

Younger women with endometrial cancer will not recognise themselves in the phrase "postmenopausal bleeding". What FIGO's 2025 report says is that premenopausal women with endometrial cancer experience infrequent periods or none at all, bleeding between periods, and heavy menstrual bleeding[2]. The denominator really is much smaller — in that Mayo cohort, among 570 women aged 45 or over with abnormal bleeding who had not yet gone through the menopause, there were 8 cases of endometrial intraepithelial neoplasia (1.4%) and 7 endometrial cancers (1.2%)[6] — small, but not zero.

ACOG's list of risk factors is worth reading alongside that: age, obesity, use of unopposed oestrogen, polycystic ovary syndrome, type 2 diabetes mellitus, atypical glandular cells on cervical cytology, and a family history of gynaecological malignancy[3]. Women taking tamoxifen for breast cancer are also on the list of people who get followed; I am not giving numbers here. The weight and diabetes thread is written up in "Weight, diabetes and this cancer"; if bowel and endometrial cancers cluster in your family, see "Lynch syndrome: what the test means for you and your family".

The Taiwan side: this is something an outpatient clinic can finish

The National Health Insurance fee schedule has item 55002C, "endometrial biopsy (not including pathological examination)", 1,163 points, and on the pathology side item 25004C, "level IV surgical pathology", 1,741 points, whose item description covers endometrial curettage and biopsy[10]. In other words, outpatient endometrial sampling is itself a listed procedure. Hysteroscopy is item 28022C, "hysteroscopic examination", 2,034 points, and its note is a frequency rule, which reads verbatim (my translation of the clause): "1. Where the result of the hysteroscopic examination is normal, it is limited to once a year at the same institution. 2. Where the result is abnormal and is managed with medication or observation, follow-up is limited to one occasion, at an interval of at least three months. 3. Where hysteroscopic surgical treatment has been performed, a follow-up examination may be carried out within three months after surgery; if the result is normal, it is limited to one occasion."[10] That rule and "should you have this done today" are not the same question; whether it can actually be claimed is one for the medical affairs office or your case manager. The Ministry of Health and Welfare has issued its own press release on this subject[11], so you are not the only one worrying about it.

Take these sentences into the room with you on the day

  • "I went through the menopause some years ago — how many episodes of bleeding is this now?" Be precise about the count and the timing; recurrent bleeding is a risk box in its own right[6].

  • "Am I on hormones, and in what form?" It changes how the ultrasound should be read[5].

  • "Is today an ultrasound only, or sampling at the same time? If it is ultrasound only, what would bring me back?"

  • "If the biopsy comes back benign but I bleed again, what is the next step?" The word "hysteroscopy" should appear in the answer[3].

Ask those four and you leave with a thread you can follow, rather than a "let's keep an eye on it". What actually holds people up is usually not the medical process; it is the thought that it can wait until next time.

From the physician

The person I least want to meet is the one who comes in six months after the bleeding started, having been told on the way that a little bleeding is normal at her age. Write down how many episodes and when, and take that with you; and push the question "is today ultrasound only, or sampling too?" until you get an answer. If the biopsy was benign and you bleed again, go back.
