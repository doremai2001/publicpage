DURING TREATMENT

When to come back to hospital the same day

One list, three actions: contact the team the same day, come back to hospital the same day, call an ambulance now. Every line has a source — and the most important one depends on your family noticing it for you.

Liver Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 12 references
#Side Effects#Daily Life#Liver Function

The ordering of this list is deliberate: the four emergencies of cirrhosis itself come before any drug side effect, because they do not care which treatment you are on — they apply to every liver cancer patient. When you have read it, copy out the lines that are yours and put them up somewhere at home where they can be seen.

Please read this one with your family. It has no comforting function — it is a checklist, and I want it read by more than the patient alone, because the most important first item on the list is often something the patient cannot notice in themselves. The whole article contains only three actions: contact the treatment team the same day, come back to hospital the same day, call an ambulance now. Not one item is "stop the drug yourself" or "take something to damp it down yourself" — stopping or adjusting a drug is always the team's decision; and by the same logic, the antivirals for hepatitis B and C must never be stopped on your own for even a day — the reasons are in "Hepatitis B and C: the antivirals matter more now" and "The liver needs care for life after treatment ends".

My position first: stereotactic body radiotherapy and proton therapy for liver cancer are treatments I deliver myself — and much of it is self-paid. The other side of every comparison in this topic is my colleagues' surgery and ablation. That is why every comparison comes with its primary source, so you can take it to the surgical and hepatology clinics for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

"He hasn't been himself lately" — a family's instinct is a warning sign

Hepatic encephalopathy is the state in which the brain of a person with cirrhosis is affected by ammonia and other toxins in the blood, and its grade 1 symptoms are subtle enough that even doctors struggle to confirm them objectively: the joint 2014 guideline of the AASLD and the European Association for the Study of the Liver lists grade 1 as trivial lack of awareness, euphoria or anxiety, shortened attention span, trouble with simple addition and subtraction, and altered sleep rhythm — and it says outright that the early personality changes are often noticed first by family[1]. So when a family member says "he hasn't been himself lately", that is a sentence I take seriously in my clinic, not small talk. Remember the thresholds this way: sleep turned upside down, simple sums not coming out, a changed personality — bring the clinic visit forward; not knowing what day of the week it is, hands flapping like a bird's wings when the arms are held out (asterixis), hard to rouse — back to hospital the same day. The international consensus the guideline cites likewise takes the loss of orientation in time, or the appearance of asterixis, as the starting point of overt encephalopathy[1].

Black stools and vomiting blood: not an appointment problem — an ambulance problem

Cirrhosis balloons the veins of the oesophagus and stomach into varices, and a ruptured, bleeding varix is the emergency among emergencies. The AASLD's 2024 portal hypertension guidance puts it without a trace of ambiguity: acute variceal haemorrhage remains an emergency of cirrhosis, and even with modern treatment the six-week mortality is still 10% to 15%; large varices bleed at a rate of about 15% a year[2]. So there is only one rule: tar-black stools, vomiting blood, vomiting something like coffee grounds — call an ambulance now, do not drive yourself, do not wait for morning. The same guidance shows the deaths cluster in the people with the worst liver function, and mortality is far lower where liver function is still good[2] — that sentence means "speed helps", not "you can wait and see".

A fever in someone with ascites is, on its own, enough

Ascites is not just distension: it can become infected on its own, which is called spontaneous bacterial peritonitis. The European Association for the Study of the Liver's 2018 guideline on decompensated cirrhosis lists the presentations — abdominal pain, tenderness, vomiting, diarrhoea, fever or chills, rigors — but warns at the same time that it can be entirely symptomless, especially in outpatients[3]. In other words, the combination "ascites (or a recent ascites tap) plus fever, chills or new abdominal pain" is by itself a reason to come back to hospital the same day — and expect to have ascitic fluid drawn for testing: the data the guideline cites show that a diagnostic paracentesis delayed more than 12 hours after admission carries a 2.7-fold increase in mortality, and antibiotics must start as soon as the diagnosis is made[3]. The same guideline sets ascites, bleeding, encephalopathy and jaundice side by side as the four signals of decompensating cirrhosis[3] — so the fourth rule is: whites of the eyes and skin visibly yellowing over a few days, urine turning tea-coloured, especially together with a swelling belly or growing drowsiness — back to hospital the same day.

"At what temperature should I come back?" — the definition I give has a source

Many patient leaflets say "seek care at 38 degrees", but few tell you where that number comes from. What I cite is the definition in the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guideline on fever in patients on cancer treatment: a single oral temperature of 38.3°C or above, or 38.0°C or above sustained for an hour[4]; if your team's threshold differs, follow your team. Targeted therapy and immunotherapy for liver cancer are not marrow-suppressing in the way chemotherapy is, but a fever during treatment can no more be shrugged off — especially if you are one of the people in the next two sections with ascites or varices.

On atezolizumab plus bevacizumab? Memorise this one number first

In its pivotal trial this combination had a fatal adverse reaction rate of 4.6%, and the leading cause of those deaths was gastrointestinal and oesophageal variceal bleeding (1.2%); serious adverse reactions ran at 38%, the commonest being gastrointestinal bleeding at 7%[5]. So the black-stools-and-vomiting-blood rule is in double bold type for you; the full reasons why an endoscopy must come before this treatment are in "Life on targeted therapy and immunotherapy". The bevacizumab half has a few more lines to memorise: gastrointestinal perforation occurs in 0.3% to 3%, mostly within 50 days of the first dose, presenting as abdominal pain, possibly with fever or a rigid abdomen — back to hospital the same day; tell your oncologist proactively about any scheduled operation (including a tooth extraction), because the drug must be stopped for at least 28 days either side of elective surgery; severe headache, visual changes and seizures are the faces of hypertensive crisis and reversible encephalopathy — contact the team at once[6]. The atezolizumab half is immunotherapy, and its side effects can arise in any organ: as a single agent the label lists immune-mediated pneumonitis 3%, colitis 1%, hepatitis 1.8%, adrenal insufficiency 0.4% — none looks high on its own, but any one of them can be fatal[5]. In plain words, the thresholds are: new breathlessness or a worsening cough, watery or bloody stools several times a day, yellowing eyes, exhaustion too deep to stand up, severe headache or a missing patch of vision — contact the treatment team the same day, and do not take antidiarrhoeals or fever reducers to push through it; that buries the signal.

Taking lenvatinib or sorafenib: a blood pressure monitor is part of your kit

Lenvatinib's rate of hypertension in its pivotal liver cancer trial was 45% (all figures in this paragraph are label summaries), and the median time to new or worsening hypertension was 26 days after starting the drug — so measuring your blood pressure at home in the first few weeks is not a ritual, it is monitoring; bleeding events 23%, grade 3 to 5 bleeding in the trial 5% including 7 deaths; hepatic encephalopathy 8% (against 3% on the comparator); hand-foot skin reaction 27%[7]. Sorafenib's hand-foot skin reaction runs at 21%, usually in the first six weeks, and the label itself requires weekly blood pressure checks for those six weeks; variceal bleeding 2.4% — the bleeding risk belongs to the cirrhosis itself and does not go away because you are on a tablet[8]. Same-day contact thresholds: blood pressure the medication cannot hold down, any black stools, blood in the urine or coughed-up blood, hands or feet broken and too painful to walk on, sudden drowsiness. Hands and feet "not yet broken but hurting more and more" is something to raise proactively at the next visit — the best moment for a dose reduction is before the skin breaks.

After TACE and after SBRT: each aftermath has its own warnings

In the first days after embolisation (TACE), fever, poor appetite and nausea are extremely common: in the placebo arm of a Japanese randomised trial, only 10.2% of patients had none of those three symptoms in the five days after the procedure — meaning about 90% had at least one; it is the embolised tumour dying, and it mostly settles on its own[9]. What you are watching for is a fever going the wrong way: a fever that keeps climbing, chills and shaking, abdominal pain getting worse rather than better, new jaundice, or a fever that settles and then returns — back to hospital the same day. The reason behind this is liver abscess: a pooled analysis puts the rate at only 0.54%, but when it happens, mortality is 7.73%[10]. A dividing line of the kind "still febrile on day X means infection" — I could not find any citable source for one, so I am not going to give you a made-up number of days: watch the trend, not the day count. After SBRT the window sits between two weeks and three months after treatment ends: the classic picture of radiation-induced liver disease is anicteric hepatomegaly with ascites and a rising alkaline phosphatase[11]; in the prospective trial of small tumours with good liver function, about 4% worsened their Child-Pugh score by 2 points or more[12]. The plain-language threshold: within three months of finishing SBRT, a waistband getting tighter, unexplained weight gain, a swelling belly, growing drowsiness — bring the visit forward; do not wait for the next scheduled appointment.

Turn this list into three agreements at home

Do three things today. First, say the sentence "black stools or vomiting blood means calling an ambulance" to the family you live with, word for word. Second, agree with your family that if they think you are "not yourself" — sleeping upside down, unable to count money, personality changed — they have the right to ring the hospital directly, without your permission. Third, put the treatment team's contact numbers (clinic, case manager, night-time emergency department) on the fridge, and save them in at least two phones. Every line of this list comes with its source, and you can take it to your doctor and ask: with my liver function and my treatment combination, which lines are my priorities?

From the physician

Everyone's priority list is different: people with ascites, people on the immune combination, people on targeted tablets each have their own top three. Take this article to your next clinic visit and ask your doctor: with my liver function and my treatment combination, which lines are my priorities? Have them circled for you.
