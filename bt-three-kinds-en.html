WHERE EVERYONE STARTS

Three tumours, three different logics

Meningioma turns on grade and location, vestibular schwannoma on hearing, pituitary adenoma on whether it is secreting. The three logics are not shared — right down to whether Taiwan can count how many people have one.

Benign Brain Tumour Guide · Where everyone starts · 18 references
#Staging#Molecular Testing#Evidence#Decisions

Some sentences are true of only one of these three tumours and get repeated as though they covered all three. This article is the taking apart of those sentences. You will not finish it knowing how you should be treated, but you will know which question is yours, and which numbers were never about you.

Meningioma, vestibular schwannoma (also called acoustic neuroma) and pituitary adenoma (pituitary tumour from here on) often turn up on the same day and get dealt with by the same one-liner: "it's benign". Most of them are indeed not cancer, but all three of these tumours have a malignant compartment, and the proportion is different in each: in the American brain tumour registry for 2017 to 2021, of 195,297 meningiomas 1,608 were malignant; among 37,436 schwannomas, of an incidence of 2.02 per 100,000 only 0.01 was malignant; and of 81,187 pituitary tumours more than 99.8% were non-malignant[1]. "Benign" is not a conclusion; it is where the conversation starts. The first question is entirely different for each of the three: for meningioma it is grade and location, for vestibular schwannoma it is hearing, and for a pituitary tumour it is whether the thing is secreting a hormone. Blending the three logics into one is the mistake I correct most often in clinic. This article does nothing but sort you into the right one; it gives no treatment detail at all.

Why these three ended up in one guide

Because between them they are very nearly the whole map. In the American brain tumour registry's 2018 to 2022 data, meningioma accounts for 42.6% of all primary brain and other central nervous system tumours (a denominator that includes non-malignant tumours), and 57.4% of all non-malignant ones[2]. The 2017 to 2021 breakdown from the same registry adds the other two blocks: pituitary tumours 17.4% and schwannomas 8.0%, which the report itself describes as together making up "slightly more than a quarter of all tumours (25.4%)"[1]. Those two percentages come from the 2017 to 2021 edition and the 42.6% from the 2018 to 2022 edition; the two reports do not share a denominator, so I am not adding the three blocks together. What can be said is that these three diseases between them are the bulk of primary brain tumours in the American registry.

There is a detail here that gets missed. This registry takes in non-malignant tumours and pituitary tumours; other American cancer registries generally take in malignant tumours only, and the report itself writes that "differences in definition affect direct comparison of published incidence rates"[1]. So the denominator under all those percentages is "brain tumours", not "brain cancers".

Taiwan does not have these three numbers, and that is not the statistics quietly dropping them

Every figure above comes from the United States. Taiwan has no counterpart, and the reason is written into the law. Article 3 of the Cancer Control Act defines cancer as "a malignant tumour confirmed by pathological section, or reasonably presumed by other examination or testing, which clinically shows recurrence or metastasis"[3]. The Taiwan Cancer Registry's long-form abstracting manual is blunter still: what must be reported is cases "with ICD-O-3 behaviour codes 2, 3, 6 or 9", and the notes on that same field place benign (0) and borderline (1) outside the scope of reporting[4]. Benign brain tumours never enter the cancer registry in the first place.

What about the health insurance statistics? The only figure that can be separated out in the National Health Insurance medical statistics yearbook for fiscal year 113 is D33 (benign neoplasm of brain and other parts of the central nervous system): 23,027 people in western-medicine outpatient care and 652 discharges[5]. But D33 does not include meningioma (D32) and does not include pituitary tumours (D35.2) — in that same yearbook both are swept into the large "other tumours" category[5]. So taking the 23,027 to stand for meningioma or for pituitary tumour is wrong.

Of the three, only vestibular schwannoma has an incidence figure from Taiwan that can be cited at all, and it is an academic study, not official statistics: using the National Health Insurance database, taking 2001 to 2012, adults over 20, and counting as confirmed cases only those who had an MRI before diagnosis, it worked out an annual incidence of 2.66 per 100,000 (95% confidence interval 2.32 to 3.05 — repeat the study many times and the answer would land somewhere in that range), highest at ages 60 to 69, at 4.86 per 100,000[6]. The National Health Insurance fee schedule, by contrast, writes all three tumours into the indications of the same stereotactic radiosurgery item[7] — one box administratively, three different questions clinically.

Meningioma: the first question is grade and where it sits

It arises from meningothelial cells, the arachnoid cap cells. One review notes that the meningothelial cells of the skull base and of the cerebral convexity have different embryonic origins (mesoderm against neural crest), and that this difference shapes which histological subtypes turn up where[8]. From the American registry, 2017 to 2021: incidence 10.15 per 100,000 (men 6.02, women 13.90), a male-to-female ratio of 0.43, median age at diagnosis 68[1].

The skeleton of the decision comes from the European Association of Neuro-Oncology guideline: the diagnosis itself is "usually made by neuroimaging, mostly MRI", but assigning a grade under the World Health Organization classification requires tissue, which means an operation; which is why asymptomatic or older patients "may be managed with a watch-and-scan strategy"[9]. How much can be removed is set by "tumour location, consistency, size, and proximity to or invasion of critical neurovascular structures"[9]. How the grade is assigned is in "How a meningioma gets called grade 1, 2 or 3"; the location layer is in "Where it sits matters more than how big it is".

Vestibular schwannoma: the first question is hearing

It sits in the registry's "schwannoma" group, incidence 2.02 per 100,000 (non-malignant 2.01, malignant 0.01)[1]. Anatomically it divides again — those confined to the internal auditory canal and those extending into the cerebellopontine angle — and the Danish national cohort keeps its statistics along exactly that line[10]. The same Danish cohort also records that the median age at diagnosis rose from 49.2 years in 1976 to 60 years in 2015, which the authors attribute to diagnostic tools becoming easier to get hold of[11]. The Taiwanese study's peak was ages 60 to 69[6].

Why say hearing is the lead? Because the guidelines for this disease are written around it. The American Congress of Neurological Surgeons' 2018 document spends itself on the serviceable hearing preservation rates of each of the three roads, and sets out explicit probability bands for the observation group at two years, five years and ten years[12]. How hearing is measured and which scoring system is used is in "Hearing is what this decision is really about".

Pituitary tumour: the first question is whether it is secreting

It arises from the endocrine cells of the adenohypophysis, the anterior lobe. The 2022 World Health Organization classification separates anterior-lobe tumours from posterior-lobe and hypothalamic ones, and types them by transcription-factor staining along cell lineages[13]. Registry incidence is 4.67 per 100,000 (men 4.03, women 5.41)[1]; in a census-style Belgian study, clinically relevant pituitary adenomas had a mean age at diagnosis of 40.3, were 67.6% female, and 42.6% were macroadenomas[14]. This one is distinctly younger than the other two, so do not describe all three as "a disease of the middle-aged and elderly".

The very first recommendation in the Endocrine Society's incidentaloma guideline is the secretion question: a complete history and examination, plus laboratory screening for hormone hypersecretion and for hypopituitarism; a visual field test is added when the lesion abuts or compresses the optic nerves and chiasm[15]. The same guideline singles prolactinoma out — the surgical referral condition is worded as a hypersecreting tumour "other than a prolactinoma"[15]. What is secreted and how it is established is in "First, find out whether it is secreting" and "Prolactinoma: the drug comes first, not the knife".

The name PitNET: both sides are still arguing

You may have seen PitNET on a report or online. The 2022 World Health Organization classification did indeed classify well-differentiated adenohypophyseal tumours "as pituitary neuroendocrine tumours (PitNETs; formerly called pituitary adenomas)"[13]. What is actually used in publication, though, is dual nomenclature: "pituitary adenoma/pituitary neuroendocrine tumour", the two names side by side[16].

The side objecting to the rename puts it hard: the overall prevalence of pituitary tumours is "more than 10%", while local invasion occurs in only one in every 2,000 and malignancy in only one in every 100,000, so applying the label "neuroendocrine tumour" amounts to "grossly mislabelling the overwhelming majority"; they also point out that markers such as synaptophysin are expressed in thyroid and adrenal tumours too, and ask whether the same reasoning would have those renamed as well[16]. The accurate statement is this: it currently has two names, and nothing has been settled.

The other benign intracranial tumours each run on their own logic

The ones this guide does not open up get a sentence each — but they are not alike, and that is the point:

  • Haemangioblastoma: an analysis of the American registry put the incidence of central nervous system haemangioblastoma at 0.141 per 100,000 person-years; in multivariable analysis, multiple tumours were an independent factor for worse survival (hazard ratio 1.715, which is the ratio of the rate at which events happen in two groups)[17]. Multiple tumours take the conversation towards inheritance — see "More than one, NF2, and what is inherited".

  • Craniopharyngioma: American registry 2017 to 2021, 3,096 cases, incidence 0.18 per 100,000, 0.7% of all brain tumours[1].

  • Epidermoid cyst: in a surgical series of 17 cerebellopontine angle cases with a mean follow-up of 126 months, gross total resection was achieved in only 5, with the surgeon deliberately leaving behind, in the other 12, whatever was stuck to a vessel, a nerve or the brainstem; extent of resection was unrelated to the risk of recurrence, and symptomatic recurrence came on average more than nine years later[18]. Not getting it all out does not change recurrence here — which is the exact opposite of the meningioma logic.

A malignant brain tumour is a different disease; there is a separate article on the site, "Glioblastoma: what it is, and what it is not".

Three logics means three different first rounds of tests

Here is the most practical sentence in this article: what the first round of tests you are booked for looks like tells you which of the three the doctor has sorted you into. In the meningioma box, the first round is the imaging itself — where it sits, what it is touching, whether there is oedema — and the grade cannot be spoken about with any confidence until there is tissue[9]. In the vestibular schwannoma box, the first round always includes a formal hearing test[12]. In the pituitary box, the first round always includes hormone bloods, with a visual field test added if the lesion is against the optic pathway[15].

Which gives you something very specific to ask at the next appointment: which of the three is mine counted as, and for that one, has the first round been done in full? Prognosis, treatment threshold and follow-up schedule are not shared between these three diseases. What each schedule looks like is in "Watching is a form of treatment" and "How follow-up is scheduled, and when it stops".

From the physician

The only thing I want you to take away from this one is that they are not shared. A meningioma's follow-up schedule is not a vestibular schwannoma's, and a vestibular schwannoma's first round of tests is not a pituitary tumour's. Next time you meet a sentence online that starts "brain tumours all…", check which of the three it is talking about — and while you are not sure, that sentence has no force over you.
