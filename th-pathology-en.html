DIAGNOSIS AND STAGING

Papillary, follicular, NIFTP: reading the report

What decides the road ahead is often not the tumour's name but two small lines on the report: whether the capsule was breached, and how many vessels were invaded.

Thyroid Cancer Guide · Diagnosis and staging · 8 references
#Staging#Molecular Testing#Evidence#Recurrence

Many people read a pathology report as a noun. It is a set of conditions — histological type, subtype, capsule, vessels, margins — and any of them can move you into a different risk box. The NIFTP renaming changed the reading criteria, not the verdicts of the day.

The pathology report after surgery is the single most important sheet of paper in this disease. It decides whether anything more has to be done, how often you come back, and what gets looked at when you do. It is also the hardest sheet to read — a tumour name, a subtype, a size, a capsule, vessels, lymph nodes, and a few things that look like English initials.

This article explains what those names stand for, which lines genuinely change what comes next, and what the NIFTP "renaming" actually changed. How all of it gets converted into a stage and a recurrence risk is in 〈Why the stage depends on your age〉; the lymph node part is in 〈Should the nodes come out too〉. Treatment choices are not in this one.

The classification carries two years; better to get both out at once

The tumour classification pathologists work from is the World Health Organization's. The current one is the fifth edition; endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours are volume 10, printed in 2025[1]. But the thyroid chapter came out ahead of the book in 2022, so the literature habitually calls it "the 2022 classification" — that is how the 2023 cytology reporting system refers to it[2]. One classification, two years attached to it, not two classifications.

Where these names actually land in Taiwan is visible in the cancer registry. Among the 6,045 thyroid malignancies newly diagnosed in 2023: papillary carcinoma, 1,381 men (91.64%) and 4,226 women (93.12%); follicular carcinoma, 60 men (3.98%) and 208 women (4.58%); medullary carcinoma, 19 men (1.26%) and 29 women (0.64%); anaplastic carcinoma, 22 men (1.46%) and 30 women (0.66%)[3]. The overwhelming majority of reports will say papillary. Medullary and anaplastic carcinoma are two other diseases, written in 〈Medullary cancer is a different disease〉 and 〈Anaplastic cancer is an emergency〉, and nothing in this article applies to them.

The names that turn up on the report

The framework below is the one the 2025 American Thyroid Association guideline assembled from the fifth World Health Organization edition[4]; I did not obtain the book itself, so this is second-hand.

The first group is the low-risk neoplasms: NIFTP, follicular tumour of uncertain malignant potential, hyalinizing trabecular tumour. This group does not sit in the malignant column at all. The second group is malignant: follicular thyroid carcinoma (split into minimally invasive, encapsulated angioinvasive, and widely invasive), invasive encapsulated follicular variant papillary carcinoma (IEFVPTC), the subtypes of papillary thyroid carcinoma (classical, encapsulated classical, infiltrative follicular, tall cell, columnar cell, hobnail, diffuse sclerosing, solid or trabecular, Warthin-like, oncocytic and others), and oncocytic (Hürthle cell) carcinoma, also in three forms. The third group is high-grade but not yet anaplastic: poorly differentiated carcinoma, and differentiated high-grade thyroid carcinoma[4].

Two things get confused here. First, oncocytic (Hürthle cell) carcinoma is now the third independent kind of differentiated thyroid cancer rather than a subtype of follicular carcinoma, and it makes up about 3% of all differentiated thyroid cancer[4]. Second, IEFVPTC is the half that split off from the old "encapsulated follicular variant of papillary carcinoma" — the invasive half. Even the guideline got this wrong in typesetting: a corrigendum in October 2025 changed a row heading in one of its tables from "follicular carcinoma" to "follicular carcinoma and IEFVPTC"[5].

Capsule and vessels: the two lines that move everything after

More often than what the tumour is called, what decides the road ahead is whether it got out.

The guideline defines vascular invasion as tumour cells invading through a vessel wall with a fibrin thrombus adherent to the intravascular tumour. In practice pathology reports often write the compound term lymphovascular invasion without separating lymphatic invasion from vascular invasion; the guideline itself concedes that how to tell them apart remains controversial among pathologists[4]. So on that line, ask which of the two is meant.

On the papillary side, the 2015 edition placed papillary carcinoma with vascular invasion at intermediate recurrence risk, a structural recurrence risk of 15% to 30% — structural recurrence meaning something genuinely findable on imaging or pathology, not just a blood number moving. I read those three figures in the 2025 edition's account of the 2015 edition, so that is where the citation points[4]. But that line appearing on your report does not mean recurrence is coming. The 2025 edition, discussing the same question, cites both data that found an association and data that did not[4]; I did not go back to those primary papers this time, so I am not putting their percentages here.

For follicular carcinoma, somebody counts

On the follicular side, pathologists really do count how many vessels were invaded, and 4 is the watershed. The 2015 edition listed extensive vascular invasion (4 or more foci) as high recurrence risk, structural recurrence risk 30% to 55%, and minimal invasion (fewer than 4) as low risk, 2% to 3% — again as related by the 2025 edition[4]. The 2025 edition also quotes recurrence rates and hazard ratios from two individual studies here — a hazard ratio is how many times faster events happen in one group than the other, and near 1 means no difference you can see[4]. I did not go back to those two papers, and one of them came without a confidence interval, so I am not copying the numbers.

So if your report says follicular carcinoma, get the number of invaded vessels out of it. That is not a detail. That is which box you go in.

The higher-risk subtypes have names

The 2025 guideline writes this as a Good Practice Statement — which the guideline does not grade — and the content is: subtypes with a poorer outlook, such as the tall cell, columnar cell and hobnail subtypes of papillary carcinoma, widely invasive follicular and oncocytic carcinoma, and high-grade non-anaplastic carcinoma, should be identified during pathological examination and written into the report; and so should the ones on the more favourable side[4].

How much difference? The guideline lists these subtypes as having a poorer outlook, but I could not get comparative figures I am able to cite, so this paragraph gives a direction and no percentages.

NIFTP: the reading rules changed, not the old verdicts

NIFTP stands for non-invasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary-like nuclear features. Before 2016 the same slide would have been called non-invasive encapsulated follicular variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma — that is, a diagnosis of cancer.

The original basis for the renaming is a 2016 study: 109 cases of non-invasive encapsulated follicular variant papillary carcinoma, followed 10 to 26 years with a median of 13 (the median is the one in the middle: half the people run shorter, half longer), of whom 67 had a lobectomy only and none received radioiodine (I-131); at last follow-up all were alive with no evidence of disease. The comparison group was 101 invasive cases followed 1 to 18 years, of whom 12 (12%) had an adverse event, including 5 distant metastases, 2 of them dying of the disease[6]. The watershed is not what the cells look like. It is whether there was invasion.

Earning the NIFTP label is strict: the tumour must be encapsulated or clearly demarcated; growth must be follicular, with no papillae, no psammoma bodies, and under 30% solid, trabecular or insular growth; it must carry the nuclear features of papillary carcinoma; there must be no capsular or vascular invasion; no tumour necrosis; and fewer than 3 mitoses per 10 high-power fields[4]. Settling all of that requires examining the whole tumour capsule and the tumour body — which is why a fine-needle cytology report cannot diagnose NIFTP[7].

How common is it? The only authoritative figure the guideline gives is that NIFTP comprises roughly 2.1% to 9.6% of follicular cell-derived thyroid neoplasms, with a relatively lower incidence in Asia than in North America and Europe[4]. Note the denominator there: follicular cell-derived neoplasms, not papillary carcinomas. The line that circulates online — that some fraction of papillary cancers turn out not to be cancer — I could not trace to a citable original source, so I am not writing it.

If what you are holding is an older report

One thing first: the reading rules changed; that does not mean the pathologist of the day got it wrong. The criteria were written that way at the time. The same slide draws a different name in different years because the classification moved, not because a person erred.

For someone holding a NIFTP diagnosis now, the guideline's position is that these tumours have a very low malignant potential, lower than the lowest-risk differentiated thyroid cancer, and that completion thyroidectomy or radioiodine is not advised routinely — but the second half of the same sentence is that the optimal approach to monitoring these tumours after surgery is uncertain, and the whole item is again a Good Practice Statement, which the guideline does not grade[4]. I insist on the second half, because what it means is: no extra treatment is not the same as no follow-up.

Also, because NIFTP was reclassified as non-cancer, the malignancy risk of each cytology category dropped by 1.3% to 9.1%[7]. Hold an old report's number against a new one and it will not match.

Be careful with survival numbers on a report

The much-quoted set of papillary carcinoma survival figures does not say in the guideline text whether the endpoint is overall survival or disease-specific survival, so I do not use it. A number with labels looks like this: in a registry of 4,881 adults under 55 with differentiated thyroid cancer, those with AJCC eighth edition stage I disease who were also low risk on the 2015 three-tier recurrence system had a 10-year disease-specific survival — counting only deaths from this disease, not other causes — of 100%[8]. Inside that same stage I, the other boxes read differently, and that whole passage is in 〈Why the stage depends on your age〉.

Which lines are worth asking about on the spot

Take the report in with you and read four sentences off it. First: "What is my histological type and subtype? Is it one of the ones listed as having a poorer outlook?" Second: "Does the report say vascular invasion? If so, is it lymphatic or vascular, and how many foci?" Third: "Was the capsule breached? Were the surgical margins clear?" Fourth, if the report says NIFTP: "Since no extra treatment is needed, how is my follow-up arranged, how often, and looking at what?"

From the physician

Before you are discharged, ask the ward for a paper copy of your pathology report and keep it. It travels with you to every new doctor and every second opinion — the capsule, vessel and margin lines are what other people judge your follow-up on, and they do not survive being repeated from memory.
