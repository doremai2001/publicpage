DURING TREATMENT

When to come back the same day: three tiers

Every item says why it is today rather than the next appointment — raised intracranial pressure, status epilepticus, fever during the course, the steroid emergencies, and the clot that gets missed most often.

Glioblastoma Guide · During Treatment · 18 references
#Urgent#Side Effects#Caregivers#Daily Life

Only 48% of brain tumour patients have headache at all, so "morning headache" cannot be used as a screening threshold. This article re-sorts every warning sign into three tiers, and lands on one sheet of paper you can write today and stick on the fridge door.

What patients actually ask is not "what is this symptom", it is "can this wait until my next appointment". Every item in this article answers that question head-on: what it might be, and why it is today rather than the next clinic.

Three tiers: call an ambulance, come back today, say it at this appointment

These are the three tiers I sort things into for families in clinic. I do not swap the wording, because swapping it loosens it.

  • Tier one, do not ask me first, call an ambulance or go straight to the emergency department: a convulsion lasting more than five minutes, one ending and another starting straight after, not waking after a seizure; a change in consciousness, one pupil bigger than the other; one-sided weakness of an arm or leg coming on over minutes to hours, speech suddenly slurred or gone, a drooping corner of the mouth; a sudden severe headache, or headache with vomiting; difficulty breathing, chest pain, coughing blood, fainting.

  • Tier two is contacting the team today and leaving the house now, not "finding time to come back today": an ear or oral reading of 38.0°C, once — do not take it again, do not wait for it to persist; black stools or vomiting something coffee-coloured; one calf suddenly swollen and painful; one-sided weakness or unsteadiness that has been getting more obvious over the past few days; a wound that is red, swollen or oozing; no strength, dizziness, vision going black on standing, after a dose reduction.

  • The remaining tier can wait for this appointment, but it has to be said out loud: the headache has changed in pattern, mood or behaviour has changed, walking is less steady than last week. These three I ask about myself every single time, because patients rarely bring them up.

"Morning headache" is not the line

The textbook writes brain tumour headache as worst first thing in the morning with projectile vomiting. The data from real patients do not look like that. In a prospective study of 111 consecutively enrolled patients with primary or metastatic brain tumours, only 48% had headache at all, and 77% of those resembled tension-type headache; the author's own sentence is that the classic early-morning brain tumour headache is uncommon, and the identifying rule he offers is three other things: nausea, vomiting, an abnormal neurological examination, or a marked change in the previous headache pattern[1].

On the other side, an emergency medicine review of what genuinely raised intracranial pressure looks like writes that the headache is "worst in the early morning, worsened by Valsalva-like straining, and accompanied by nausea or vomiting"[2]. The two do not conflict — they are answering two different questions: what headache looks like in brain tumour patients, and what it looks like when the intracranial pressure really has gone up. Morning headache can be kept as a reference point, but some people get it and many do not, and its absence does not mean nothing is wrong.

What to use are three lines: it is not the same as the headache you used to have; it comes with vomiting, or gets worse when you bend over or cough; something new has appeared neurologically (walking off to one side, one-sided weakness, slurred speech, seeing double)[1][2]. And the reason one pupil being bigger than the other, and a progressively falling level of consciousness, mean calling an ambulance is that raised intracranial pressure goes on to herniation, and what that causes is ischaemia and irreversible brain injury[2].

A convulsion past five minutes: stop waiting

5 minutes is the line for acting, and after 30 minutes irreversible consequences begin — where those two time points come from and how they are defined is in "Seizures, anti-seizure drugs, and driving"[3]. The first-aid page of the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention turns it into an action: time the seizure, and if it goes past 5 minutes get medical help or call an ambulance immediately; one seizure ending and another starting straight after, and not waking after a seizure, also mean calling[4].

Why can you not watch a little longer? Getting from home to the emergency department, then a line into a vein, then a drug taking effect, eats those 30 minutes by itself. Five minutes is not an observation period, it is the time to set off. What the family should and should not do at the time is in that same article.

Fever during treatment: one reading of 38.0°C and you leave the house

The Infectious Diseases Society of America's 2010 guideline on febrile neutropenia defines fever as a single oral temperature of 38.3°C or above, or 38.0°C or above sustained over one hour; neutropenia is an absolute neutrophil count below 500/mm³[5]. The problem is that at home you cannot confirm "sustained over an hour", and you do not know what today's neutrophil count is. So the operating rule set on this site in "The fever that cannot wait until morning" is deliberately stricter than the guideline, and this article carries the same sentence over: one reading of 38.0°C — go; do not re-measure, do not wait for it to persist, do not wait for 38.3°C. The two temperatures in the guideline are there for interpretation; the only number you have to hold on to is one.

The wording is "leave the house now" and not "find time to come back today", and the reason is in the 2018 guideline from the American Society of Clinical Oncology and the Infectious Diseases Society of America: patients with febrile neutropenia should have their first dose of antibiotic within one hour of triage[6].

This is not a low-probability event. In the randomised trial that established standard treatment, grade 3 or 4 haematological toxicity during the concurrent stretch was 7%[7]; in a contemporary cohort of 206 people, thrombocytopenia of any grade was 61.1%[8]; and a single-centre audit of 52 people is more specific still — grade 3 or 4 thrombocytopenia in 19%, lasting a median of 32 days, with 17% of people stopping the drug because of it, and the word the authors use is "possibly irreversible"[9]. Thirty-two days is not three days, and you will spend most of them at home. So bleeding gums when you brush, unexplained bruising, a wound that is red, swollen or oozing, are all same-day. Fever together with a stiff neck, severe headache, photophobia or a change in consciousness should make you think of postoperative meningitis, and that one is immediate — I could not find a source I could cite for the incidence of postoperative infection, but the threshold does not loosen because there is no number.

Three steroid emergencies, and one of them looks just like the tumour turning worse

The first is gastrointestinal bleeding: black tarry stools, vomiting something coffee-coloured, persistent severe pain in the upper abdomen. What really drives the risk up is the combination of a steroid plus a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory painkiller; the numbers are in "Steroids: the drug that saves you, and has to come down", and the conclusion is one sentence: ask your doctor before you swallow a painkiller for a headache. Why it is today: a black stool means the bleeding has already happened, and during the course you may be thrombocytopenic at the same time.

The second is a hyperglycaemic emergency: thirst, going to the toilet constantly, blurred vision, deep rapid breathing, a deteriorating level of consciousness. Glucocorticoids increase gluconeogenesis in the liver, reduce insulin sensitivity and impair β cell function, and the oral route carries the highest risk[10]. Why it is today: a deteriorating level of consciousness is also how raised intracranial pressure shows itself, and at home the two cannot be told apart. How high the blood sugar has to be to count as an emergency, I could not find a guideline threshold I could cite, so I am not giving a number here.

The third is the one most easily misread: no strength, nausea, dizziness, vision going black on standing, after a dose reduction. Adrenal insufficiency can happen to anyone after a reduction, and no form of administration, dose or length of course can rule it out in advance[11] (why, is in "Steroids: the drug that saves you, and has to come down"). It looks exactly like "the tumour has grown again", the treatment points the opposite way, and the cost of guessing wrong is asymmetric — so this one is not watch-at-home, it is say-it-today.

One leg swollen, breathless after a few steps: do not put it down to the radiotherapy first

This is the one most often missed. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's page for cancer survivors puts it directly: cancer patients are at much higher risk of deep vein thrombosis than the general population, and patients with brain, pancreatic, stomach and lung cancer are at the highest risk of all[12]. The incidence figures are wide: in a systematic review pooling 8 cohorts and 7,779 people, grade 4 glioma ran from 6.2% to 31%, with the events made up of roughly 60% deep vein thrombosis, 35% pulmonary embolism and 5% cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, and a median time from surgery to the clot being diagnosed of 21 to 90 days[13]; the Danish national registry's 3,630 patients work out at only 6.8% for grade 4, highest in the first three months after diagnosis[14]. Not one of them says it is rare.

That time window sits right across the concurrent chemoradiotherapy stretch, and the symptoms are easily put down to something else: brain tumour patients often have one-sided weakness already, and that leg was moving less anyway; breathlessness and tiredness get charged to the radiotherapy. The official page grades it: swelling, pain, tenderness, redness or warmth in a leg or an arm that was not caused by a knock — the wording there is "call your doctor right away"; difficulty breathing, a fast or irregular heartbeat, chest pain that is worse on a deep breath, coughing blood, fainting — the wording there is "get medical help immediately"[12].

Whether to take a drug to prevent clots is not decided by you at home, and the direction runs opposite to the intuition: the 2022 international guideline divides the question by whether there has been surgery and whether a clot has already happened, and recommends separately for each of several situations, with a different answer every time; that is picked by your doctor for the situation you are actually in, not a rule you can match yourself against[15]. Whether or not you are on a drug of this kind at the moment, do not stop it or start it yourself because you have read this passage.

I write the cost side out from the source too. In a meta-analysis pooling 9 retrospective cohorts, glioma patients on therapeutic-dose anticoagulation had an odds ratio for intracranial haemorrhage of 3.75, while brain metastasis patients had no increase[16] — an odds ratio is not "the probability multiplied by 3.75", and where the events are not rare those two are very different things. Only one randomised trial has been done on this question, and it had enrolled just 186 people when it closed early because the drug supply expired: bleeding looked more frequent, but it did not reach statistical significance (hazard ratio 4.2, 95% confidence interval 0.48 to 36, P=0.22) — a hazard ratio divides the speed at which bleeds occurred in the two groups, and the confidence interval in the bracket is the plausible range for that estimate; running from 0.48 all the way to 36, it has not even settled its direction. One descriptive fact the authors set down is that the major bleeds were all intracranial and all occurred while the drug was being taken[17]. These are the things your doctor is weighing up, not reasons for you to stop a drug. There is only one thing for you to do: say the swollen leg, the breathlessness, the chest pain out loud, today.

Two kinds of breathlessness, and at home you cannot tell them apart

"Breathless" has two sources over this stretch of treatment. One is pulmonary embolism. The other is pneumocystis pneumonia: difficulty breathing, a dry cough, fever, which can progress to respiratory failure, and in immunosuppressed patients who do not have HIV the course is more abrupt. During concurrent chemoradiotherapy it is not common, but it is not zero[18]. The two kinds of breathlessness cannot be separated at home; both are for a doctor to separate, the same day. This passage is not telling you to stop that preventive tablet; why you are on it and how long for is in "What those six weeks of radiotherapy are actually like".

The three lines on the fridge door

This article has to land on one sheet of paper, written today and stuck on the fridge door. Line one: which number to ring in the daytime — your case manager's or the treatment team's number, not the switchboard. Line two: where to go at night and at weekends. Line three: what to take with you — the medication list, the radiotherapy card, the most recent blood test report. All three differ from hospital to hospital; ask face to face at the next appointment, and write them down.

And one more thing to settle in advance. The items in tier one do not need the patient's opinion first: when there is a change in consciousness, when one pupil is bigger than the other, when a convulsion has gone past five minutes, the person who can decide is often not him. I have watched families argue in the living room about whether to wait a bit longer far too many times — there is not a single item in this article that is "watch and see".

Write those three lines down today: which number to ring in the daytime, where to go at night and at weekends, and what to take with you. The fever rule matches the one already on this site — one reading of 38.0°C and you go; do not re-measure, do not wait for it to persist.
