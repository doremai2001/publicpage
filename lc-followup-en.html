SIDE EFFECTS, EMERGENCIES AND FOLLOW-UP

One kind of change that is not a recurrence

Over half of those with no recurrence develop high-risk features on the scan — what that means, and what quitting after diagnosis still changes.

Lung Cancer Guide · Side Effects, Emergencies and Follow-up · 14 references
#Lung#Follow-up#Imaging#Recurrence#Prevention

The most useful question at a follow-up appointment is not “has it come back”. It is “what changed since the last scan”. This piece explains why the answer usually needs more than one image, and where the follow-up schedule stops covering you.

Something often turns up on the follow-up scan, and the report may describe it as a shadow that has grown. Most people read those words and hear one thing: it is back. This article is about a counter-intuitive fact that has been measured — after treatment has worked, the lung is left with something that looks a great deal like cancer, and it is more common in people who have not relapsed.

"The shadow has grown" turns up in more than half of the people who have not relapsed

A Dutch group did something clever: they took 88 patients treated with stereotactic radiotherapy who had at least two years of follow-up imaging and confirmed absence of local recurrence, and gave 747 CT scans to five readers blinded to the clinical outcome. Among the "high-risk radiological features" recorded by three or more readers, the commonest were an enlarging opacity, 64.8%, and "still enlarging after 12 months", 50.0%; while the finding already linked to recurrence, three or more features present together, occurred in 22.7% of these people who had not relapsed. The authors' conclusion: more than half of patients without recurrence after stereotactic ablative radiotherapy develop high-risk radiological features[1]. In that cohort's routine care, 6 had a PET scan for suspected recurrence and 4 were booked for biopsy[1].

The other side has to go in too, or this becomes a different misreading. In an earlier study by the same group, 12 pathologically confirmed local recurrences were matched to 24 patients without recurrence: the most informative feature was "opacity still enlarging beyond 12 months", with a sensitivity of 100% and specificity of 83% for recurrence[2]. That was a derivation study with only 12 recurrence cases. Put the two together and you get not a table to check yourself against, but one sentence: the judgement is made across several consecutive scans, not one; and it is made by a physician, not by you holding a report. "Let's scan again in three months" is not the doctor being unsure — time is itself part of the test.

Why that patch looks like cancer: the timeline explains it best

An irradiated lung changes on a schedule: the pneumonitis phase runs 3 to 12 weeks after treatment, while radiation fibrosis is not pneumonitis; it does not begin until 6 to 8 months after irradiation, and it goes on progressing for years[3]. The chronic-phase imaging shows parenchymal distortion, traction bronchiectasis and pleural thickening[3]. What to do about breathlessness and cough in the pneumonitis phase is in "Breathless after treatment — which pneumonitis is it"; the two articles run on the same facts.

What stereotactic radiotherapy leaves behind differs again: it sits in the shell of normal tissue just outside the tumour and takes complex shapes; and when those changes take on a consolidative or mass-like appearance, distinguishing them from tumour recurrence is extremely difficult[4]. That is not a physician failing to read a scan. It is what this thing looks like.

A PET scan is not the referee either

People assume a PET scan settles it. A Danish multicentre study took 150 post-treatment PET scans done at six months for recurrence assessment and gave each to two randomly assigned teams (a nuclear medicine physician and a radiologist each) to read independently: without predefined criteria, inter-reader agreement was κ=0.55 (overall agreement 79%); with predefined criteria, κ=0.61 (agreement 87%), with areas under the curve of 0.80 and 0.82, not significantly different[5]. Two sets of specialists looking at the same scan agree about eight times in ten. That is why the next step is so often "look again" rather than a conclusion.

How often to scan: reasonable, but never shown to make you live longer

Neither side of this can be overstated. Say too much and someone believes extra scans buy time; say too little and someone stops coming back. Only one large trial has randomised the follow-up method: France's IFCT-0302 enrolled 1,775 patients with completely resected stage I to IIIA disease, randomised to clinic visits plus chest X-ray, or the same plus chest and abdominal CT, over five years. Median overall survival was 8.5 years against 10.3 years, adjusted hazard ratio 0.95 (95% confidence interval 0.83–1.10), not significant — a hazard ratio compares how fast deaths occur in the two groups over the same period, and a figure like 0.95 hugging 1, with an interval crossing it, means in plain words the two groups cannot be told apart. But the CT arm did find more: recurrence 32.6% against 27.7%, second primary lung cancer 4.5% against 3.0%[6]. The authors concluded CT surveillance did not bring longer survival, but found more early recurrences and second primaries, the ones more likely to be treatable with curative intent[6].

So the correct word is "reasonable", not "prolongs survival"; and reading it backwards as "scans make no difference, so why bother" is equally wrong. After its systematic review, the 2013 American College of Chest Physicians guideline admitted as much: this question suffers from a paucity of well-designed prospective studies. It recommends chest CT at specified intervals after surgical resection, and recommends against tumour marker testing outside a clinical trial[7].

As for how often: I did not obtain the full text of a current guideline setting out an interval-by-interval schedule, and there is no Taiwanese official recommendation, so I will not write "guidelines recommend every three months". One reference point — a US stereotactic radiotherapy cohort described its own schedule as every 3 to 4 months in year 1, every 3 to 6 months in year 2, every 6 to 12 months in years 3 to 5, and annually thereafter[8] — and that is one centre's practice, not a Taiwanese rule. On the National Health Insurance (健保) side: CT without contrast is 33070B at 3,800 points, with contrast 33071B at 4,560 points (effective 2020-09-01), a chest examination 32001C at 200 points[9]; the payment schedule (支付標準) does not set an interval. That is decided by your physician according to stage and treatment, which is why hospitals schedule differently. I did not find the reimbursement clause for PET in surveillance, so whether to do one and whether it is covered are questions for your attending physician and the hospital billing office (醫務課). The above is the 114.03.13 version, verified 2026-08-30.

One boundary needs drawing clearly: the surveillance schedule governs how often you are seen when you have no symptoms. It does not govern what to do when you have some. New breathlessness, coughing blood or swelling of the face and neck: see "Breathlessness, blood, fluid: which one is today". New neurological symptoms: "Brain metastases are not the end". A new cough with breathlessness: "Breathless after treatment — which pneumonitis is it". All three use one threshold: new, and different from you last week, does not wait for the next scheduled scan.

A new cancer growing in the lung is not the same thing as a recurrence

These numbers mean nothing without the method that produced them. A 2024 review writes that within five years, up to one in six long-term survivors may develop a second primary lung cancer — an upper bound, not an ordinary risk[10]. Population cohort figures are far lower in absolute terms: a US cohort of 200,000 linked to cancer registry data found a 10-year cumulative incidence of 2.29% to 3.11% after a first primary lung cancer[11]. An analysis of 11 German cancer registries points out that registry rules cause the conventional method to underestimate by roughly 30%[12]. The figures disagree because the method, the population and the intensity of follow-up differ.

The second half is not in doubt. In that radiotherapy cohort, 18 of 134 patients (13.4%) developed a second primary lung cancer, 21 lesions in all, at a median of 28.5 months after treatment; 20 of those 21 lesions (95.2%) were detected on surveillance imaging, and 18 (85.7%) could be treated with curative intent[8]. That is one institution and 134 people, and the authors note the proportion is higher than in other cohorts. It explains two things: why surveillance imaging is worth having, and why a physician will sometimes suggest a biopsy rather than simply treating it as a recurrence — mistaking a second primary for a recurrence leads to an entirely different management strategy[10]. Whether you qualify for the national lung cancer early detection programme (肺癌早期偵測計畫) is a separate question, covered in "A nodule you neither cut out nor forget".

If you are still smoking, it is not too late for this to count

This section is only for people who were still smoking at diagnosis. Taiwan has a great many lung cancer patients who never smoked, which is the territory of "I never smoked. Why me?"; and blaming someone who already has the diagnosis has no therapeutic value.

A prospective cohort at two medical centres in Moscow enrolled 517 patients with stage IA to IIIA non-small cell lung cancer who were still smoking at diagnosis and followed them for a mean of seven years: five-year overall survival was 60.6% in those who stopped smoking after diagnosis against 48.6% in those who continued; the adjusted hazard ratio for all-cause mortality was 0.67 (95% confidence interval 0.53–0.85) and for disease progression 0.70 (0.56–0.89)[13]. That is a Russian observational cohort with exposure from self-completed questionnaires, but the direction is clear: the benefit of stopping is not only "you will be less likely to get another cancer later" — it is a difference still measurable when you stop after the diagnosis.

Taiwan's pathway already exists. The NHI payment schedule includes smoking cessation items: a cessation consultation fee E1027C 300 points, cessation health education E1022C 100 points, and follow-up items at 3 months, 6 months and 1 year at 50 points each[9]. The Ministry of Health and Welfare has announced that from 15 May of ROC year 111, no co-payment is charged for smoking cessation medication services, for anyone — before that the charge was up to NT$200 each time[14]. As for which medication, how many courses a year, and how eligibility is defined, I did not obtain the primary text from an official page, so I am not writing drug names, prices or course limits; take it to a smoking cessation clinic. Verified 2026-08-30, and please confirm with your case manager (個管師) or the hospital billing office.

Three questions that get you further than "has it come back"

First, "compared with my last scan, where has it changed?" Asking "compared with last time" gets you far more than asking "is it back", because that is how the scan is read in the first place.

Second, "how is my follow-up scheduled — when is the next one, and what scan?" Put the date in your phone calendar. What most often goes wrong in follow-up is not the reading of the scan; it is someone not coming back.

Third, "between two appointments, what change brings me back early?" That answer is printed on no scheduling slip, and you only get it by asking. Once you have it, treat it as part of your follow-up, because that is exactly the stretch the schedule does not cover.

From the physician

The figures here are population-level; yours depend on stage and on what was treated. Two things are worth doing today: put the next scan date in your calendar, and ask what change in between brings you back early. If you were still smoking at diagnosis, a referral to the cessation clinic is worth asking for at the same visit.
