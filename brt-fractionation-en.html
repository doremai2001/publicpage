WHY, AND HOW IT IS DECIDED

Why am I not on the shorter course?

16 treatments against 25 is not a ranking of severity: the ten-year data on whole-breast hypofractionation is mature, the randomised trials in the nodal areas have only just read out, and each of the two choices has reasons that stand up.

Breast Radiotherapy Guide · Why, and How It Is Decided · 14 references
#Short Course#Decisions#Evidence#NHI Coverage

The international randomised trials and the guidelines in their own words, the three reasons our department keeps conventional fractionation, and the population the NHI provision restricts it to — this article stands all three legs side by side. When you have read it you can ask in clinic whether the 15-fraction data applies to you, and you will know what each of the two answers means; neither side is the wrong one.

This conversation happens all the time in the radiation oncology waiting area: one patient is having 16 treatments and will be finished in a bit over three weeks; the woman next to her is having 25, a full five weeks. When the second one comes into my clinic, the first thing she says is usually, "Does that mean mine is worse?" The number of treatments is not a ranking of severity. It is the intersection of three conditions: the type of operation, the node status, and whether the nodal areas have to be treated. This article lays out the evidence on both sides — including the question "courses are getting shorter internationally, so why are some of our patients still on 25?" You are entitled to ask it, and I ought to be able to answer it.

My position first: every technique compared in this topic — ordinary intensity-modulated radiotherapy, TOMO, deep inspiration breath-hold — has a self-paid component among the more expensive options, and all of them are delivered in my own department; protons mean a referral elsewhere. So every "better" here comes with its primary source and the level of evidence behind it, and on cost I cite the official schedule where one exists and tell you to ask the medical affairs office where it does not. Concluding that the basic option is enough for you is a completely reasonable conclusion.

How our hospital draws it: one line, two courses

Let me be clear about our own practice first (the evidence label on this paragraph is "institutional policy", not literature): after breast-conserving surgery, with a pathological stage of pT1 to 2 and negative nodes, where the treated volume is the whole breast — hypofractionation, 15 to 16 treatments. Everything else — node-positive disease, a chest wall that needs treating after mastectomy, a supraclavicular or internal mammary nodal volume that has to be included — conventional fractionation, 25 treatments. That line was not drawn out of a hat. There is a whole layer of evidence on each side of it, and I will take them in order.

What 15 to 16 treatments have to show for themselves: ten years, and no nodal areas

The UK's START-B trial randomised 40 Gy in 15 fractions against 50 Gy in 25 (population pT1-3a, pN0-1, including both conserving surgery and mastectomy): 10-year locoregional recurrence was 4.3% against 5.5% (hazard ratio 0.77, p=0.21 — a hazard ratio compares the rate at which events occur in the two groups, and a ratio under one means events came more slowly in the shorter-course arm), which is not worse; and breast shrinkage, telangiectasia and breast oedema were in fact less common in the 40 Gy arm[1]. The Canadian randomised trial enrolled 1,234 patients after breast-conserving surgery with clear margins and negative axillary nodes: 42.5 Gy in 16 fractions against 50 Gy in 25 fractions, 10-year local recurrence 6.2% against 6.7%[2]. The American Society for Radiation Oncology's 2018 guideline says, word for word, "the preferred dose-fractionation scheme is hypofractionated WBI to a dose of 4000 cGy in 15 fractions or 4250 cGy in 16 fractions" — and it spells out who that applies to: whole-breast irradiation, with or without the low axilla, not covering the regional nodes[3]. Which is exactly the population in the hypofractionation box in our own department.

Five treatments in one week has ten-year data now — and it comes with edges

FAST-Forward took 26 Gy in 5 fractions, finished inside a week, and compared it with 40 Gy in 15 (population pT1-3, pN0-1, conserving surgery or mastectomy, excluding anyone needing axillary irradiation): 10-year recurrence in the same breast was 2.1% against 3.6%, and in the 5-year report the UK authors wrote "supporting its use as a standard of care"[4][5]. But the same trial carries two edges of its own. First, the dose detail is not a trifle: the other arm, at 27 Gy, had clearly more moderate-or-worse change in appearance at ten years than either the 26 Gy or the 40 Gy group — a few tenths of a Gy more per treatment and the late appearance is a step worse[5]; the exact percentages for the three arms I have put in "After it ends: recovery and the long run". Second, the axillary substudy had only 466 patients and a median follow-up of 7 years, and the paper itself concedes that the sample size limited how precise the estimate could be[5]. The full trade-off of the five-fraction schedule, and the details of the boost, are in "Five treatments, fifteen, or twenty-five".

On the node-positive side, how far has the international evidence got

This is the other layer laid alongside, and it is no longer a matter of "still waiting for evidence". A Chinese randomised trial enrolled 820 high-risk patients after mastectomy (4 or more positive nodes, or T3-4) and compared 43.5 Gy in 15 fractions with 50 Gy in 25 to the chest wall plus the nodal areas: 5-year locoregional recurrence 8.3% against 8.1%, and non-inferiority was met — non-inferiority means the trial set out to show the shorter course is not worse by more than a pre-specified margin, not to show it is better; grade 3 acute skin reactions were actually less common in the hypofractionated arm (3% against 8%)[6]. The Danish-led SKAGEN-1 enrolled 2,908 high-risk node-positive patients needing regional nodal irradiation, 40 Gy in 15 fractions against 50 Gy in 25: 3-year arm lymphoedema 8.0% against 9.4%, inside the pre-specified non-inferiority margin, and a hazard ratio for locoregional recurrence within 8 years of 0.96; breast cancer mortality hazard ratio 1.25 (95% confidence interval 0.93 to 1.66 — a confidence interval is the range in which the true value plausibly sits) — statistically not significant, but the point estimate sits on the high side, the interval crosses 1, and the median oncological follow-up is still 5.25 years, and I write all three of those down as they are rather than covering them with the words "no difference"[7]. France's HypoG-01 enrolled 1,265 patients needing nodal irradiation, and non-inferiority was met on its primary endpoint of arm lymphoedema (p<0.001); its 3-year lymphoedema figures were 23.4% against 22.2%, a great deal higher than the Danish ones because the measurement definitions differ — the lymphoedema numbers from these two trials cannot be put side by side[8]. On the guideline side, the European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology's 2022 consensus says, word for word, "moderately hypofractionated radiotherapy can be offered to any patient for whole breast, chest wall (with or without reconstruction), and nodal volumes"[9] — the verb is "can be offered", not "must", and I copy the strength of that wording across without upgrading it. As for the European Society for Medical Oncology's 2024 guideline, I could not obtain the original text, so I do not cite it.

So why are our node-positive patients still on 25?

Three reasons with names on them, not habit. One, the length of follow-up: SKAGEN-1's median oncological follow-up is 5.25 years and HypoG-01's is 4.8[7][8]; set beside the ten-year data in the whole-breast layer, the nodal layer is young. Two, in patients who need axillary irradiation the five-fraction schedule is short on precision by FAST-Forward's own admission[5]. Three, late normal-tissue effects — the heart, the brachial plexus, fibrosis — run on a time scale of a decade and more; in the EBCTCG post-mastectomy meta-analysis, the breast cancer mortality benefit also only showed itself in full once follow-up reached 20 years[10]. That is conservative, not behind the times; and turned round the other way, the hospitals that have moved to 15 fractions are not being reckless either — three randomised trials are standing right there. History left an Easter egg as well: the 1970s British Columbia trial treated 318 premenopausal node-positive patients, and what it used was precisely regional irradiation of 37.5 Gy in 16 fractions, with 20-year overall survival of 47% against 37%[11] — hypofractionated nodal irradiation is not a new invention, it is a return trip. The difference is that this time round everyone wants the late-effects accounts filed in full as well.

There is one box those three reasons do not answer, and I am adding it here: after breast-conserving surgery, with 1 to 3 positive axillary nodes, but a treatment plan that covers the whole breast only and leaves the nodal areas out. The evidence position in this box is not like the ones above — the guideline sentence quoted earlier defines who it applies to by the treated volume (whole breast, with or without the low axilla), not by node status; and the UK's START-B enrolled a population that included pN0 through pN1 from the start. What actually blocks it is the provision: the first contraindication listed for the NHI hypofractionation bundled codes is nodal metastasis (quoted word for word in the next section), so once there is a positive node on the pathology report, that bundled code cannot be used even when the volume actually treated is the whole breast alone. This is an institutional limitation, not a doubt about the evidence — the two should not be blurred into one thing. You have a right to know which of the two you have been put in, and a right to ask: "If the provision changes, would 15 fractions apply to me?"

The NHI provision has the same shape as that line

Taiwan's reimbursement provision is a third leg in its own right. The indications written for the NHI breast cancer hypofractionation bundled codes 36022B (combined with a local boost, a course of 20 treatments) and 36023B (without one, 16 treatments) read: post-operative radiotherapy after local excision of the tumour for early breast cancer or carcinoma in situ, with a treatment volume that includes the whole breast and "does not include the supraclavicular, axillary or internal mammary nodes"; in the version I can find, the first contraindication listed is nodal metastasis[12] — the shape of "breast conservation plus negative nodes goes hypofractionated" is the same shape as that line in our department. The NHI's own question-and-answer document states that the fee schedule sets the treatment numbers for 36022B, 36023B and 36024B at 20, 16 and 5 — so the claims framework for five fractions exists, but I could not find the full indication text for 36024B, and the reimbursement conditions have to be confirmed with the hospital or the medical affairs office[13]. And one thing is currently in motion: the draft amendment published in the Executive Yuan Gazette on 29 October 2025 proposes deleting the contraindications from these two hypofractionation codes, with the stated reason "to align with international clinical guidelines"[14] — the NHI is moving in the international direction too. But that is a draft, not the provision in force; which version applies at the time you are treated is a question for the medical affairs office or your case manager.

"Why am I not on the shorter course?" — how to ask it out loud

With this article in your hand you can put the question in clinic in three sentences: "With my operation and my node status, am I inside the population the hypofractionation trials enrolled?" "Does the new 15-fraction data apply to my situation?" "If it does apply and the plan is still 25, is the reason the length of follow-up, the boundary of the population, or the reimbursement provision?" All three are reasonable questions, and both answers stand up — a hospital giving 16 treatments is not cutting corners, and a hospital giving 25 is not lagging behind; what differs is the judgement about how mature evidence has to be before practice changes. Once the number of treatments is settled, the machine and the posture are a separate question: see "The machine, and the breathing".

From the physician

The fractionation is a decision you may ask about, not a one-way notification. Take three sentences to your next appointment: which trial population am I in, does the new 15-fraction data apply to me, and if it stays at 25 which of the reasons is it. Whichever answer comes back, a discussion that can spell out its reasons is a good discussion.
