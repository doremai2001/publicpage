AFTER THE DIAGNOSIS

Where T3N1 actually comes from

Takes T, N and M apart box by box: how the stage is assembled out of them, where the twelve-node line came from, and what CEA can and cannot tell you.

Colon Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 11 references
#Staging#Getting Ready#Decisions

Nobody decides your stage for you — it is looked up out of those boxes on the report, and you can look it up yourself. It also settles a question that gets passed around wrongly: in 2026 colon cancer still uses AJCC 8th edition, and the articles online saying it has moved to the 9th are not talking about the bowel.

"Doctor, what stage am I?" The question usually comes with half a sheet of paper behind it, printed with something like pT3 N1b (2/16) M0, and a finger pressed on the line as though holding down a sentence he can't read.

It isn't a verdict. It records what the pathologist saw down the microscope. I'll take the boxes apart one at a time, and you'll see the stage is calculated out of them, not decided for you out of sight.

T is how deep it drilled, not how big it grew

The commonest misunderstanding. T does not measure diameter; it measures how many layers of the bowel wall the tumour has crossed. Under AJCC 8th edition, pTis is carcinoma in situ or intramucosal carcinoma, invading the lamina propria but not through the muscularis mucosae; pT1 is through the muscularis mucosae into the submucosa; pT2 is into the muscularis propria; pT3 is through the muscularis propria into the pericolonic tissue; pT4a perforates the visceral peritoneum; pT4b directly invades or is adherent to an adjacent organ or structure[1].

So a three-centimetre pT1 is a lighter stage than a one-centimetre pT3. The wall is built in layers, and the layer the cancer has crossed decides whether it has had the chance to reach the lymphatics and blood vessels. If your report says pT1 and the polyp is already out endoscopically, you face a different decision — that one is "The polyp is out. Why operate again?"

N is not "has it spread", it's "how many"

The b in N1b and the a in N2a are not decoration. pN0 means no cancer in any node removed; pN1a is one node, pN1b two to three, pN2a four to six, pN2b seven or more. A node counts as involved when the tumour inside it reaches 0.2 millimetres or more[1]. N1b (2/16) means 16 nodes were examined and 2 were involved.

Another box, pN1c, gets explained less often: nodes all clean, but tumour deposits — a clump of cancer cells with no lymph node structure attached — found in the subserosa, mesentery or non-peritonealised pericolic tissue[1]. Here I have to be honest: how a tumour deposit should be defined is still being rewritten by the expert group for the 9th edition as of 2026, and the call leans heavily on the individual pathologist's judgement[2]. Hand the same slide to two people and this box can come back different.

M is far simpler: M1a is metastasis to one site or organ with no peritoneal involvement, M1b is two or more sites or organs, M1c is metastasis to the peritoneal surface[1].

Only the three boxes together make a stage

The stage is a lookup table. The US National Cancer Institute's health professional version sets out the AJCC 8th edition combinations: T3N0M0 is IIA, T4aN0M0 is IIB, T4bN0M0 is IIC; in stage III, IIIA is T1–2 with N1/N1c or T1 with N2a, IIIB covers T1–2 with N2b, T2–3 with N2a, and T3–T4a with N1/N1c, and IIIC is T3–T4a with N2b, T4a with N2a, or T4b with N1–N2[3].

Look it up once: T3, N1, M0 lands in IIIB. I want you able to do that yourself, because a stage gets compressed in the retelling into something heavier or lighter than it is, and you hold the raw material.

A stage describes how much we know now, not how long is left. Two people with the same IIIB can go on to be very different.

The internet says it changed to the 9th edition. Is my report out of date?

No. The UICC's 9th edition of TNM was released on 3 July 2025, recommended for use from 1 January 2026, but the sites its official announcement lists as revised are nasopharynx, HPV-related oropharynx, lung, thymus, mesothelioma, appendix, anal canal, cervix, vulva and prostate — colon and rectum are not among them[4][5]. AJCC is releasing its change site by site, and its official page states all 8th edition sites remain current until a 9th edition version replaces them; colorectal adenocarcinoma has not had its turn[6].

People are validating the proposed 9th edition colon staging: single-centre data on 1,222 cases from Peking Union show 79.9% of patients would change stage on conversion[7]. That is a revision under way, not current practice. Your report uses the 8th edition and usually says so. Whether Taiwan requires pathology reports to use a particular edition, I could not find an official document from the Ministry of Health and Welfare or the NHI Administration that I could cite. If you can't see a version note, ask.

Where the number 12 came from, and why some people disagree

The pathology protocol says it outright: the National Quality Forum lists "at least 12 lymph nodes in the surgical specimen" among the key quality indicators for US colon cancer care, and explains that the chance of detecting metastasis rises with the number examined, so 12 is a minimum target, not a ceiling[1]. The original evidence is a secondary analysis of INT-0089: 3,411 patients with stage II and III colon cancer, median follow-up 8.4 years, in whom the more nodes recovered the better survival was — and the association held within the N0, N1 and N2 groups separately[8].

In plain terms, the problem with too few is not the number. It is misclassification: someone who does have nodal disease is recorded as N0 because the involved nodes were never turned up.

Using that number to grade hospitals had a question mark put against it in 2025. Analyses of CORECT-R in England (84,116 people) and SEER in the United States (287,974 people) found node positivity stopped rising in any clinically meaningful way beyond 9 nodes retrieved, while survival went on improving beyond 9 (each extra node lowering the risk of death by roughly 1%); the authors also found ten English hospitals retrieving more or fewer nodes than expected, with no effect on overall survival. They argue node yield is a surrogate marker of tumour biology and prognosis, not a hospital quality score[9]. So "my report only has 9 nodes, was the surgery poor?" does not hold up. That is not to say the number doesn't matter: too few nodes makes the stage II risk estimate unreliable, and the guidelines list it among the variables to discuss together — that layer is in "Stage II: chemotherapy or not?". Whether node yield is one of the indicators in Taiwan's cancer care quality accreditation, I could not find an official page I could obtain this time, and writing it as a gap beats guessing.

How surgery gets enough nodes belongs to "What the node count really decides"; here I only explain the number.

A high CEA means something. A normal CEA doesn't mean you're fine

CEA is a protein measured in blood. An analysis of 9,083 SEER patients with colon adenocarcinoma diagnosed in 2004 with CEA data showed a raised preoperative CEA was independently associated with worse survival, hazard ratio 1.60 (95% confidence interval 1.46–1.76; a confidence interval is the range this estimate could reasonably swing across, and one that does not cross 1 means the association holds up). A hazard ratio divides the rate at which events happen in one group by the rate in the other: 1.60 means deaths happen 60% faster, not that 60% more people die, and those are very different sentences. The same analysis carries something else worth keeping: a raised CEA at a lower stage did worse than a normal CEA at a higher stage[10].

The reverse inference does not follow. These data support "a high CEA is worse". They do not say "a normal CEA means you're fine", and a substantial share of colon cancer patients have a CEA that stays normal from beginning to end. CEA is also pushed up by things that are not cancer: in a cross-sectional study of 750 adult Korean men, median CEA in conventional cigarette users, heated tobacco product users and quitters was 2.4, 2.0 and 1.6, the cigarette group significantly higher than the other two[11]. That is one cross-sectional study in a Korean health-screening population, men only, and cannot be used to set a cut-off — but it makes one point: a slightly high CEA has more than one explanation. That applies to the reading before treatment started. A CEA rising during follow-up is for your team to interpret, not something to put down to smoking on your own.

How often CEA is checked after treatment, and how a rise is read, is what "Is closer follow-up worth it?" deals with. As for NHI reimbursement conditions for the CEA test itself, I did not verify a provision in the fee schedule I could cite, so confirm that one with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.

Spread the report on the desk and point at it

Next appointment, don't only ask what stage you are. Open the paper out, point, and ask:

  • What are my T and my N? Which edition of the staging system does this report use?

  • How many lymph nodes were examined? If it is fewer than 12, how do you read that?

  • Does the report mention tumour deposits, lymphovascular invasion or perineural invasion?

  • What was my CEA before surgery? What do you do with that number now?

The MMR, KRAS and BRAF lines further down are in "Your gene report decides more than your treatment". Rectal cancer uses the same TNM system, but its treatment logic differs from colon cancer and this series does not cover it.

From the physician

A stage describes how much is known now, not how long is left, and two people with the same IIIB can turn out very differently. One thing you can do today: turn to the pTNM line of the report and copy the edition, the T, the N and the total number of nodes examined onto a piece of paper, then point at it and ask at your next appointment.
