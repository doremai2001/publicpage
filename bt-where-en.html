WHERE EVERYONE STARTS

Where it sits matters more than how big it is

That location word on the report decides who picks you up first, which extra test belongs in the first round, and which direction the follow-up scans should be watching.

Benign Brain Tumour Guide · Where everyone starts · 13 references
#Imaging#Staging#Neurosurgery#Decisions

Size has not been thrown away; it is still sitting in the prognostic model as a parameter. This article does one thing only: it reverses the order in which you ask.

The number I get asked about most in clinic is centimetres. "Doctor, how many centimetres is mine?" Of course size has to be measured. But if I could keep only one piece of information in my head, I would keep the location. The same 2 cm, sitting on the surface of the frontal lobe, may go ten years without you noticing it; sitting on the tuberculum sellae, it may already be eating the sight in one eye. Every one of the correspondences below has a primary source behind it. Not one of them is me looking at an anatomy diagram and reasoning it out.

Meningiomas have a distribution, and it is not an even one

A review pooling several series gives ranges rather than single numbers, and I am copying them as they stand: convexity 20 to 37%; parasagittal 13 to 22% (of which falx 5%); skull base 43 to 51% in total, which breaks down into anterior skull base 10 to 20%, sphenoid wing and middle fossa 9 to 36%, posterior fossa 6 to 15% (of which cerebellopontine angle 2 to 11% and petroclival less than 1% to 9%); intraventricular 1 to 5%; orbital less than 1% to 2%; spinal 7 to 12%[1]. The spinal entry needs care: the American brain tumour registry counts the spinal meninges under a different site code[2], so the denominator is not the same one.

Location and grade are related too. The same review is direct about it: grade 1 meningiomas are more often at the skull base, while higher-grade ones turn up more often at the convexity, parasagittally, on the falx, at the torcular and intraventricularly[1]. A pathology analysis of 2,608 meningiomas points the same way: the Ki-67 proliferation index of skull base meningiomas was significantly lower than that of convexity meningiomas, and it held up after stratifying within the same grade[3]. How the grade is assigned is in "How a meningioma gets called grade 1, 2 or 3".

Same tumour, different address, and the whole set of symptoms changes

Start with the overall picture. The symptom mix for meningioma pooled in that same review runs: headache 33.3 to 36.7%, focal cranial nerve deficit 28.8 to 31.3%, seizure 16.9 to 24.6%, cognitive change 14.4%, weakness 11.1%, and no symptoms at all in 9.4%; and skull base meningiomas present more often with a neurological deficit, non-skull-base meningiomas more often with a seizure[1].

Now location by location. Every line below is about meningioma and only about meningioma — the same address can grow a different tumour, and this set of correspondences cannot be carried over to another one (all of the following come from the same review[1]):

  • Anterior cranial fossa meningioma (olfactory groove, orbitofrontal, anterior falcine): often already large by the time of diagnosis; impaired vision 54%, headache 48%, loss of smell 40%, seizure 20%, sometimes with behavioural change amounting to a breakdown of personality. Those percentages come from a population that was already large at diagnosis and cannot be applied to a small incidental tumour.

  • Parasagittal meningioma: can reach a considerable size before causing anything; commonly presents with a Jacksonian seizure starting in the leg, or with headache; as it progresses, characteristically papilloedema and a homonymous hemianopia.

  • Tuberculum sellae meningioma: it "usually presents with insidious unilateral visual loss, followed by a scotoma in the other eye" — note the order, one eye slowly going, then the other. That is the meningioma route; a pituitary tumour in the same region takes a different one, which is in the pituitary section below.

  • Lateral sphenoid wing meningioma: commonly presents with painless unilateral proptosis, followed by visual loss on the same side.

  • Petroclival meningioma: gait ataxia and cranial neuropathies, for instance trigeminal involvement. Anterior clinoid meningioma: visual disturbance of various kinds, cranial nerve palsies, proptosis.

  • Posterior fossa meningioma: can cause obstructive hydrocephalus, presenting as papilloedema and early-morning headache. Meningioma around the torcular: focal occipital pain, papilloedema, homonymous field deficits, with ataxia and nystagmus.

  • Spinal meningioma: most often thoracic, presenting as a slowly progressive spastic paresis, sometimes with radicular or night pain; the cervical spine and craniocervical junction are the second commonest.

  • Meningioma sitting against bone: local hyperostosis, which the review says is "almost invariably a sign of bone invasion by meningioma cells", and which can cause a lump and pain.

The cerebellopontine angle is not only where acoustic neuromas live

This is the address that gets misread most often. Two to 11% of meningiomas are in the cerebellopontine angle[1], and it is also where epidermoid cysts like to sit — that surgical series of 17 cases was collected from exactly here[4].

And vestibular schwannoma (also called acoustic neuroma) divides again within this address. The Danish national cohort separates those confined to the internal auditory canal from those extending into the cerebellopontine angle; the natural history is different and the statistics are kept apart[5]. So when a report says "cerebellopontine angle lesion", that sentence has not yet finished naming the disease.

Hearing worse on one side: when does that earn a scan?

The American Congress of Neurological Surgeons' 2018 screening guideline gives a threshold that can be quoted word for word: on the audiogram, MRI screening is suggested for people with an interaural difference of 10 dB or more at two or more adjacent frequencies, or of 15 dB or more at a single frequency, in order to reduce the number of undiagnosed vestibular schwannomas; if the aim is instead to reduce the number of scans that find nothing, screening can be restricted to those with an interaural difference of 15 dB or more at the single frequency of 3000 Hz[6].

That line is written for the doctor ordering the scan. It is not for you to hold your own audiogram up against. In your hands it should turn into one question only: given this audiogram of mine, which threshold are you using, and does it mean a scan? The two versions have different sensitivity and specificity, and that trade-off belongs to the neuro-otologist.

The same guideline also writes down the unflattering side. Of MRI scans done for asymmetric tinnitus, fewer than 1% turn up a vestibular schwannoma; of those done for sudden sensorineural hearing loss, fewer than 3%[6]. Those two numbers support "it should be looked into" and "the chance of finding something is actually low" at the same time, and both sentences have to stay.

Taiwan has wording of its own. The National Health Insurance review guidance for medical expenses, item number 100901030, lists ten clinical situations in which "a CT or MRI may reasonably be arranged for an otological patient", among them an auditory brainstem response suspicious for a retrocochlear lesion, unilateral hearing loss greater than 65 dB at 2 to 4 kHz, a poor speech discrimination score, focal neurological signs, pulsatile tinnitus, and unusual vertigo or central-type nystagmus[7]. Its threshold is not the same ruler as the guideline's — one talks about the difference in dB between the two ears, the other about how many dB one ear has lost. I am setting them side by side and not judging which is the looser.

Pituitary tumour: the direction it grows decides what you notice first

For a pituitary tumour, "location" is really "direction", and a 2022 review sets out five directions and their consequences very clearly[8]. Upwards is the commonest, because the diaphragma sellae and its aperture are a weak barrier to an expanding tumour, and the tumour may compress and damage the optic nerves and chiasm; in people whose chiasm sits more posteriorly the tumour grows forwards under the frontal lobe, and in those whose chiasm sits more anteriorly it grows backwards into the third ventricle and hypothalamus. Downwards it remodels, enlarges and resorbs the bone of the sella, leaving a path for extension into the sphenoid sinus. Forwards it invades the planum sphenoidale, the inferior surface of the frontal lobe and the ethmoid sinuses. Backwards it expands into the interpeduncular cistern and towards the brainstem. Laterally it expands into or invades the cavernous sinus. Invasion is not rare: "as many as 35% of adenoma types demonstrate gross invasion", and the proportion is higher for macroadenomas[8].

Which is why the Endocrine Society's incidentaloma guideline lays it down: anyone whose lesion abuts or compresses the optic nerves and chiasm should have visual field testing, and during follow-up at 6 months and then annually[9]. What patients report first, a Swedish study of 654 people separates out cleanly: for non-functioning adenoma it is headache and visual disturbance, for prolactinoma menstrual irregularity and headache, for acromegaly a change in appearance and snoring, and for Cushing's disease weight gain and fatigue[10]. How functioning status is established is in "First, find out whether it is secreting".

Location matters, which is not the same as saying size can be ignored

The title of this article is a rhetorical device, not a conclusion. In the meta-analysis of 2,130 people with incidental meningioma, a diameter of 3 cm or more was a factor associated with developing symptoms during active monitoring, but its 95% confidence interval — the range the answer would be expected to land in if the same study were run many times — was wide enough that only the direction can be stated, not a multiple[11]. And in a prognostic model built on 441 people with 459 incidental meningiomas, increasing tumour volume carried a hazard ratio of 2.17 (95% confidence interval 1.53 to 3.09), a hazard ratio being the ratio of the rate at which events occur in two groups[12]. Size is one of the parameters in the model. It is not something that can be thrown away.

The reverse has to be said as well: however typical the location, some people feel nothing at all — that 9.4% of meningiomas with no symptoms is exactly how they get stumbled upon[1]. The same address can also be a different grade; the European Association of Neuro-Oncology states plainly that there is considerable individual variation between grade and clinical course[13].

So what does your tumour's address actually decide?

Location is not a table for you to look yourself up in. The correspondences above are at the level of populations; reasoning backwards from a symptom to a location, or forwards from a location to what will happen to you, both go wrong. What location genuinely decides is three things: who picks you up first (against the optic pathway and ophthalmology and endocrinology are involved; in the internal auditory canal and neuro-otology is); what extra has to be measured in the first round (visual fields, hearing, hormones — whichever one it is, it must not be left out); and which direction the follow-up scans are watching (growing upwards and growing laterally are not the same thing to look at).

At your next appointment, put the question in this shape: which structure is mine touching or pressing on? Because of that location, what extra should the first round of tests include? Which direction should the follow-up imaging be paying particular attention to? Write the three answers down and you have your priorities for the year. How the timetable is set is in "Watching is a form of treatment".

From the physician

I read a report in the opposite order from most people: the location word first, the centimetres second. Location decides which specialty picks you up over the coming year and what extra gets measured; the centimetres decide how often that measurement is repeated. The correspondences themselves are statistics at the level of populations, not a table to look yourself up in.
