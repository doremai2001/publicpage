WHAT CANNOT WAIT

A fever the night after a vaccine

Post-vaccination fever is common, but on chemotherapy it explains nothing away. The live-vaccine line, funded eligibility, and who sets timing.

Patient Education · What Cannot Wait · 14 references
#Vaccine#Prevention#Chemotherapy

Two things you can act on today: if you have had immunosuppressive therapy or radiotherapy for a malignancy within the past year, you may already qualify for the publicly funded pneumococcal vaccine; and family living with you need not postpone their own vaccinations because you are in treatment. Timing, though, is arranged with your team, not by you.

"A fever after a vaccination is normal, just rest." That holds for most people. It does not hold for you while you are on chemotherapy. I am putting it first because the dangerous thing about vaccines here is not the vaccine — it is that a vaccine can be used to explain away a fever that must not be explained away.

First, block the most dangerous sentence: a vaccination is not a reason to stay away from the emergency department

Reactions after vaccination are normal and common. In ZOE-70, 13,900 people aged 70 and over, local or systemic reactions within seven days occurred in 79.0% against 29.5% on placebo[1]; in people who had just had an autologous stem cell transplant, injection-site reactions reached 86%, with grade 3 pain in 11%[2]. A sore arm, tiredness, aching muscles, sometimes feeling hot — all expected, and knowing that in advance saves a panic at midnight.

But read this with it. While you are on chemotherapy, you cannot use "I was vaccinated yesterday" as a reason not to go to the emergency department. Fever after vaccination usually comes within 48 hours, without rigors or other signs of infection — but that is not a judgement you make at home. During chemotherapy, one ear or axillary reading of 38.0°C or above is treated as febrile neutropenia: go to the emergency department, say "I am on chemotherapy", and tell them which vaccine you had and when. That is information for the doctor, not a reason to stay home. "The fever that cannot wait until morning" sets out the threshold and what to do.

Two things can be true at the same time: the fever really did come from the vaccine and really will settle by itself, and you are in the few days when your counts are at their lowest. Telling those apart takes a blood test.

The real line is drawn at live attenuated vaccines

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's chapter on altered immunocompetence is explicit: most people with impaired immune function should not receive live attenuated vaccines, and the list includes MMR, varicella, MMRV, live attenuated (nasal spray) influenza vaccine, yellow fever, oral typhoid Ty21a, BCG, smallpox and rotavirus; nor should they be given for at least three months after immunosuppressive therapy ends[3]. Non-live vaccines do not have that line drawn across them; their question is how much effect they lose, not whether they are safe.

The specialist document is the Infectious Diseases Society of America's 2013 guideline on vaccinating the immunocompromised host, covering adults and children; its own abstract concedes the evidence was often limited[4]. If the answer you get in clinic comes with reservations, that is not the doctor holding back. The evidence is simply like that.

Zoster vaccine: Taiwan licenses only the non-live one, and the efficacy figure depends on the population

Taiwan first, because it simplifies things. Taiwan's Centers for Disease Control states that the zoster vaccine licensed in Taiwan is the non-live recombinant vaccine; those advised to have it include adults aged 50 and over and people aged 18 and over who are immunodeficient or immunosuppressed, as 2 doses 2 to 6 months apart, and it is self-funded[5]. In Taiwan you will not find yourself accidentally given a live attenuated zoster vaccine. Price and availability are questions for the clinic giving it.

Now efficacy, where the misreading happens. The 97.2% you see online comes from ZOE-50 — 15,411 immunocompetent adults aged 50 and over, mean follow-up 3.2 years[6]; ZOE-70 gave 89.8% in those aged 70 and over[1]. The population closest to this article's readers is ZOE-HSCT: 1,846 adults who had recently completed an autologous stem cell transplant, median follow-up 21 months, efficacy 68.2% (incidence rate ratio 0.32, 0.22–0.44); postherpetic neuralgia in 1 case against 9; serious adverse events and relapse of the underlying disease similar in both groups[2].

68.2% is still meaningful protection. It is not 97%. A separate overview across five immunocompromised populations (including patients on chemotherapy for solid tumours) reports that the recombinant vaccine produced persistent immune responses even when given close to maximum immunosuppression, and even alongside chemotherapy cycles[7] — that document was written by employees of the manufacturer and measured immune response rather than clinical protection, so I write "response" and not "protection".

Pneumococcal: you may already be eligible for the funded programme without knowing it

This is the section I most want people to see. On Taiwan CDC's adult pneumococcal vaccine page, the publicly funded groups include, besides adults aged 65 and over and indigenous people aged 55 to 64, people aged 19 to 64 at high risk of invasive pneumococcal disease — and the high-risk definition states in so many words "malignancy treated with immunosuppressive therapy or radiotherapy within the past year", along with impaired splenic function, immunodeficiency, cochlear implants, cerebrospinal fluid leak and organ transplantation[8]. The 20-valent or 21-valent conjugate vaccine may be given: one dose if never vaccinated; one dose if the 23-valent polysaccharide vaccine was given at least a year ago; none if already fully vaccinated[8].

A great many patients in treatment have no idea they are already on that list. Whether it applies to you, and when to have it, is something to confirm with the vaccinating clinic or your case manager (個管師) — this one is worth making a phone call for.

Influenza vaccine: weak evidence, consistent direction; timing less fiddly than you think

The strength of the evidence first. A Cochrane review of influenza vaccination in adults with cancer included 6 studies and 2,275 participants: two observational studies found fewer deaths among the vaccinated (adjusted hazard ratio 0.88, 95% CI 0.78–1.00; odds ratio 0.42, 0.24–0.75), but the certainty was graded very low; the one randomised trial reporting mortality found no difference (odds ratio 1.25, 0.43–3.62, 78 participants). In plain words: a hazard ratio compares event rates in two groups, 1 meaning the same rate; an odds ratio divides one group's odds of the event against no event by the other's and is not a multiple of probability — 0.42 does not mean "nearly 60% less chance of dying"; the interval in brackets is where the estimate plausibly sits. No life-threatening or persistent adverse effects were observed. The authors conclude that the evidence, though weak, is consistent in direction, that benefits outweigh harms, and that a further placebo-controlled trial here would be ethically questionable[9].

On timing there is a genuine randomised answer. 97 adults with solid tumours on three-weekly cytotoxic chemotherapy were randomised to influenza vaccination on day 1 (with chemotherapy) or day 11 (the count nadir), 83 analysable: seroprotection did not differ (H1N1 67% against 75%, p=.403; H3N2 77% against 80%; B 21% against 27%), but vaccine-related adverse events were commoner in the day-11 group (13% against 32%, p=.040)[10]. One trial, 97 patients, solid tumours on three-weekly schedules; it does not carry over to weekly regimens or transplant patients. Timing is the team's decision, not something you arrange yourself.

In Taiwan, the funded groups under the 2026 (民國115年度) influenza vaccination programme include adults aged 50 and over and people with underlying conditions, the latter explicitly listing "holders of catastrophic illness status (a note on the NHI card or a paper Catastrophic Illness Certificate)"; the programme uses inactivated vaccine[11]. Most cancer patients hold that status and are covered; the carer living with you may not be, so ask the vaccinating clinic. Taiwan CDC's page carries no question-and-answer on whether or when to vaccinate during chemotherapy, and I will not invent one — settle timing with your treating team.

Some drugs blunt a vaccine, but that is not "no point having it"

The clearest effect is from anti-CD20 targeted drugs. A meta-analysis of 23 studies and 1,342 people previously treated with anti-CD20 antibodies found a humoral response rate to COVID-19 vaccines of 0.40 (0.35–0.47) and a cell-mediated rate of 0.71 (0.57–0.87); the interval was decisive — over 6 months since the last dose gave 0.63, under 6 months only 0.20 (p<0.01)[12]. That study mixed haematological malignancy, autoimmune disease and kidney transplant recipients; not a pure cancer population. In cancer patients specifically, 20 cohort studies and 5,499 people found anti-CD20 drugs within the past year and chemotherapy both associated with poor seroconversion, while targeted therapy, immunotherapy and hormonal therapy showed no significant difference[13].

Patients on immune checkpoint inhibitors often ask whether a vaccine will provoke immune-related side effects. Using Ontario health administrative data, a self-controlled case series analysed 1,133 records of influenza vaccination during immunotherapy, taking emergency visits or hospitalisation as a proxy for severe immune-related events: the incidence rate ratio for days 1 to 42 after vaccination against control periods was 1.04 (0.75–1.45)[14]. A proxy measure from administrative data, mostly elderly, mostly lung cancer, mostly PD-1 inhibitors — so I will not say it is proved safe, only that no signal has been seen. The rule in the other direction is unchanged: a new symptom during immunotherapy — diarrhoea, rash, cough, breathlessness, fatigue, thirst, palpitations — is not to be treated yourself as a cold or gastroenteritis; contact the team that day.

As for who is eligible for COVID-19 vaccination in Taiwan now, at what doses and intervals, the policy is revised annually and I could not find a citable current announcement; go by Taiwan CDC's latest notice or the vaccinating clinic.

Vaccinating your family also protects you, and three questions to ask

This costs almost nothing and almost nobody thinks of it. The US CDC recommends that household members and close contacts of people with impaired immune function receive all age- and exposure-appropriate vaccines (smallpox excepted), and that no special precautions are needed when giving those contacts live vaccines, apart from care around varicella-vaccine rash and rotavirus-vaccine shedding[3]. If there are children at home due for vaccinations, they need not be postponed because you are in treatment — tell the team the dates.

Three questions for your next appointment. First, with the treatment I am on, which vaccines can I have and which should wait, and for how long? Second, do I qualify for the funded pneumococcal or funded influenza vaccine? Third, if I run a fever after a vaccination, what do you want me to do? Write the answer to the third one down and stick it on the fridge. At two in the morning that piece of paper is more use than any article.

From the physician

Which vaccines you can have, and how long you must wait, depends directly on the class of drug you are on — the answer for someone who had an anti-CD20 drug within the past year is not the answer for someone on hormonal therapy alone. Ask one question at your next appointment: if I run a fever after a vaccination, what do you want me to do? Write the answer down and put it on the fridge.
