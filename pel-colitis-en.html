DURING TREATMENT AND AFTER

Bowel reactions: when they come, how to get through

It starts in the second week, the last week is the worst, and it runs at least another one to two weeks after the course ends; for anyone on a hypofractionated course, the symptoms may not begin until the treatment is over.

Pelvic Radiotherapy Guide · During Treatment and After · 5 references

On diet, one and the same guideline says two opposite things: what it names is high fibre rather than low residue, and it also says that dietary interventions as a whole have insufficient evidence and should not be used outside a clinical trial. So this article hands you no list, only the people to ask.

The bowel runs out of step with what people expect, twice. At the beginning: the first few fractions pass with nothing happening and you decide you must be one of the lucky ones. And at the end: the last fraction goes in and the trouble does not stop with it — for one group of people, it does not even start until the course is over.

My position first: pelvic radiotherapy is what I do every day, so every good word about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it. Implants such as the rectal spacer are not something my department provides; I write about them as an option you can go and ask about, and for cost and for who performs them, please ask that department and the medical affairs office.

Four stretches of time, and the guideline names all four

The 2025 practice guidance from the BSG, the British Society of Gastroenterology, on gastrointestinal side effects of cancer treatment is specific about this: acute gastrointestinal symptoms usually appear in the second week after radiotherapy starts, tend to peak in the final week of the course, and continue for at least a further one to two weeks after treatment ends[1]. The fourth stretch belongs to people on few fractions at a high dose per fraction: the same guidance says that in those having hypofractionated courses, acute symptoms may not begin until after the course has finished[1].

Those four sentences are clinical practice statements written into a guideline, not a curve calculated by a meta-analysis, and the level of evidence has to be labelled as such. If you are having the five-fraction short course for rectal cancer and you walk out on the last day thinking you have got away with it, only to start opening your bowels loosely a week later — that is in the guideline. You are not imagining it. The week-by-week acute diarrhoea curve for rectal cancer is in "Ten trips to the toilet and nothing feels finished", and the difference between a cumulative incidence and a point prevalence is in "That 13.7% is being read wrong"; I am not repeating either block here.

How many people, and to what grade — this box holds only late figures

The one set of hard numbers I can give comes from the EMBRACE-I prospective multicentre cohort, and the labels travel with it, the first one mattering most: these are "late" figures, not figures from during the course. The population is cervical cancer, treated with concurrent chemoradiotherapy plus image-guided brachytherapy; this analysis included 1,199 patients with a median follow-up of 48 months. The two commonest late gastrointestinal symptoms at grade 2 or worse were diarrhoea at 8.5% and flatulence at 9.9%; grade 3 or worse, split by site, was 2.8% for anus and rectum, 1.8% for the sigmoid, and 2.3% for colon and small bowel[2]. The analysis in the same cohort looking specifically at late diarrhoea used crude incidence and reported grade 2 or worse at 8.3% and grade 3 or worse at 1.5%, with persistent grade 1 or worse symptoms in 16% and 7% of patients rating themselves as persistently bothered "quite a bit" or more[3].

The proportion during the course is a different matter, and a great deal higher. In the randomised gynaecological trial cited in this topic's "What you buy is side effects, not survival" — endometrial and cervical cancer after surgery, randomised allocation, patient-reported, and measured at the end of the course — the proportion reporting "frequent or almost constant diarrhoea" was 33.7% in the modulated arm and 51.9% in the conventional-technique arm[4]. During the acute phase most people do open their bowels loosely, and that is the norm; the ones who reach grade 3 are a minority, and mostly it happens in the acute phase rather than years later. The two sets have to be read side by side, otherwise "how much is left a few years on" gets misread as "how much there will be over these few weeks". The figure to keep in mind is that 16%: not severe, but never fully gone. As for tenesmus, the sensation of needing to go with nothing to pass, and mucus in the stool, this cohort reported proctitis, bleeding and diarrhoea as separate items and gave no percentage for either of those two, and I am not going to invent one for you.

Same pelvis, so why do some people barely notice

On dose, the gap is clear enough. The same EMBRACE-I analysis calculated 3-year grade 2 or worse diarrhoea at 9.5% with a 45 Gy prescription and 19.9% at 50 Gy; at 8.7% where the volume receiving 43 Gy was under 2,500 cubic centimetres and 14.0% where it was over 3,000; and among those having a nodal boost, at 9.4% where the volume receiving 57 Gy was under 165 cubic centimetres and 19.0% where it reached 165 or more[3]. Those are variables at the treatment end, and you cannot change them.

The patient end is a different story. The same analysis names pre-existing diarrhoea before treatment, smoking and diabetes, with hazard ratios — the relative rate at which events accumulate over time — falling between 1.4 and 7.3[3]; the overall gastrointestinal analysis in the same cohort adds increasing age and a low body mass index[2]. The BSG guidance turns this into one sentence you can act on: smoking and a low body mass index both raise the risk of toxicity, and where possible they should be addressed before radiotherapy is given[1].

On diet, the same guideline says two opposite things

Both sentences have to sit side by side; copying out only one of them misleads people. The first is in the BSG's recommendation statements: dietary counselling and protein supplementation "may" reduce the risk of toxicity during pelvic radiotherapy; probiotics containing lactobacilli and bifidobacteria "may" reduce radiotherapy-related acute diarrhoea; and a high-fibre diet "may" reduce the risk of toxicity during pelvic radiotherapy[1]. Note the direction: what the guideline names is high fibre, not low residue.

The second sentence is in the body of the same document, and it is about dietary interventions as a whole — elemental diets, low fat, low lactose and fibre modification included: most of them have a reasonable scientific rationale, but the available data are insufficient to determine which of them genuinely helps; all of them may impose a burden, and they should not be used outside the context of a clinical trial[1].

Both sentences come from the same guideline, so I am not going to tell you that low residue does not work, and I am not going to tell you to go and eat high fibre. There is only one practical conclusion: take diet to your dietitian and your treatment team, and do not work through a list off the internet until you are malnourished. The probiotic line cannot be copied out on its own either — people who are immunocompromised carry a risk of bacteraemia, and it has to be stopped when the white count is low; the full version is in "Ten trips to the toilet and nothing feels finished". Do not start one without your treating team's agreement.

Diarrhoea is not automatically the radiotherapy's doing

The BSG's principles for handling acute diarrhoea are clear enough: stool should be sent for infection screening; starting loperamide while the results are awaited is generally safe; and the patient should be reassessed regularly to exclude toxic dilatation of the colon[1].

So what proportion of diarrhoea in radiotherapy patients is actually Clostridioides difficile infection? I could not find out. In the BSG's table of physiological abnormalities, the boxes for C. difficile during and after radiotherapy are both filled with a question mark, annotated to mean that there are no published data and no clinical experience[1]. There is a sentence in the guidance saying that stool culture should be done to exclude C. difficile before antidiarrhoeals are used, but that sentence is addressed to haematopoietic stem cell transplant patients[1] — a different population, and the rule cannot simply be carried across.

For the acute phase, the guideline says the evidence is not there

I am copying the summary verdict out as it stands: on managing toxicity during treatment, the BSG's judgement is that "management remains empirical as evidence is lacking"[1]. So every acute-phase measure, mine included, has to be read next to that sentence.

A few boundaries are firm, though. For anyone on concurrent chemotherapy, grade 3 or grade 4 diarrhoea means the chemotherapy has to stop, with a dose reduction if it is restarted afterwards[1]. For anyone bleeding rectally during the course, the guidance's clinical experience is that if there has been no recent lower gastrointestinal endoscopy or CT colonography, the prudent approach is to arrange a flexible sigmoidoscopy six weeks after treatment ends[1]. Both of those investigations have their own codes in the full NHI fee schedule: 28013C sigmoidoscopy at 1,069 points and 28017C colonoscopy at 2,363 points, the latter noted as restricted to particular specialists[5].

There is one piece of positive evidence: a single-centre feasibility randomised trial in which a multidisciplinary team rapidly screened for lactose intolerance, small bowel bacterial overgrowth and bile acid malabsorption during chemoradiotherapy and then individualised nutritional support accordingly produced better outcomes[1]. Finding the cause and then treating it beats applying a generic list.

Which kind of diarrhoea cannot wait at home

For any of the following, contact the team the same day or get seen; do not hold out until your next appointment. Fever, especially with diarrhoea — if you are having chemotherapy, this one means contacting the team the same day or going to A&E, and saying up front that you are having chemotherapy and radiotherapy; do not take a fever medicine at home and watch. Diarrhoea with dehydration: blacking out on standing, passing markedly less urine, a dry mouth. Worsening abdominal pain, an abdomen distended and hard to the touch, feeling sick with no wind and no stool coming out. Passing a large amount of fresh blood or clots. And one more sentence: however bad it gets, never decide on your own to skip today's fraction or push the course back. If you cannot cope, tell the team the same day so they can adjust your supportive medication; whether to pause is the doctor's call — that is a decision taken with you, not one made at home.

Turn these weeks into a table someone can read in five seconds

A treatment team can only deal with what it knows about. What I want is not much: two lines a day — how many times, and how loose; whether there was blood, mucus or tenesmus. That page takes five seconds to read in clinic, and it is worth a great deal more than "it has been a bit odd these last few days".

Two other things can be started now. If you smoke, this is one of the two things the guideline explicitly says should be addressed before radiotherapy, and the referral to a smoking cessation clinic can be written today. And book one appointment with a dietitian, and lay out everything you are currently eating, item by item. Bowel function after surgery for rectal cancer is a separate matter, and that article is "Keeping the anus is not the same as keeping control".

From the physician

Diarrhoea is not automatically the radiotherapy's doing, and stool testing is a necessary step — do not skip it on your own. Smoking and weight are the two things the guideline explicitly says should be addressed before radiotherapy, and both the smoking cessation referral and the dietitian appointment can be arranged today. And however bad it gets, never decide on your own to miss today's fraction.
