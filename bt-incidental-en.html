WHERE EVERYONE STARTS

They found something on the scan

Three-quarters of the people referred on did not go to an operation that day — but "no operation" and "case closed" are two different things, and telling them apart takes denominators, measurement methods, and knowing what the thing is actually touching.

The two things this article has to block run in opposite directions, and the tool that blocks both is the same one: denominators and rulers. "How common is it" has three denominators, "will it grow" has two rulers, and swapping the ruler turns the answer round.

The last line of the report usually reads something like this: a homogeneously enhancing dural-based lesion over the right frontal convexity, suspicious for meningioma, follow-up advised. You may have had the scan because you banged your head, because you had been dizzy for two months, or because you added it on to a health check. The first sentence I hear in clinic is almost always "doctor, is this cancer", and the second is "so do I need an operation". Both are asked too early — there is a whole stretch of ground in between that nobody has walked you across yet, and that stretch decides what your next year is going to be like.

One question, three ways of scanning, three different answers

"How common is this sort of thing" has no single answer. It depends on who was scanned and on what machine. The Rotterdam cohort study put 5,800 members of the general public, mean age 64.9, through a research brain MRI: 9.5% had an incidental finding, of which meningioma 2.5% (143 people) and cerebral aneurysm 2.3%[1]. Count it another way and the number moves: a meta-analysis — the pooling of several studies into one estimate — of 16 studies and 19,559 people with no neurological symptoms put neoplastic incidental findings at only 0.70% (95% confidence interval 0.47 to 0.98% — run the same study many times over and the answer would land somewhere in that range); within that same analysis, high-resolution sequences found 4.3% and standard-resolution ones 1.7%[2]. The better the machine, the more it finds.

The pituitary is a separate matter, and it is not an ordinary brain MRI that finds it. A 1994 study took 100 normal volunteers and gave them a high-resolution contrast-enhanced MRI dedicated to the pituitary: 10% had lesions of 3 to 6 mm read as pituitary adenoma by at least two readers[3]. That 10% cannot be used to talk about a general brain MRI. The European Association of Neuro-Oncology's 2021 meningioma guideline has its own wording again: incidental meningiomas appear in "0.9% to 1.0%" of brain MRIs in the general population[4].

The third tumour changes the units altogether. A systematic review pooling Danish, Dutch, Taiwanese and American populations puts the incidence of sporadic vestibular schwannoma (also called acoustic neuroma) at 3.0 to 5.2 per 100,000 person-years; the small slice of that which is asymptomatic and incidentally diagnosed was, in the American 2012 to 2016 figure, 1.3 per 100,000 person-years[5]. These numbers are not fighting each other; they were never measuring the same thing. The meta-analysis's own closing sentence belongs here too: findings like these are "worth mentioning when obtaining informed consent for a brain MRI, but are not sufficient to support screening of healthy asymptomatic people"[2]. I am not going to suggest you buy yourself a brain MRI for peace of mind.

"Most do not come to harm" and "most do not grow" are two different sentences

This is the misunderstanding I most want to take apart: the same thing, measured with a different ruler, gives you the opposite answer.

Measured by volume: a prospective study followed 64 people with an incidental meningioma for five years and defined growth as 15% more than baseline. Forty-eight of them (75%) grew in volume, 13 were unchanged and 3 shrank[6]. Measured by a composite "disease progression" endpoint — growth, the appearance of symptoms, meningioma-related death and the loss of the window for curative treatment, all counted together — a cohort across 33 centres in 15 countries, 1,248 people and 1,010 untreated meningiomas with a median follow-up of 61 months, showed 88.1% progression-free at five years and 85.7% at ten[7].

Seventy-five per cent growing, and 85% free of progression at ten years, are true at the same time. What conflicts is not the tumour; it is the definition of "grew". The authors of the volume study wrote their own conclusion: not one patient developed a tumour-related symptom within those five years, and more than six in ten showed a self-limiting growth pattern[6]. So the sentence that is actually correct is "most of them do not end up causing trouble" — with the ruler named in the same breath. This is not hair-splitting. A systematic review of 33 observational studies found that of 268 outcomes reported in their own words, only 77 had a definition, and they could not be meaningfully pooled[8].

Three-quarters of those referred did not end up in an operating theatre — which is not the same as the case being closed

Back to the 5,800. One hundred and eighty-eight (3.2%) were referred on to a specialist because of an incidental finding, and of those, 144 (76.6%) either continued on observation or were discharged after the first appointment[1].

A meta-analysis of 20 studies and 2,130 people with incidental meningioma gives the spread of what was done at diagnosis: surgery 27.3%, stereotactic radiosurgery 22.0%, active monitoring 50.7%. What the authors put in their conclusion is that "intervening at the point of diagnosis may lead to unnecessary overtreatment"[9]. The same analysis, together with its 2019 correction, gives the other side as well: 8.4% of those on active monitoring developed symptoms during follow-up (2.8 to 16.7%), and 24.8% went on to have an intervention in the end[9][10]. So the three-quarters describes "did not go to an operation at that moment", not "this is over". And in that cohort of 1,248, 383 people (40.5%) died of something that had nothing to do with the meningioma, while it was still quiet and while they were still untreated[7]. That single sentence blocks the misreading in both directions.

Which situations were never a watching question in the first place

The three paragraphs below come from three specialties' guidelines. Each one governs its own tumour, and they do not add up to one general checklist.

The pituitary adenoma paragraph is the most concrete. The Endocrine Society's 2011 incidentaloma guideline lists the circumstances in which it recommends referral for surgery: a visual field deficit; other visual abnormality caused by tumour compression, such as ophthalmoplegia; neurological compromise caused by the lesion pressing on something; a lesion already abutting the optic nerves or the optic chiasm; pituitary apoplexy with visual disturbance; or an incidentaloma that is a hypersecreting tumour other than a prolactinoma[11].

The meningioma paragraph is short, in the European Association of Neuro-Oncology's own words — "for most symptomatic or growing meningiomas, surgery is the first-line treatment"[4]. The vestibular schwannoma paragraph has to be understood back to front: hearing loss already present on one side does not mean "this has to be treated immediately", it means "this cannot be treated as nothing". The American Congress of Neurological Surgeons' 2018 hearing preservation guideline puts the chance that someone taking the observation road still has usable hearing at the tenth year at only "moderately low (greater than 25% to 50%)"[12]. How hearing is measured is in "Hearing is what this decision is really about".

What "benign" and "malignant" mean inside the skull

The American brain tumour registry counts non-malignant tumours as well. In its 2018 to 2022 data, five-year relative survival — survival set against what would be expected in people of the same age and sex in the general population — was 91.7% for non-malignant tumours and 34.8% for malignant ones[13]. The distance between those two figures is the weight the word "benign" is carrying; and 91.7% is not 100%, which is equally true.

The breakdown from the same registry for 2017 to 2021: 195,297 meningiomas in all, incidence 10.15 per 100,000, of which malignant meningiomas number only 1,608, incidence 0.08[14]. On the pituitary side, a group of endocrinologists cite these: local invasion in only one in every 2,000, malignancy in only one in every 100,000[15]. The equals sign between "brain tumour" and "brain cancer" does not hold for these three diseases; a genuinely malignant brain tumour is a different illness, and there is a separate article on the site, "Glioblastoma: what it is, and what it is not".

Risk inside the skull does not come from histology alone. The European Association of Neuro-Oncology writes down something counter-intuitive: a grade 1 meningioma can still recur early, and someone whose grade 2 was completely resected can go a long time with nothing happening — there is considerable individual variation between grade and clinical course[4]. A grade 1 tumour sitting against the optic nerve and one on the surface of the frontal lobe are two different situations. See "Where it sits matters more than how big it is".

A disease label can change what you decide, and somebody has already measured that

After the 2022 World Health Organization classification renamed well-differentiated adenohypophyseal tumours neuroendocrine tumours, a group of pituitary physicians published a public objection, and one passage of it props up this entire article: patients with low-risk disease are "less likely to benefit from treatment and more likely to suffer the adverse consequences of intervention, anxiety from the disease label, and the financial harm of additional and often unnecessary testing and treatment". They cite a randomised trial in which renaming the same disease from "papillary thyroid cancer" to "papillary lesion" changed patients' treatment decisions and their anxiety[15].

The blade is sharp on both sides. The words "brain tumour" send someone with a grade 1 convexity meningioma off to ask for an operation they do not need; the word "benign" lets someone whose pituitary tumour is sitting on the optic pathway think it can keep until next year. What is really deciding things is where it sits, whether it is moving, and whether it is doing anything. The name itself is still being fought over, and both sides are set out in "Three tumours, three different logics".

Say these out loud at this appointment

Every one of the following is a question for your doctor. Whether to treat and when to treat is a judgement your doctor makes from the location, the size, the imaging features and your overall condition.

  1. Which tumour is mine being read as, which imaging features make that reading, and do I need any extra sequences?

  2. Is it touching or pressing on anything — optic nerve, optic chiasm, internal auditory canal, brainstem, venous sinus — and which of those has to be dealt with now?

  3. If it is a pituitary lesion, should the hormone blood tests and the visual field test be arranged now? (Both belong to the initial evaluation in the incidentaloma guideline[11].)

  4. How many months until the next scan, which sequence, and what symptoms in the meantime should bring me back early? If the problem is hearing on one side, hearing should be formally measured once now, as a baseline.

  5. Which category does this thing fall into: deal with it soon, keep monitoring, or lengthen the intervals? And which findings is that based on?

Two more things on the Taiwan side. The first is imaging. The National Health Insurance review guidance for medical expenses has general principles for MRI — for instance, that repeating one within a short interval requires the necessity to be stated — but for follow-up frequency specifically in meningioma, vestibular schwannoma or pituitary tumour, I could not find a listed item anywhere in the document[16] — and not finding a listed item is not the same as there being no cover. How the scans are scheduled and claimed is a question for the hospital's medical affairs office.

The second is the catastrophic illness certificate. I have been through Appendix 1 of the Regulations for Exemption of Insured Persons from Copayment under National Health Insurance word by word. Under "cancers requiring aggressive or long-term treatment" there are five sub-items, and every one of them is a C-code malignant tumour, while D32 (benign neoplasm of meninges), D33 (benign neoplasm of brain and other parts of the central nervous system) and D35 (which covers benign pituitary tumours) return zero hits in a full-text search of the whole schedule[17]. The only line in Appendix 1 that comes anywhere near a benign brain tumour is the spinal cord one, and it carries its own threshold of "a disability rating of moderate or above"[17]. That is what the provision says; whether it applies to you is for the insurer to determine — take your diagnostic certificate to the hospital's medical affairs office and ask. What else can be applied for besides the catastrophic illness certificate has its own article on the site, "Beyond the Catastrophic Illness Certificate: what else you can claim". How watching itself is scheduled is in "Watching is a form of treatment".

From the physician

My advice is concrete. Photograph that line on the report, and at the next appointment ask three things out loud: which structure is it touching or pressing on, has the first round of tests been done in full, and which month and which sequence is the next scan. Three answers in the notes app on your phone are worth more than a month of searching online.
