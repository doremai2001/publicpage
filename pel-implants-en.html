WHO IS TREATED, AND GETTING READY

Markers and spacers: two purposes, two kinds of evidence

Fiducials help the machine see; no randomised evidence, but safety numbers with a denominator. Spacers push the rectum away; randomised evidence exists, but in the severe-toxicity cell both meta-analyses found no difference.

Two objects, both put into the body before treatment starts, and their names often end up in the same sentence. One is three metal dots smaller than a grain of rice; the other is a blob of hydrogel that the body absorbs. What they do is different: the fiducial marker's job is to let the machine see where your prostate is each day, and the spacer's job is to push the rectum away by a certain distance. More to the point, the shape of their evidence is exactly opposite — one has no randomised trial but clear safety numbers, the other has randomised trials but a benefit that stops in the mild cell.

My position first: pelvic radiotherapy is what I do every day, so every good word about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it. Implants such as the rectal spacer are not something my department provides; I write about them as an option you can go and ask about, and for cost and for who performs them, please ask that department and the medical affairs office.

Fiducials: no randomised trial, but safety numbers that come with a denominator

The commonest way of placing fiducials is under transrectal ultrasound guidance, using a fine needle to put 3 of them into the prostate. A single-centre experience of 795 patients over 5 years supplies complication rates with a denominator attached: technical success in every case, post-procedure sepsis 1%, temporary retention of urine 1.6%, only 2 patients with short-term marker migration after placement, and no further migration during the course[1]. The routes have been compared too: a systematic review and meta-analysis of 13 observational studies found that transperineal placement carries a significantly lower risk of urinary tract infection and rectal bleeding than the transrectal route, with the paper describing both routes as effective, safe and well tolerated, and the advantage of the transperineal route being that it avoids puncturing the rectum[2].

What they contribute can be measured as well. A study of 132 men having prostate VMAT across 2,659 fractions triggered a kV image every 15 seconds and interrupted for correction whenever any two markers exceeded a 3 mm tolerance: 17% of fractions were interrupted and corrected because of it, and 77% of the men were corrected at least once over the whole course; on multivariable analysis the only things associated with displacement were rectal volume and rectal width, with 47% of men whose rectal width exceeded 3.6 cm needing frequent correction against 18% of the rest (p=0.0016)[3].

Not every cancer is having fiducials put in

Prostate cancer is the fiducial's home ground, and the other cancers are at very different stages of maturity. Bladder cancer has a prospective trial: 15 patients had an absorbable radio-opaque hydrogel injected around the tumour bed at the time of endoscopic resection, and 94% of the injection sites were visible when radiotherapy started and 71% when it finished; matching to the markers allowed the planning target volume margin for the initial field to come down from 1.56 cm to 0.67 cm, with no complications attributable to placement at a median follow-up of 22 months[4]. Endometrial cancer goes no further than a 6-patient single-centre preliminary experience: all completed treatment, with no acute or chronic toxicity and no marker loss[5]. Rectal cancer stops at a 20-patient feasibility study: of 64 markers, 42 (66%) were still in place at a median of 17 days, being 55% of those placed within the tumour and 90% of those placed in the mesorectum (p=0.009)[6]. For cervical cancer, I could find no clinical series I could cite. Fiducials depend on your situation; they are not a compulsory step in the workflow.

The spacer: randomised trials, with the benefit stopping in the mild cell

A rectal spacer is a mass of hydrogel injected between the prostate and the rectum to push the two apart, and absorbed by a year later on MRI. The pivotal randomised trial enrolled 222 men with prostate cancer, allocated in a 2:1 ratio, all of whom had image-guided intensity-modulated radiotherapy to 79.2 Gy, and all of whom had fiducials placed — so what it compares is "spacer or no spacer", not "implant or no implant": placement succeeded in 99%, the separation between prostate and rectum was 12.6±3.9 mm against 1.6±2.0 mm, and rectal V70 fell from 12.4% to 3.3% (p less than 0.0001)[7]. The final results at 3 years: grade 2 or worse rectal toxicity 5.7% (no spacer) against 0% (spacer, p=0.012), while grade 2 or worse urinary toxicity was 7% in both arms, with no difference[8].

Put it all together and the picture changes. A 2026 meta-analysis of 35 studies and 4,664 patients calculated a 56.8% reduction in rectal V70, but no significant difference in acute or late gastrointestinal toxicity at grade 2 or worse, and the same for urinary toxicity; the authors' conclusion reads that the effect on severe toxicity is limited and that more research is needed[9]. A second meta-analysis from 2025, of 17 studies and 3,200 patients including 3 randomised trials, points in a similar direction but with more detail: late grade 2 or worse rectal toxicity 1.62% against 9.35% (relative risk 0.25, 0.15 to 0.42) and early grade 2 or worse 3.07% against 6.05%, but no difference in grade 3 or worse gastrointestinal events, whether acute or late, and no statistical difference in bowel quality of life either[10]. This is the sentence this article cannot leave out: in the severe-toxicity cell, both meta-analyses found no difference.

Post-marketing reports: three analyses, three tones of voice

The pivotal trial had no device-related adverse events[7]; post-marketing databases are another matter, and the same body of data has been read by three groups of people in three different tones. The analysis that used the manufacturer's sales volume as its denominator (2015 to 2023, 1,005 reports against 251,836 units sold) calculated an overall reporting rate of 0.40%, with 5 deaths making up 0.002% of units sold, infection 0.052%, fistula 0.037% and bleeding 0.033%, and concluded that most had no clinical consequence[11]. A separate academic analysis (981 reports, 990 events) counted 91 abscesses and 91 fistulas, 5 deaths and 13.1% at grade 3 or worse, and pointed out that 22.4% of related adverse events occurred before radiotherapy began — that is, caused by the device itself; the authors' conclusion states that many of these events were more severe than those reported in clinical trials[12]. The third of them (574 cases) gave a grade distribution of 1.6% at grade 4 and 15.9% at grade 3, with 9% of those undergoing faecal diversion, and listed death, gel embolism, rectal ulceration and infection requiring diversion in so many words[13]; anaphylactic shock, and acute prostatitis with septic shock after placement, each have a case report as well[14][15].

How to read that set of numbers: this is a voluntary reporting database, the denominator is units sold rather than implants performed, and the numerator depends on people's willingness to report — the reporting rate is under 1%, but of the cases that were reported, 13% are grade 3 or worse. There are data guarding against over-alarm as well: an imaging sub-analysis of the pivotal trial showed that 24% of men had gel infiltrating the rectal wall visible on MRI (full thickness in 4.0%), but the depth of infiltration had no correlation with rectal toxicity at the time of the procedure, acutely, or late (p=0.64, 0.64, 0.85)[16].

Who does not need it, or should not have it

Start with what genuinely falls into the "does not need it" cell. Anyone whose anatomy will not allow placement is unsuitable, and that is a judgement for the doctor doing the procedure. Anyone thinking of it as a general preventive measure does not need it either: the British Society of Gastroenterology's 2025 guidance lists rectal spacers, balloons and rectal emptying together and says of them that they "have not improved GI outcomes"[17] — and this needs labelling clearly: that sentence appears in the context of preventing acute toxicity, and cannot be extrapolated to late rectal toxicity.

A secondary analysis of the pivotal trial went looking for who benefits particularly, and across subgroups by age, body mass index, previous surgery, target volume and plan quality, the relative benefit of the spacer in reducing the risk of a decline in bowel quality of life showed no statistical heterogeneity; the benefit was still there even in the more than 95% of plans that had met their rectal dose constraints, and prostate volume had no correlation with rectal V70[18]. So both intuitions — "men with a big prostate need it more" and "a well-made plan means you can do without it" — are ruled out; you cannot pick yourself out using those criteria. But that statement holds only within its own endpoint: what this sub-analysis measured was the risk of a decline in bowel quality of life, which is the mild cell. It did not answer the severe-toxicity cell at all, and the two meta-analyses that did answer it found no difference. What it proves is that nobody can be identified as benefiting particularly — not that everybody should have one.

The one group that can be named as "not suitable" comes from failure. Eight consecutive patients who had previously had pelvic radiotherapy and had a spacer placed for re-irradiation had complications running from pelvic pain to rectal perforation, abscess and fistula, with 2 of the 8 suffering a serious complication, and the authors state plainly that use in this group calls for caution[19]. The answer to "who does not need it" also has to be read off the edge of the evidence: its randomised evidence exists only in prostate cancer, and not in the other four; and if what worries you most is severe late rectal side effects, the current meta-analyses cannot give you that promise. One more plain institutional fact: the rectal spacer is not something my department provides, and I write it as an option you can go and ask about, not as a procedure I am recommending.

To ask about these two things, get the question right first

Cost and status first. Searching the full table of the NHI fee schedule column by column, the search terms 金標 (gold marker), fiducial, 間隔物 (spacer), 水膠 (hydrogel) and spacer each return zero entries; the table does contain generic ultrasound-guidance items, but no public document states whether fiducial placement is claimed under one of them[20]. As for the spacer's approval status and its charging status in Taiwan, I could find no official announcement I could cite. Take that cell straight to the department concerned and to the medical affairs office, and do not work the price out from figures circulating online. There is only one question to carry into clinic: given where my tumour is and the technique I am being treated with, what would each of these two things change? Following that sentence, the doctor will need to know how many fractions your course is, how much dose each one carries and whether the rectal constraints have been met, and will also ask whether you have had pelvic radiotherapy before and what operations you have had that might affect placement. Which department a spacer belongs to, who performs it and how the charge is worked out is a separate thread; take it to the medical affairs office.

From the physician
