FLASH: radiotherapy in under a second, still in phase 1

The animal evidence is real, yet in humans there are only about thirty people's worth of phase 1 and feasibility data, with no efficacy comparison of any kind. It is this topic's first lesson in reading the news.

#FLASH#Evidence#Clinical Trials

This article has two key numbers: the 'one second' in the headlines, and the 'thirty people' the papers add up to. On FLASH, news volume and evidence move in opposite directions — that is nobody's fault, but it happens to train one skill: reading a radiotherapy news item back down to its original size.

Warning 1 (what this displaces): Most of the treatments in this topic are paid out of pocket, and most sit outside standard treatment. Before you decide, you need to know that what they displace is usually not time but three things of real value: standard treatment you have not yet used up, money you will need for later lines, and your eligibility for clinical trials (many trials exclude people who have recently received other experimental treatments). And there is one more, harder to see — physical reserve. The strength burnt through in a course that does not work may no longer be there when a genuinely useful option appears. So the question is not whether this is worth trying — it is what you give up by doing it.

FLASH differs from the other three technologies in this topic in one more way: in Taiwan, not even a way to pay for it exists. So what it displaces today is not money — it is your attention and your hope, and both of those, like physical reserve, are finite.

Warning 2 (finding the right doctor): Do not make this kind of decision alone, and do not make it on the word of the person offering the treatment. The doctor you are looking for is one willing to tell you "this is not right for you" — if they never once say who this is NOT for, that is not a consultation, it is a sale.

My position first: proton therapy and hyperthermia are both technologies within my own field, and the hospital I work at has the equipment for them. So I draw every line in this topic tighter, not looser.

It changes only one thing: how fast the dose goes in

Protons change where the dose stops; carbon ions change what happens once it arrives; FLASH changes time. Conventional radiotherapy delivers at roughly 0.03 gray (Gy) per second; FLASH's operational definition is 40 Gy per second or more, a difference of over a thousand-fold — dose rate means "how much radiation dose is delivered each second", so FLASH gives the entire dose within a beam-on time of under one second[1]. One thing needs saying clearly at the start: FLASH is not the name of a machine. It is a dose-rate condition, and different devices reach it in different ways.

The animal evidence is real, and it looks good. The founding study, published in Science Translational Medicine in 2014, irradiated mouse thoraxes at conventional dose rate and at FLASH: 15 Gy at the conventional rate produced lung fibrosis, while FLASH at up to 20 Gy, with follow-up beyond 36 weeks, produced no lung complications; in the same series of experiments, FLASH suppressed tumours just as well as conventional irradiation — normal tissue protected, tumour control undiminished. That is the "FLASH effect"[1]. What it represents is a reproducible animal phenomenon that stands up scientifically; it does not represent a human conclusion — as for why the effect exists, a 2025 review of the mechanism states outright that the mechanism and its influencing factors remain unclear, and oxygen depletion is only the most famous of the hypotheses[2][3].

All the human evidence, added together: about thirty people

The first person ever treated with FLASH was in Lausanne, in 2019: a patient with cutaneous lymphoma refractory to multiple treatments, a single 3.5 cm skin tumour, 15 Gy delivered in 90 milliseconds, with a mild skin reaction at three weeks[4]. Ninety milliseconds is startling — but this is a feasibility observation in one patient, not a trial.

FAST-01, the first human trial of proton FLASH, enrolled 10 adults with painful bone metastases in the limbs; the prescription was a single fraction of 8 Gy — identical to conventional palliative radiotherapy, the only difference being the dose rate. The questions it asked were whether the workflow was feasible, what the toxicity was, and whether the pain was relieved; adverse events were mild and comparable to conventional radiotherapy[5]. The same paper carries one number that deals with the fantasy of "radiotherapy done in a second": the beam really did take under one second, but the average time a patient spent on the treatment table was still 15.8 minutes per treatment site — positioning and image verification saved not a single minute[5]. The follow-on trial FAST-02 was published in 2026: 12 patients enrolled and 10 treated, thoracic bone metastases, the same single 8 Gy, with no related adverse events of grade 2 or higher[6] — safety data, still not an efficacy comparison.

On the electron side: IMPulse is a phase 1 dose-escalation trial in cutaneous metastases from melanoma (dose escalation means stepping the dose up one level at a time to find where the safe ceiling lies). Single doses climbed from 22 to 28 Gy; 11 patients were enrolled, 10 treated, 15 lesions in all, with no dose-limiting toxicity at any dose level; the original plan was to climb to 34 Gy, but accrual was so slow the trial was stopped early after 28 Gy[7] — it could not even fill a phase 1, and that sentence deserves to be pasted beside every "about to transform the clinic" headline. A Zurich team converted a conventional accelerator to produce an ultra-high-dose-rate electron beam; what they published is a technical feasibility report of the first patient[8]. Add it all up, and the published human experience to August 2026 is roughly 30 people; every one of these studies asked "can it be delivered, and is it safe" — the question "is FLASH better than conventional radiotherapy" has never been asked by any human trial, and no randomised comparison exists anywhere[4][5][6][7][8].

News volume and evidence volume run in opposite directions here

This is the one thing this article really wants to say: of the four technologies, FLASH has the most news coverage and the least human evidence. That is not fraud on anyone's part — the animal data are real, the physics is real — it is a choice about what gets reported: "a whole dose in one second" makes a headline; "a ten-patient feasibility trial" does not. The field itself knows perfectly well the evidence is not there yet: as early as 2019, a review asked in its own title whether FLASH is a silver bullet or fool's gold, and laid the limitations out one by one in the middle of the excitement[9]. So FLASH coverage is good material for practising how to read the news, and the method is three questions: how many people are in the denominator? Does the endpoint ask about safety or efficacy? Who is the comparison group? Use those three to shrink a news item back to its original size — the full use of that ruler is in "New does not mean better".

Between the mouse and the clinic, these are the gates

The first gate is mechanism: without knowing where the effect comes from, it is hard to know which conditions will make it disappear[2]. The second is dosimetry: at tens of Gy per second, existing detectors saturate, so measurement and verification are themselves a hard problem[3]. The third is fractionation: spreading conventional radiotherapy over several weeks has its biological reasons; on whether and how FLASH should be fractionated, a 2025 Delphi consensus of 21 international experts voted through two rounds and reached no consensus on any of the three questions — multiple beams, multiple fractions, single-fraction dose[10]. The fourth is depth: to push the dose rate ultra-high, FAST-01 and FAST-02 used transmission proton beams — protons passing straight through the body instead of stopping in the tumour — which means giving up, from the outset, protons' original advantage of "the dose stops in the tumour" (what that advantage is about is in "Protons: precise. Then what?"); so all human FLASH to date is confined to superficial or palliative settings, and "the precision of protons" and "the speed of FLASH" cannot yet be had at the same time[3][5].

Taiwan's current status is a verified zero

I searched the international clinical trial registries (29 August 2026): worldwide, registered trials with FLASH radiotherapy as their subject number 6; located in Taiwan, 0. Nor could I find any official announcement by any institution in Taiwan of a FLASH clinical programme[11]. So FLASH's status in Taiwan sits below even "trials only" — there is not even a trial, which puts approval and coverage further out of reach still; why those three things have to be read separately is in "Approved, covered, effective: three different things".

That settles what its displacement effect looks like. You cannot spend money on FLASH, but you can spend two things on it that are just as finite: attention, and hope. The reading I least want to see is "faster radiotherapy is nearly here — better put the current course on hold for now". No current evidence supports delaying, shortening or interrupting any treatment in order to wait for FLASH; the harm of delay is certain, while at the other end, even how the next round of trials should be designed is still being settled slowly, by expert consensus[10].

You have seen the news. Here is what to do today

To be blunt: there is no common situation today in which I would, in clinic, put FLASH on the table as a treatment option of my own accord. Only one reasonable way of reaching it remains — being in a country that has a trial, happening to meet enrolment criteria of the bone-metastasis or skin-metastasis kind, and joining the trial after assessment by your original treatment team[6][7]; for everyone else, it is a scientific development you may follow with interest, not a treatment you can ask for. So after seeing the news, what you should do is this: change nothing. The course proceeds as planned; the follow-up visits stay booked. If you really want to do something, take the news item to clinic and ask one question: "With my disease, which options actually stand up right now?" For the great majority of patients with common cancers, what decides the outcome is the stage, the subtype, and whether the full course of treatment was completed — not how fast the machine delivers the dose. One day, FLASH may change what radiotherapy looks like; today, it changes none of your decisions.
