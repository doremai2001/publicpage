HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

Five days or six weeks of your life

The trial data is settled elsewhere; this is the other half — what twenty-five half-days of commuting costs a household, and when six weeks are six necessary weeks.

Rectal Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 10 references
#Short Course#Decisions#Work & Travel#Side Effects#NHI Coverage

Distance, leave from work and who does the driving are real inputs into a radiotherapy schedule, and your team would rather hear them now than find out in week three.

"Doctor, I've heard some people only get five treatments. Why do I need twenty-five?" I am asked this most weeks in clinic. The person asking is usually not after a short cut — they live in Yunlin, their daughter has to take leave to drive them up, and when they add six weeks up, their face falls.

This article does not retell the trial details; the site's Insights section already carries a piece that takes the data apart line by line. This one is about the other half: what each schedule asks of your life, and what it gives back.

Two calendars, before anything else

Short course is five fractions of 5 Gy, finished inside a week, with no concurrent chemotherapy. Long course is 50.4 Gy in 28 fractions of 1.8 Gy, about five and a half weeks, with 5-FU or oral capecitabine running alongside it, and surgery four to six weeks after the last fraction[1][2].

The trials I will deal with in two sentences. A Polish randomised trial of 312 patients compared the two: local recurrence 9.0% (short) versus 14.2% (long), which did not reach statistical significance (p=0.170), and four-year overall survival 67.2% versus 66.2%[2]; the Trans-Tasman TROG 01.04 trial enrolled 326 people and found three-year local recurrence 7.5% versus 4.4%, a difference of 3.1% (95% CI −2.1 to 8.3, p=0.24), with five-year survival 74% versus 70%[3]. The TROG authors' own reading of that confidence interval is honest: it "is consistent with either no clinically important difference or differences in favor of LC"[3]. For the full interpretation, read the Insights piece "Five fractions or twenty-five?".

Six weeks of commuting, priced in your own household

On paper, the difference between five visits and twenty-five to twenty-eight is a number. In a life it is these:

  • The treatment itself usually takes a little over ten minutes, but between the journey, waiting, parking and set-up, most people write off half a day. Twenty-five fractions is twenty-five half-days.

  • Anyone travelling up to a medical centre from central or southern Taiwan or the east coast will almost certainly have to rent somewhere near the hospital for the long course, or leave before dawn and get home after dark, six weeks running. Short course is one week.

  • Whoever comes with you needs six weeks off work too. I have seen too many families where the daughter or daughter-in-law carries this, and her own job cannot carry her doing it.

  • The long course runs concurrently with chemotherapy. From the third or fourth week the diarrhoea, sore skin and fatigue start stacking up, and the commute gets much harder than it was in week one.

These are real costs, worth saying out loud and worth saying to your doctor. But one thing has to be said first: travel and time off can enter the discussion; they cannot override what the tumour itself indicates. If your tumour's position, or what the MRI shows, points to the long course, then those six weeks are six necessary weeks.

What the five days buys, and what it costs

The clearest thing it buys is far less acute toxicity. In the Polish trial, early radiation toxicity was 3.2% (short) versus 18.2% (long) (p<0.001)[2]. The gap is that wide because the long course is delivering chemotherapy at the same time.

But "short course is safer" cannot be stretched into the long term. In the same trial, severe late toxicity was 10.1% (short) versus 7.1% (long), p=0.360 — numerically slightly higher after short course[2]; in TROG 01.04, late grade 3–4 toxicity was 5.8% versus 8.2% (p=0.53), no real difference either way[3]. So the honest version is: short course makes these few weeks easier. It does not promise you an easier five years from now.

And what the five and a half weeks buys

Shrinkage. In the Polish trial, long-course chemoradiation shrank tumours by an average of 1.9 cm more than short course did (p<0.001)[4].

When does shrinkage genuinely matter? When the tumour is very low, when the mesorectal fascia is threatened on MRI, and when the team's goal is to preserve an organ. TROG 01.04 holds one signal worth noticing that did not reach significance: among tumours less than 5 cm from the anal verge, 6 of 48 patients in the short-course arm had a local recurrence, against 1 of 31 in the long-course arm (p=0.21)[3]. Far too few patients to settle anything — but it is why many centres still prefer the long course for very low tumours. ASTRO's 2025 guideline sits in the same place: it prefers neither fractionation, but where organ preservation is the priority it strongly recommends chemoradiation followed by consolidation chemotherapy[5].

"If it shrinks more, do I get to keep my sphincter?"

This is the easiest wrong step to take, and the Polish trial measured it directly. Sphincter preservation was 61% (short) versus 58% (long), p=0.57; the median distal bowel margin was identical in the two arms, 2 cm in both; and most tellingly, five patients in the chemoradiation arm reached a clinical complete response and had an abdominoperineal resection anyway[4].

Which is to say: what mainly decides the operation is the judgement the surgeon formed before treatment began, not how much the tumour shrank. The stoma question is covered properly in "Will I need a stoma, and when will anyone know"; only one sentence belongs here. Do not take the long course in exchange for the hope of keeping your sphincter.

What happens after the five fractions matters more than the five

Here is an option many people have never heard of. Stockholm III randomised 840 patients into three groups: short course with immediate surgery, short course with surgery delayed four to eight weeks, and long course with delayed surgery. Local recurrence events were 8/357, 10/355 and 7/128, and both delayed approaches were judged non-inferior[6].

More practical still are the complications: postoperative complications after short course with delayed surgery were 144/355, against 188/357 after short course with immediate surgery, odds ratio 0.61 (95% CI 0.45–0.83, p=0.001)[6]. What that means concretely for a Taiwanese patient: five days of radiotherapy does not have to be tied to an operation the following week — there can be a few weeks in between for recovery and for arrangements.

And the thing I most dread being misunderstood: "five fractions" does not mean "treatment finished". In current practice, five fractions of 5 Gy is often the front end of total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT), with eighteen to twenty-four weeks of chemotherapy behind it, rather than the whole course[7]. Anyone who hears "five days" and expects to be back at work a fortnight later will be caught out by what comes after. "TNT is a trade, not an upgrade" sets that out in full.

As soon as 5-FU, capecitabine or oxaliplatin appears in the plan, four things mean contacting the team the same day rather than waiting for the next appointment: a fever over 38°C, severe diarrhoea with dehydration (dizzy on standing, passing noticeably less urine), mouth ulcers bad enough to stop you swallowing, or numbness in the hands and feet that is worse than before. On capecitabine, watch your palms and soles: redness, peeling and tingling is hand-foot syndrome, to be reported rather than covered with a cream and endured. On oxaliplatin you will get cold-induced nerve symptoms — fingers or throat feeling shocked when you touch cold water or drink something cold; keep away from cold and say so at every visit. Never stop or reduce a dose yourself.

How the NHI sees the two schedules

Taiwan has a payment item written specifically for the short course: 36024B, preoperative hypofractionated radiotherapy for rectal cancer (直腸癌術前低分次放射治療), 204,966 points, effective 1 July 2023. Its text is explicit. The indications are rectal malignancy at clinical stage T3 or above or with positive pelvic nodes, and T1N0 or T2N0 tumours of the lower rectum (within 5 cm of the anal verge); it is a bundled payment, one course of five fractions, payable once in a lifetime; and it may not be used for patients who need salvage pelvic re-irradiation after a recurrence following surgery[8]. The long course is claimed separately — per fraction delivered, plus treatment planning and CT simulation[8].

The drugs are a separate question. In Section 9 of the drug reimbursement regulations, the colorectal indications for oxaliplatin and capecitabine cover only metastatic disease and adjuvant treatment after surgery for stage III colon cancer — there is no reimbursed indication for preoperative treatment of rectal cancer[9][10]. Long-course chemoradiation in Taiwan mostly uses infusional 5-FU; if your doctor mentions an oral drug, ask your case manager (個管師) which part is covered and which is self-pay.

Put your household's calendar on the table too

First: "given my tumour's position and my MRI, is short course a safe option for me?" If the answer is no, follow it with: "is that because the tumour is too low, or because the fascia is threatened?"

Second: "is the goal of this radiotherapy local control, shrinking the tumour so it can be operated on, or preserving an organ?" Different goals, different schedules.

Third: "if I have the five fractions, is surgery straight afterwards, or after a few weeks' wait, or after chemotherapy first?"

Fourth, say your travel and caregiving situation out loud: where you live, who drives you, who has to take the leave. That is not complaining. It is information the team genuinely uses when it builds the schedule.

From the physician

Whether short course is safe for you depends on the tumour's height and what the MRI shows, not on how far you live from the hospital. Say your travel and caregiving situation out loud anyway — it is scheduling information and the team uses it. Then ask what happens after the five fractions, because that is usually the longer half.
