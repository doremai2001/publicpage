DURING TREATMENT

Can't swallow, weight falling: a feeding tube first?

A 10% weight loss is the threshold that starts the assessment, not an order to place a tube. Nasogastric tube, gastrostomy and jejunostomy each have their own place in the guidelines; this article lays them out on the medical side and the day-to-day side.

Oesophageal Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 16 references
#Feeding Tubes#Nutrition#Swallowing#Daily Life

This article will not tell you which tube is better, because the guidelines do not rank them that way. It will tell you what question each tube is answering: how long you will need it, whether surgery is still ahead, who will look after it, when it comes out — and why a metal stent is not the answer before chemoradiotherapy.

"I can still get congee down — why are we talking about a tube already?" I get this question every week. I usually ask one back: how many kilos have you lost in three months? Many people lose the weight while they can still "swallow" — slowly, less, painfully — and without noticing they are eating a third less. How fast the weight is falling is the first thing I look at to judge how much longer this road can be walked.

The conclusion first: a weight loss of more than 10% is the threshold that starts the assessment, not an order to place a tube. A tube is one of the options that comes out of that assessment; the three kinds of tube each have their own place in the guidelines, not one box in which they compete to be best. I will take them one at a time.

Where the "10%" figure actually comes from

Among 922 patients in Rotterdam who underwent oesophagectomy (squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma reported together), 17% had lost more than 10% of their body weight in the three months before diagnosis. Their complications and hospital stay were no worse than anyone else's, but their five-year risk of death was 34% higher (hazard ratio 1.34, 95% confidence interval 1.02 to 1.74). A hazard ratio divides one group's event rate by the other's; read it as "on the whole, they died sooner", not as "each person is 34% more likely to die"[1]. The way I read it: the person who has lost 10% is not necessarily riskier to operate on, but the outlook is worse, and what has to be dealt with is "why is the weight falling", not putting the number back.

Weight lost during treatment counts too. Japan's OGSG1003 was a randomised phase II trial of preoperative chemotherapy for locally advanced squamous cell carcinoma, 134 patients; losing more than 5% of body weight during chemotherapy was the only independent factor for post-operative infectious complications, odds ratio 2.69 (95% confidence interval 1.12 to 6.46). An odds ratio is not a multiple of probability; it can only be read as "clearly higher"[2]. The ESPEN cancer nutrition guideline asks for regular assessment of intake, weight change and BMI from the day of diagnosis, and during thoracic radiotherapy it asks that "adequate intake be ensured"[3]. Note the wording: assess, ensure intake — neither of those says "place a tube".

Does placing a tube first give a better result? The data point both ways

There is no randomised trial on this; the retrospective data run both ways. At the University of Virginia, of 127 patients who received preoperative treatment, 80% had dysphagia from the start and 48% had a tube placed; among those who did, 27.9% did not use it or did not use it regularly, and surgical outcomes were the same as in those without a tube[4]. In a Florida cohort of 99, placing the tube before chemoradiotherapy against placing it only at surgery gave six-month weight change of −11.2% against −15.4% and complications of 64% against 51%, neither statistically different; the authors suggested reserving a pre-treatment tube for those with clear obstruction at endoscopy[5].

The Taiwanese data ask the question more precisely. A secondary analysis of a prospective cohort at National Cheng Kung University, 260 patients with advanced squamous cell carcinoma receiving concurrent chemoradiotherapy: 60.7% developed cachexia after treatment, and 23.8% had a gastrostomy or jejunostomy placed before treatment. Across the whole cohort, having a tube placed first was unrelated to survival; but in the group whose tumour occupied 70% or more of the lumen, a pre-treatment tube was associated with less cachexia, a better response (odds ratio 2.78) and a longer median survival (11 against 9 months), while below 70% no difference could be seen[6]. A single-centre post-hoc analysis of a prospective cohort, not the last word, but the real question has been put into words: not "whether to place one" but "who should have one placed first".

Three tubes, which the guidelines put at different points in time

The European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) 2021 guideline cuts it this way: if enteral nutrition is expected for less than four weeks, use a temporary nasal tube; beyond four weeks, consider a percutaneous tube; for percutaneous access the stomach is the first choice, and the jejunum is chosen only when the gastric anatomy has been altered, gastric emptying is poor, gastric feeding is not tolerated or the aspiration risk is high[7]. Where ESPEN speaks of severe radiation-induced mucositis or an obstructing thoracic tumour, what it names is "a nasogastric tube or a percutaneous tube (for example a PEG)"[3]. The jejunostomy appears in the ESPEN surgical guideline: in patients undergoing major upper gastrointestinal surgery who need enteral nutrition, a nasojejunal tube or a jejunostomy placed during the operation should be considered[8]. No guideline says the three are equivalent, and no guideline recommends a jejunostomy over a gastrostomy before oesophageal cancer treatment. Below, each is split into the medical side and the day-to-day side; the day-to-day side is my description from clinic.

The nasogastric tube: quickest in, easiest out, and the one that most often comes out on its own

The medical side: no anaesthetic or operation, it can be placed in clinic, and it has no effect on later using the stomach to rebuild the oesophagus. The comparative data ESPEN cites: compared with a PEG, patients with a nasogastric tube had less dysphagia and came off the tube sooner after radiotherapy ended, with similar weight maintenance; the price is a higher risk of displacement[3]. ESGE adds a separate reminder: a nasogastric tube entering the airway is a "serious but avoidable" adverse event, and there must be a procedure to confirm its position after placement — auscultation alone is not enough[9].

The day-to-day side: the tube is taped to your face and visible when you go out; there is a foreign-body feeling in the throat; turning over, coughing or vomiting can all bring it out, and then it is back to hospital to have it replaced. Family members have to learn to check its position before each feed and flush it afterwards. The advantage is that it can come out at any time: once swallowing recovers it is pulled out in clinic and that is the end of it, no trace left. ESGE's slot for it is a short-term need expected to last under four weeks; chemoradiotherapy itself runs five to six weeks, and the oesophagitis drags on past the end, so anyone choosing it should decide in advance: if you cannot last until the day it comes out, when do you switch to a percutaneous tube?

Gastrostomy (PEG): that stomach has to become an oesophagus later — will it be damaged

On the medical side this is the question I am asked most, because after an oesophagectomy it is usually the stomach that is pulled up to make the join. A Taiwanese team's 2022 meta-analysis of four retrospective studies, 1,027 surgical patients: those who had previously had a PEG showed no statistical difference from those who had not in anastomotic leak, stricture, pulmonary complications, wound infection or hospital stay, and there was no PEG-related failure of the gastric conduit[10]. As for tumour seeding along the tube track to the abdominal wall, I will not say "never": a Pakistani series of 96 had one case where a biopsy at the PEG site was malignant[11]. To avoid dragging the tube through the tumour, ESGE states plainly that patients with oesophageal cancer or severe oesophageal stricture can have the direct percutaneous "push" technique instead[9]. The NHI fee schedule has an item for "percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy", but its note reads (my translation of the clause): patients in a persistent vegetative state after cerebrovascular accident or road traffic injury, and "other conditions that require the patient to use a nasogastric tube for years" — the clause neither names oesophageal cancer nor excludes it, I cannot rule on it from the wording, so ask the medical affairs office or your case manager[12].

The day-to-day side: the tube hides under your clothes, which is why many people still working choose it. It rarely displaces, and families feel safer feeding through it; ESPEN says quality of life "may" be better[3]. The price is one more opening in the abdominal wall that has to be cleaned every day; removal means a visit to gastroenterology, and the hole closes by itself within a few days. It suits those expected to need it for more than a month, or who still have surgery ahead, or whose swallowing is already clearly limited.

Feeding jejunostomy: "fewer infections" has to be said in the same breath as bowel obstruction

The medical side: it has to be placed laparoscopically or at open surgery under anaesthesia, so it is almost always the surgeon putting it in during the operation, and feeding can begin on the first post-operative day[8]. It bypasses the stomach completely and does not affect the gastric conduit. But the benefit of "routine" placement has not been shown: one meta-analysis of 18 studies found bowel obstruction in about 6% (95% confidence interval 3% to 12%), of whom more than 60% needed re-operation; tube-site infection about 7%, blockage or displacement about 7%[13]. Another meta-analysis of 12 studies, 2,173 patients: hospital stay 2 days longer, odds ratio for bowel obstruction 11.67, no difference in overall complications or pneumonia[14]. The sentence "a jejunostomy is safer" does not stand up in the literature — fewer infections is true, but bowel obstruction is its own particular complication, and the two have to be looked at together.

The day-to-day side: unlike the stomach, the jejunum has no reservoir, so feeds have to be slow, dripped through a pump over several hours, and the family has to learn the machine and its alarms; too fast and you bloat or get diarrhoea; the tube is thin and blocks easily. It comes out once you are eating enough after surgery; and if something goes wrong at the anastomosis, this tube is the lifeline for that period.

Why a stent is not the answer before chemoradiotherapy

Why not just put in a metal stent to hold the oesophagus open so you can eat? Recommendation 4 of the ESGE 2021 stenting guideline, in its own words: ESGE does not recommend self-expanding metal stents as a bridge to surgery or before preoperative chemoradiotherapy, because of the high rate of adverse events, and "other options such as feeding tubes are preferable" — a strong recommendation, low-quality evidence[15]. Behind it is a French multicentre surgical cohort: 38 patients who had had a stent first against 152 matched controls — 2 tumour perforations at placement, complete resection rate 71.0% against 85.5%, three-year survival 25% against 44%; after adjustment the stent itself remained an adverse prognostic factor[16]. Stents have their place in the palliative setting, which is written up in "After recurrence or spread, what roads remain". What has to be kept separate is this: the NHI item "oesophageal metal stent placement" lists its indication (my translation of the clause) as "malignant tumour of the oesophagus or cardia", with no exclusion of the preoperative setting[12] — being reimbursed and being recommended by the guideline are two different things.

A set of scales, your own swallowing grades, and how long each meal takes

First, weight: same scales, same time of day, once a week, and write down your weight from three months ago too — what the doctor wants is the rate of change. Second, swallowing: use your own grading — dry rice, congee, pudding, thin liquids — and note which grade you started to struggle with this week. Third, how long each meal takes; twenty minutes becoming an hour is a signal. You can also ask your case manager today: the NHI "general tube feeding" item for tube-feed formula lists its indications (my translation of the clause) as including "oesophageal stricture, oesophagectomy, oesophageal cancer"[12]; for the tube consumables themselves I could not find a clause I could cite — ask the medical affairs office. The last line is ESPEN's, and I say it to every patient who has a tube placed: during tube feeding, the dysphagia should go on being treated and swallowing function maintained[3]. The tube is there so you do not starve, not so you stop swallowing; every mouthful you can still swallow is helping you keep the day the tube comes out.

From the physician

If you are already losing weight, bring your weight from three months ago to the next visit, not just today's. A tube is an option, not a sentence, and you keep swallowing after it goes in; which one suits you depends on how much of the lumen the tumour is blocking, whether surgery is still ahead, and who at home can look after it — three things we go through together in clinic.
