NON-MUSCLE-INVASIVE

BCG is not chemotherapy, and not a vaccination

It is live attenuated mycobacteria instilled into the bladder; the first line of the label says it is not a vaccine for the prevention of cancer, and a fever is something that has to be read on two levels.

Bladder Cancer Guide · Non-muscle-invasive · 13 references
#Immunotherapy#Side Effects#Urgent#Evidence

This article turns "when do I go back the same day" into concrete thresholds with times attached, and it lays out both directions of the evidence on cutting back: lowering the dose and cutting the number of instillations are a long way apart in how well they are supported, and they are not the same thing.

"Next you will be having BCG" is a sentence I take apart in clinic nearly every time it is said. Most people react in one of two ways: some take it for chemotherapy, and some think of the injection given in the arm at birth that leaves a small scar. It is neither.

It is a live, attenuated bovine mycobacterium, run into the bladder through a catheter, and it works through the immune reaction it sets off in the bladder wall against whatever tumour cells are left behind. The mechanism, the side effects and the contraindications are all different, and there is one thing many people are never told: it can cause a systemic infection. Who gets it and how many instillations they get is decided by the risk stratification — see "Low, intermediate, high: what decides whether you get instillations". If the disease comes back anyway after a full course, that box is in "After BCG stops working".

The first line of the label says what it is not

The description section of the US TICE BCG label says exactly that: it is an attenuated, live culture preparation of the bacillus of Calmette and Guérin strain of Mycobacterium bovis. Each vial holds 1 to 8 × 10⁸ colony-forming units, roughly 50 mg wet weight, diluted in 50 mL of preservative-free saline before instillation[1].

The first sentence of the warnings section of the same label: "BCG LIVE (TICE BCG) is not a vaccine for the prevention of cancer. BCG Vaccine, not BCG LIVE (TICE BCG), should be used for the prevention of tuberculosis."[1] What a Taiwanese reader has in mind when they hear "BCG" is usually the tuberculosis vaccination given to infants; that is a different product for a different purpose from the one instilled into a bladder. The indications for the intravesical product are carcinoma in situ of the bladder, and prophylaxis of Ta or T1 papillary tumours after resection — and the label states in as many words that it is not for stages above T1.

Against instillation chemotherapy it is not stronger or weaker, it is a different mechanism

BCG gets described as "chemotherapy, only stronger", and that is a misunderstanding: it works through an immune reaction, and maintenance treatment is part of it. A meta-analysis of individual patient data from 2,820 patients in nine randomised trials found that in the trials that included maintenance, BCG reduced the risk of recurrence by 32% compared with mitomycin C; in the trials without maintenance it increased that risk by 28%[2]. At the progression end, a meta-analysis by the EORTC Genito-Urinary Cancers Group covering 4,863 patients in 24 randomised trials (20 of them with maintenance in some form), median follow-up 2.5 years: progression in 9.8% of the BCG arms against 13.8% of the controls, a 27% reduction in the odds[2]. An odds ratio is the odds of having it against not having it in each group, not a multiple of probability; the two figures you can read straight off are the absolute ones. The other direction has to sit beside it: another individual-patient-data meta-analysis and a Cochrane review both failed to confirm any statistical difference between mitomycin C and BCG in progression, survival or cause of death, and BCG has clearly more side effects than chemotherapy[2].

The shape of the course: six weeks is only the beginning

Induction is six weeks, once a week. The full schedule the guideline describes runs on from there into a maintenance phase: three weekly instillations again at 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30 and 36 months, up to 27 in all[2].

Whether maintenance is worth having rests mainly on SWOG 8507: 660 patients had the six-week induction, 550 were randomised at three months to maintenance or no maintenance, and the main analysis population was the 384 who were disease-free at randomisation. Median recurrence-free survival was 35.7 months without maintenance against 76.8 months with it, p less than 0.0001; median worsening-free survival was 111.5 months against not reached; five-year overall survival was 78% against 83%[3]. The authors note themselves that anyone with bad side effects simply had maintenance stopped, which is why the trial does not show severe toxicity.

How long to carry on for was answered directly by another randomised trial. EORTC 30962 randomised intermediate- and high-risk patients in a 2×2 design into four arms (one-third dose or full dose, each with one year or three years of maintenance), 1,355 patients in the intention-to-treat analysis, median follow-up 7.1 years. High-risk patients gained fewer recurrences from the extra two years (hazard ratio 1.61, 95% confidence interval 1.13 to 2.30, p=0.009; a confidence interval means that if you ran the same study many times over the answer would land in that range), but only when full dose was used; intermediate-risk patients on full dose gained nothing from the extra two years; and progression and survival did not differ between the four arms[4]. The guideline files this at evidence level 1a[2]. What the extra two years buy is fewer recurrences, not fewer deaths.

The days after an instillation, and how many people get to the end

The label is specific: symptoms of bladder irritability are reported in approximately 60% of patients, typically beginning 4 to 6 hours after instillation and lasting 24 to 72 hours, and usually becoming apparent after the third instillation[1]. Each one being harder than the last is the normal behaviour of this drug, not you getting weaker.

The adverse event table in the label covers 674 patients (including 153 with carcinoma in situ); the figure in brackets is the proportion at grade 3 or above: painful urination 60% (11%), frequency 40% (7%), flu-like syndrome 33% (9%), fever 20% (8%)[1]. As for how many people get to the end: the guideline writes that more than half of patients will fail to complete a one-year maintenance course, and in EORTC 30962 only 34% of the three-year maintenance arm completed the whole schedule[2].

Three things that get mistaken for contraindications and are not: white cells in the urine, blood in the urine you cannot see, and bacteria in the urine without symptoms — none of them calls for prophylactic antibiotics; and a positive tuberculin skin test should not exclude anyone either[2]. What genuinely has to be postponed is a different set: the label says instillation with an actively bleeding mucosa may promote systemic BCG infection, and "Treatment should be postponed for at least 1 week following transurethral resection, biopsy, traumatic catheterization, or gross hematuria"; the contraindications section separately lists immunosuppressed states and active tuberculosis[1]. The guideline's own advice is to start no sooner than two weeks after the resection[2].

What sends you back the same day instead of waiting for the next appointment

The label leaves no cushion in its wording: "Deaths have been reported as a result of systemic BCG infection and sepsis."[1] So "a fever is a normal reaction" cannot stand as a whole sentence; it has to be split in two. The label says that malaise, fever and chills lasting one to two days often reflect hypersensitivity reactions; but the following, the label says, suggest systemic BCG infection and may require antituberculous therapy —

  • Febrile episodes with flu-like symptoms lasting more than 72 hours[1].

  • A fever reaching 103°F (about 39.4°C on conversion), or a temperature of 38.5°C (101.3°F) or above lasting longer than two to three days[1].

  • Systemic symptoms increasing in intensity with each repeated instillation, or persistent abnormalities of liver function tests[1].

  • Acute local inflammation — prostatitis, epididymitis, orchitis — lasting more than two to three days[1].

The label goes on: if a patient develops persistent fever, or an acute febrile illness consistent with BCG infection, BCG treatment should be discontinued and the patient immediately evaluated and treated for systemic infection[1]. The corresponding row in the guideline is harder still: a persistent high fever above 38.5 °C for more than 48 hours means permanently stopping BCG instillations, immediate urine culture, blood tests and chest X-ray, and an infectious diseases consultation[2]. Those are instructions for the medical side. What you have to do is one thing: go back the same day, and say clearly which day your last instillation was.

The probabilities belong in the same paragraph: the guideline says serious side effects occur in fewer than 5% of patients and can be treated effectively in almost all cases; the incidence of systemic BCG infection is 1% to 2%; and in a Swedish national database of 5,033 exposed people the five-year cumulative rate of a notified tuberculosis diagnosis was 1.1%[2].

The shortage: one list is the only current official status I can find

The shortages page of the US Food and Drug Administration's Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research lists TICE BCG as an ongoing shortage: onset January 2019, status currently available but going under allocation when demand exceeds production plans and available inventory, with increased demand given as the reason; the page was last updated on 10 April 2026[5]. That is an allocation status that has been hanging there from 2019 to 2026, not a sudden absence of the drug.

The shortage documents from the two big associations are old enough that the year has to be stated. Section 7.4.3.f of the guideline points only to an online appendix, whose embedded title is a statement dated 13 August 2012 about the Connaught strain going out of production that year. It advises that during a shortage, patients who have completed one year of BCG can safely stop instillations, and it puts down one sentence that still holds today: for people who are unsuitable for or unwilling to have their bladder removed, "there is no scientifically proven alternative to BCG treatment"[6]. The American Urological Association's shortage page was last updated on 4 September 2020, and what it contains is an order of priority: low risk should not be given BCG, high risk should be given full dose first, and where supply cannot be had the dose may be cut to a half or a third if feasible[7].

The Taiwan box: I could not find any official or professional-society announcement I can cite. The Taiwan FDA's drug supply information platform would not connect, the Centers for Disease Control page is about the infant tuberculosis vaccine, and I could find no statement from a local society either. Not being able to find one does not mean Taiwan has no problem, and it does not mean Taiwan is worse off — it means I could not find one.

The evidence on cutting back runs in both directions

Start with the side that supports it. EORTC 30962 reports that there were no significant differences in toxicity between one-third dose and full dose[4]. Real-world data on 563 patients at one cancer centre who received adequate BCG (150 at one-third dose, 413 at full dose) showed the reduced dose was no worse on time to recurrence, time to progression or cancer-specific survival (p values 0.449, 0.716 and 0.320)[8].

The other side has to be written out too. In that same EORTC 30962, neither reduction — one-third dose, and one year instead of three — could be shown to be non-inferior, and one-third dose for one year was worse than full dose for three years (hazard ratio 0.75, 0.59 to 0.94, p=0.01)[4]. Non-inferiority not being proven means "we still cannot say it is not much worse", not "it has been shown to be worse". A Spanish medical centre compared 64 patients during the shortage (median 8 instillations) with 94 patients from 2017 (median 12): median time to recurrence 285 days against 382 days (p=0.025), multivariable disease-free survival hazard ratio 1.87 (1.04 to 3.37, p=0.036)[9].

The hardest piece is NIMBUS, a randomised phase III trial: high-grade non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer, 51 centres, 345 patients, 15 instillations in the standard arm against 9 in the reduced arm. At a median follow-up of 12 months there had been 46 recurrences in the reduced arm and 21 in the standard arm, which met the pre-specified inferiority stopping rule, and accrual was halted immediately[10]. The quality-of-life analysis from the same trial (359 patients) found that cutting the number of instillations did not make quality of life any better[11]. Read the two sides together: the evidence for lowering the dose is a good deal better than the evidence for cutting the number of instillations, and the two must not be run together as one idea.

Three things to settle before the next instillation

First, what shape is my course — how many induction instillations, is there maintenance, out to which month? Second, if the schedule gets adjusted, is that being done because of my side effects, my risk group, or the supply of the drug?

Third is money. I have been through both sets of Taiwanese rules: in a full-text search of section 8 (immunological agents) and section 9 (antineoplastic agents) of the National Health Insurance drug reimbursement regulations, "卡介苗", "BCG" and "Calmette" all return zero hits[12][13]. Zero hits means I could not find a listed item — it does not mean "not covered" and it does not mean "covered", and I am not going to infer either. Ask the medical affairs office or your case manager, and ask for a written statement of the costs.

A last line for anyone counting up how many instillations they have had: the length of a course is not a score. It is what comes out of the risk group, what you can tolerate and what the supply allows, all discussed together — and the person to have that discussion with is the one in clinic who can see your reports.

From the physician

For the first three days after an instillation, take your temperature and write it down — not to make yourself anxious, but so that at the next appointment you can say how high it went and how long it lasted. If the fever has not settled after two or three days, or the symptoms are heavier each time, go back the same day, and tell them which day your last instillation was.
