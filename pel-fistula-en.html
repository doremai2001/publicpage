Fistula: risk, warning signs, order of care

Under the most modern image-guided technique there is, five-year grade 3 or worse fistula still runs at 3.2%; the risk factors come one by one with their numbers, and the day the symptoms appear is the day to be seen, not the day to wait for clinic.

Pelvic Radiotherapy Guide · During Treatment and After · 11 references
#Fistula#Late Effects#Urgent#Evidence

That 3.2% blocks two readings at once: "a newer technique avoids it" and "there is nothing to worry about". This article counts the hole made by the tumour separately from the hole made by the treatment, and puts the most important section where it will not be skipped: which situations mean being seen the same day.

Most patients meet the word "fistula" on the internet before they meet it anywhere else, and they come into clinic carrying one question: if I switch to a newer machine, will it not happen? I will put the answer at the front — no. This article handles three things: what the actual probability is, whose risk is higher, and which situations mean being seen that same day. The first two are there so that you do not have to frighten yourself. The third is the part of this article that genuinely had to be written.

My position first: pelvic radiotherapy is what I do every day, so every good word about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it. Implants such as the rectal spacer are not something my department provides; I write about them as an option you can go and ask about, and for cost and for who performs them, please ask that department and the medical affairs office.

Under the most modern technique there is, five years still gives 3.2%

The labels have to be remembered along with the number. EMBRACE-I is a prospective multicentre cohort of 1,251 patients with cervical cancer, treated with chemoradiotherapy plus MRI-guided brachytherapy — the most modern set of techniques on the table. Its five-year rate of grade 3 or worse fistula is 3.2% (95% confidence interval 2.2 to 4.5, the band the true figure most plausibly sits in), and the same cohort also recorded 13 treatment-related deaths[1]. "Switching to a newer technique will let you avoid a fistula" is a sentence I stop outright in clinic: that 3.2% is what was measured under the most modern technique there is. The most that can be written on technique's behalf is the BSG's line, copied out as it stands — gastrointestinal fistulae are relatively uncommon, and the incidence may be falling because of changes in the way treatment is delivered[2]. And the same goes in the other direction: 3.2% is not zero, it is higher in people whose organs are invaded, and I am not going to tell you there is nothing to worry about either.

A hole made by the tumour, and a hole made by the treatment

There are three roads in: recurrence, tumour and treatment. The tumour road has a hard contrast to offer. A 43-patient study (cervical cancer with bladder invasion, recruited across the earlier period of 1999 to 2015) reported vesicovaginal fistula in 12.8%, with the only significant factor on multivariable analysis being the height of bladder wall invasion, which reached 75% at 41 mm or more[3]. At the other end, EMBRACE-I after excluding patients with bladder invasion gives a crude incidence of grade 2 or worse bladder fistula of 0.7%[4]. The two are calculated differently and have to be read together: 3.2% is a five-year actuarial incidence for the whole cohort, and 0.7% is a crude incidence counting only the bladder variety after invasion was excluded. Stage points the same way: FIGO stage IVA carries an odds ratio of 6.87 — an odds ratio compares one group's odds of the event against the other's, so read it as direction rather than as a multiple — and the direction there is unambiguous[5].

The treatment road, item by item, each with its number

Smoking: in a 150-patient single-centre study, current smoking carried a multivariable odds ratio of 5.14 and was the only significant multivariable factor in that study; the overall fistula rate was 9%, at a median follow-up of 20 months[6]. Anti-angiogenic drugs: a meta-analysis of 597 cervical cancer patients who had received pelvic radiotherapy calculated an odds ratio of 4.03 for gastrointestinal fistula or perforation and 4.71 for the genitourinary tract among those given bevacizumab[7]; Japanese post-marketing surveillance (142 patients, of whom 64.1% had had radiotherapy) adds a crude incidence: 6 patients developed 7 pelvic fistulae, giving 4.2% (95% confidence interval 1.56 to 8.96)[8]. Instrumenting tissue that has been irradiated: in a case series of urinary fistulae after radiotherapy for prostate cancer, 81% of those who developed a rectal fistula had a history of rectal or urethral stricture, rectal biopsy, argon beam therapy, or transurethral resection after radiotherapy[9] — no denominator, but the association is enough to change a decision.

Previous pelvic surgery: in the same Japanese post-marketing surveillance, all 6 patients with a fistula had a history of pelvic irradiation, and 5 of them also had a history of pelvic surgery, with a further 2 having had salvage re-irradiation for pelvic recurrence[8]; the systematic review of rectourethral fistula likewise writes that irradiated patients, compared with those who have not been irradiated, have "higher complication rates and poorer healing", frequently requiring additional surgery or permanent urinary diversion[10]. The denominators are small in both, so this is a direction rather than a probability — but it is enough to explain why, in the box marked "operated on and then irradiated", any subsequent procedure has to be approached more carefully. Re-irradiation is named as a direction only in the case series above, and so is diabetes; neither has a citable effect size.

And one box I want to label honestly as empty: for the percentage incidence of rectourethral fistula after radiotherapy for prostate cancer, I could find no citable denominator. What is findable is surgical series or case series with no denominator, and the one review that does mention a prevalence has the range mangled in the typesetting of its abstract, so I am not citing it. All I can write in that box is "infrequent, but with serious consequences". I am not giving you a number.

What a fistula looks like — these situations do not wait for your next appointment

A fistula is a channel opening up between two organs that were never meant to communicate, so the symptoms are things turning up where they should not be. If any of the following appears at any point after radiotherapy ends, contact the team or get seen the same day, do not wait for your next appointment: stool, watery stool or faecally malodorous discharge coming from the vagina; a continuous leak of clear fluid from the vagina, heavy enough to need a pad; passing gas while passing urine, or visible faecal debris in the urine; a continuous leak of urine-like fluid from the anus or perineum. Any one of those together with fever, chills, or severe lower abdominal or pelvic pain is an emergency — the first thing to be dealt with in a fistula is not the hole, it is infection: the BSG puts "aggressive management of sepsis" first among all fistula measures, ahead of any investigation or surgical planning[2].

Rule out recurrence first; only then ask whether the radiotherapy did it

The BSG sentence belongs pinned to the notes: once a fistula is suspected clinically, it is essential to rule out disease recurrence before assuming that it is secondary to radiation injury[2]. So the imaging and endoscopy you get booked for over those few days are not a delay; they are the order this thing is supposed to be done in.

What comes after that I am also copying out plainly, without softening it. In that prostate cancer case series, of the 20 patients with post-radiotherapy urinary fistula who met the inclusion criteria, 80% had rectal-to-urinary fistulae with a mean diameter of 3.2 cm, and every patient with a rectourethral fistula who achieved symptom relief required both urinary and faecal diversion[9]. Timing changes the results too: a 72-patient multicentre retrospective study split fistulae into early and late using 31 days after surgery as the boundary and found final complete healing rates of 83.8% for early and 42.9% for late (p less than 0.005), with about 30% of patients ending up needing a permanent colostomy, that being 48.5% in the late group against 13.5% in the early group (p=0.001)[11]. These are retrospective surgical series with small denominators, and the population is weighted towards people who have already reached the point of surgery — but the direction is enough to establish one thing: this is not a complication to leave for a while and see whether it settles on its own.

The one factor you can move today

Lay that whole row of risk factors out and only one and a half of them belong to you. Smoking is the one: it is the only significant factor in the multivariable analysis of fistula[6], and the BSG lists smoking and a low body mass index as the two things that "where possible, should be addressed before radiotherapy"[2]; the half is nutrition and weight. The rest of them — stage, whether the tumour has invaded the bladder, whether bevacizumab is used, whether re-irradiation is given — are not things you decide on your own; those are conversations to have together in clinic. For how the late bleeding line runs, what the options are and what level of evidence each carries, this topic has a separate article, "Late bleeding: treatments and their evidence". There is one action to take away: get the referral to a smoking cessation clinic written today.

From the physician

The first danger in a fistula is not the hole, it is sepsis — any one of the symptoms together with fever, chills or severe pelvic pain is an emergency. When a fistula is suspected, recurrence gets ruled out first, and that is not a delay. Smoking is the only significant factor in the multivariable analysis, and it is the one you can move today.
