HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

The real numbers behind watch and wait

About one in four regrow within two years, three years of three-monthly endoscopy and MRI, and why none of it is permission to decline an operation.

Rectal Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 11 references
#Organ Preservation#Recurrence#Follow-up#Decisions

The people who keep their rectum are the ones who never miss an appointment. Read this for the numbers, not for permission.

"Doctor, I've seen people whose tumour disappeared after chemoradiation and who never had surgery. Could that be me?" I have heard this many times, and it is almost always missing half of itself. Those people did not avoid surgery. They postponed it, and what they gave in exchange was three years in which they can barely miss a single follow-up appointment.

Start by getting the name right. Watch and wait means "not operating for now, and staying ready to put the operation back at any moment". It is not standard treatment, and it does not suit everybody. This article puts the real numbers down, including the ones that do not look good — on this question, telling only the flattering half does harm.

The most important number first: one in four

The International Watch & Wait Database gathered 1,009 patients from 47 institutions in 15 countries; 880 of them (87%) reached a clinical complete response and entered the analysis, at a median follow-up of 3.3 years.

  • The two-year cumulative incidence of local regrowth was 25.2% (95% CI 22.2–28.5). Roughly one person in every four.

  • 88% of local regrowth appeared in the first two years.

  • 97% of regrowth grew in the bowel wall — which is why endoscopic surveillance is the load-bearing wall of the whole arrangement.

  • Distant metastases in 71/880 (8%); five-year overall survival 85% (80.9–87.7); five-year disease-specific survival 94% (91–96)[1].

The authors' own words: the distribution of regrowth "emphasises the importance of endoscopic surveillance to ensure the option of deferred curative surgery"[1]. Notice where the weight falls. The operation stays on the table the whole time. It has only been deferred.

Where this came from, and why that paper cannot be a guarantee

The source is the 2004 report from Habr-Gama's team in São Paulo. 265 patients with distal rectal adenocarcinoma had preoperative chemoradiation, and 71 of them (26.8%) reached a clinical complete response and were observed; in the observation group, five-year overall survival was 100% and disease-free survival 92%, with two endorectal recurrences[2].

"100% five-year survival" is the line most often lifted out of it. Its problem: a single centre, non-randomised, 71 selected patients, and no modern MRI assessment. That is where an idea started. It is not proof that the idea is safe.

Can a regrowth be rescued? And a number that rarely gets mentioned

A Memorial Sloan Kettering study compared 113 watch-and-wait patients with 136 whose surgical specimens confirmed a pathological complete response, at a median follow-up of 43 months:

  • All 22 local regrowths in the observation group were picked up at routine surveillance, and every one of them had salvage surgery.

  • Pelvic control was maintained after salvage in 20 of the 22 (91%) — meaning 2 of them lost it.

  • 93 of the 113 (82%) ended up keeping their rectum.

  • 36% of the patients who had a regrowth went on to develop distant metastases, against 1% of those who did not (p<0.001)[3].

That last line is the one I most want you to remember. A regrowth is not simply "the tumour came back, so we operate after all"; it also marks out a group at a far higher risk of distant disease. Treating regrowth as a small setback is the most dangerous optimism on this topic.

In the same study, five-year overall survival was 73% (60–89) in the observation group against 94% (90–99) in the group with a pathological complete response after surgery[3]. Those two figures cannot be set against each other directly — the observation group was older with lower tumours, and nobody was randomised. But they are a reminder of one thing: watch and wait is not a parallel road that is "as good as surgery".

How anyone decides the tumour has gone, and how imprecise that is

A clinical complete response is not the result of one test; it is a bundle of judgements. A Dutch prospective study assessed 50 patients with digital rectal examination, endoscopy and MRI, and 17 (34%) were complete responders. Examination plus endoscopy alone already discriminated reasonably well (area under the curve 0.88, where 1.0 is perfect and 0.5 is a coin toss), with MRI a little lower (0.79). Read together, the three can put confidence in a genuine complete response as high as 98%.

But the same study carries another sentence: when every test indicated residual tumour, 15% of those patients in fact had a complete response[4]. Read it the other way round: the three together are good, but none of them answers a yes/no question, and a biopsy is not the final arbiter either. This is a probability judgement made by an experienced team.

Taiwanese data point the same way. In the Linkou Chang Gung MRI study of 124 patients treated with total neoadjuvant therapy, the decision tree discriminated at 90.1% with a sensitivity of 77.6%[5] — even a Taiwanese imaging pathway built for exactly this purpose still misses about a fifth of complete responders. The limits of MRI are set out in full in "The one scan that decides your treatment".

One more sentence has to be written down: nobody can judge for themselves that their tumour has gone. "I have stopped bleeding" and "my tumour has disappeared" are two completely different statements. Symptoms usually improve early in treatment, while the tumour is still there.

It is a programme, not a decision

The Dutch organ-preservation programme enrolled 100 patients, at a median follow-up of 41.1 months: 61 were clinical complete responders at the first assessment and 39 were near-complete, of whom 24 converted to complete at a second assessment three months later. 15 developed a local regrowth, and every one of them could be salvaged. Three-year overall survival was 96.6% (89.9–98.9).

Its surveillance specification reads: endoscopy plus MRI every three months in the first year, and every six months after that, for years. The authors' conclusion comes with a condition attached — the approach is oncologically safe "when applying strict selection criteria and frequent follow-up"[6].

The NCCN patient guideline is blunter still: watch and wait "is only an option for carefully selected patients who agree to close surveillance programs", and the same document states that the benefits and risks of watch and wait against surgery "are not fully known"[7].

So think of it as a contract that signs away the next three years of your diary. If distance, work, caring for someone at home or cost mean you cannot be sure of getting there every single time, this road is not for you. That is not a moral judgement, it is a safety judgement — 88% of regrowth happens in the first two years, and an appointment missed in year one is the dangerous kind.

How time-critical is it? OPRA gives the answer

OPRA is the closest thing to prospective evidence here: 324 patients with stage II or III disease, at a median follow-up of 5.1 years, five-year disease-free survival 71% and 69% in the two arms (p=0.68). Of the 81 patients who had a regrowth, 94% occurred within two years and 99% within three. The reassuring finding is this: disease-free survival was 64% both for patients operated on straight after restaging and for watch-and-wait patients operated on because of a regrowth (p=0.94)[8].

Two things have to be read together. The good half: a regrowth caught in time gives a result much like operating at the outset. The limiting half: OPRA enrolled selected patients at experienced centres, and "caught in time" is the premise sitting underneath that 64%.

The guidelines sit in the same place. ASTRO 2025 holds that non-operative management may be considered in selected patients and should be settled by multidisciplinary consensus[9]; NCCN only brought watch and wait formally into its professional guideline with Version 3.2024[10]. This road is new.

Two asymmetries to know about in Taiwan

I could not find a citable formal reimbursement provision covering this surveillance schedule under the National Health Insurance (健保), so I will not claim here that endoscopy or pelvic MRI is certainly covered — check with your case manager (個管師) or the hospital's billing office, and ask specifically whether "every three months" falls inside what is covered. What actually stops Taiwanese patients is usually not just the money: appointment scheduling, MRI waiting times, and the round trip to a medical centre every three months are what make people drop out of a surveillance programme. Before deciding on this road, ask about the cost and the scheduling together.

Two other things are worth knowing in advance. First, the bundled payment item for preoperative short-course radiotherapy, 36024B, is payable once in a lifetime, and patients who need a second course of salvage pelvic radiotherapy for a recurrence after surgery may not use it[11]. Second, the oxaliplatin and capecitabine that consolidation chemotherapy runs on have no preoperative rectal indication in the drug reimbursement regulations. Which parts are self-pay is a question for your case manager; the regulation text is quoted in "How the treatment order is set, and why it is not a menu".

Most people taking this road have to finish consolidation chemotherapy first, and the emergency warnings for that stretch are the same as everybody else's, with nothing left out: a fever over 38°C, severe diarrhoea with dehydration (dizzy on standing, passing noticeably less urine), mouth ulcers bad enough to stop you swallowing, or numbness in the hands and feet that is worse than before. On capecitabine, watch your palms and soles: redness, peeling and tingling is hand-foot syndrome. On oxaliplatin, touching cold water or drinking something cold will make your fingers or throat feel as if they have been given an electric shock; keep away from cold and say so at every visit. Never stop or reduce a dose yourself.

The thing I least want to happen

What I least want is for somebody to read this and go back to their doctor saying "I don't want surgery." This article gives nobody that standing.

Two questions can be asked out loud. First, "could a case like mine be suitable for watch and wait? And if it is not, what makes it unsuitable?" The answer might be the tumour's position, it might be the degree of response, it might be that the hospital does not run a surveillance programme of this kind — and every one of those is more use to you than a vague hope. Second, "if I take this road, what does the follow-up timetable look like, and who arranges it?" Get that timetable in your hand before you decide, rather than deciding first and working out afterwards how to fit it in.

One last thing to be clear about. If the team recommends an operation, that recommendation rests on things you cannot see — the detail on the MRI, the mucosa under the endoscope, the reading of the pathology. The threshold for watch and wait is not your willingness. It is your tumour.

From the physician

Whether watch and wait is even on the table depends on your tumour's position, how complete the response is, and whether your hospital runs a surveillance programme — not on how much you want it. Ask what the follow-up timetable would look like and who arranges it, and get that timetable in your hand before deciding. If the team has recommended surgery, that recommendation rests on findings you cannot see.
