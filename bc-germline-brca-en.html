Family history: should you test for BRCA now?

Germline testing changes the surgical decision and the age at which your family starts screening, so it is discussed before the operation; this also sets the guideline threshold and the NHI provision side by side.

Breast Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 14 references
#Genetics & Family#Screening#Surgery#NHI Coverage#Decisions

From blood draw to result, a median of 10 days and of 18 days — the time is there. The real gap is on the other side: the guideline says everyone aged 65 or under should be offered testing, while the only thing listed in the NHI provision is triple-negative breast cancer.

Nobody in my family has had it, so I probably do not need testing — ten years ago that reasoning would barely have been challenged in clinic, and today it no longer matches the guidelines. Of the three reports that all get called "genetic testing", this article deals with the one taken from blood: it looks for a variant you were born with and may pass on to your family, which is a different thing from a variant the tumour grew; how the three lines divide up is in "Which lines of the report actually change treatment". This one is time-critical: it may change your surgical decision, so it is discussed before the operation, not after treatment is over.

The threshold is no longer "test only if there is a family history"

The 2024 ASCO and Society of Surgical Oncology guideline on germline testing is explicit: all newly diagnosed breast cancer patients aged 65 or under should be offered BRCA1/2 testing; those over 65 should be offered it selectively according to personal history, family history, ancestry, or eligibility for particular drug treatments; women with a second primary breast cancer in the same or the opposite breast should be offered testing; and genes other than BRCA1/2 should be offered selectively according to family history and need[1].

"Offered" is not "must be done" — it means this should be brought up and discussed with you, and you decide.

What are the chances of finding something

CARRIERS, a population-based case-control study, sequenced 32,247 women with breast cancer and 32,544 women without. Across 12 established breast cancer susceptibility genes, pathogenic variants were present in 5.03% of the breast cancer patients and 1.63% of the controls; by odds ratio, BRCA1 came out at 7.62 (95% confidence interval 5.33 to 11.27) and BRCA2 at 5.23 (4.09 to 6.77), both high risk, while PALB2 at 3.83 (2.68 to 5.63) was moderate risk[2]. An odds ratio here is one group's ratio of carrying-to-not-carrying divided by the other's; it is not a multiple of probability.

The same study also reports that BARD1, RAD51C and RAD51D were associated with the risk of ER-negative and triple-negative breast cancer, while ATM, CDH1 and CHEK2 were associated with ER-positive breast cancer[2] — the genes line up with the subtypes. This is American population data; for the carrier rate in Taiwan I could not find statistics I could cite, so the percentages above should not be read as Taiwanese proportions.

"72%" — which group of people is that number about

The most quoted figure comes from this one: a prospective cohort of 6,036 women with BRCA1 and 3,820 with BRCA2 variants, in which the cumulative risk of breast cancer to age 80 was 72% for BRCA1 (95% confidence interval 65% to 79%) and 69% for BRCA2 (61% to 77%); cumulative ovarian cancer risk was 44% and 17%; and the cumulative risk of cancer in the other breast within 20 years of the breast cancer diagnosis was 40% and 26%[3].

But look closely at the denominator: 94% of that cohort was referred through familial cancer clinics and only 6% came from population studies, and the risk rose with the number of first- and second-degree relatives affected[3]. It describes carriers already known to have familial clustering, not a carrier picked at random. PALB2 often gets put in the same box by mistake: a study of 524 families in 21 countries worked out an absolute risk to age 80 of 53% (44% to 63%)[4] — high, but not in the same class as BRCA1/2.

Why this has to be discussed before surgery

Because when you know the result changes which operation you end up having, and several independent datasets show it. At four academic centres in Toronto, 1,007 women with breast cancer had rapid genetic testing, the median time to disclosure of the result was 10 days, 6% carried a BRCA variant, and 95.7% of the carriers said they had used the result to make their surgical decision[5]. In Britain, among 580 women with breast cancer whose testing was ordered directly by the treating team, the median time from initiation to receiving the result was 18 days and 64% received it before surgery; among those who learned they were carriers before surgery 88% chose bilateral mastectomy, against 5% of the non-carriers[6]. In an American cohort of 633 Black women aged under 50, 82.8% of carriers tested before surgery had bilateral mastectomy, against 40% of those tested only after surgery[7].

Ten days, eighteen days: these are achievable timescales, so asking "do I need this test, and is there time" at the first clinic visit where surgery is discussed is a reasonable request. It does not amount to "wait for the test before operating" — the waiting time from diagnosis to surgery is itself associated with survival, which is in "What the first month after diagnosis looks like". What you want is to get both matters into the same clinic visit so the team can schedule them together, not to push your own surgery back.

A positive result does not put you automatically on the road to bilateral mastectomy

The 2020 ASCO, ASTRO and Society of Surgical Oncology guideline on the management of hereditary breast cancer is more reserved than most people assume: newly diagnosed patients carrying a BRCA1/2 variant may be considered for breast-conserving therapy, with local control of the index cancer similar to non-carriers; but because the risk of cancer in the other breast is substantial, particularly in young women, a discussion of bilateral mastectomy is necessary. The same guideline also states that there is no evidence that BRCA1/2 carriers have increased toxicity or contralateral breast cancer events from radiotherapy, and that ATM carriers should not be refused radiotherapy; for those with a germline TP53 variant, mastectomy is recommended and radiotherapy is contraindicated[8]. "Must be discussed" and "must be done" are two different things.

The data on contralateral mastectomy and survival are not clean in themselves. Among 390 women with stage I or II breast cancer who were carriers, from 12 cancer genetics clinics, median follow-up 14.3 years: 20-year survival was 88% in those who had contralateral mastectomy against 66% in those who did not, and in multivariable analysis the hazard ratio for breast cancer death was 0.52 (95% confidence interval 0.29 to 0.93) — deaths occurring at roughly half the rate; but in an analysis of 79 propensity-score-matched pairs it no longer reached statistical significance (0.60, 0.34 to 1.06, P value 0.08), and the authors' concluding sentence says the number of events was small and further study is needed to confirm it[9]. Both results have to be read together. Preventive contralateral mastectomy in non-carriers is a separate question, and it is in "Conservation or mastectomy: is survival the same?".

There is one more kind of result that frightens people: the variant of uncertain significance. The guideline states plainly that such results should not influence treatment management and that those carrying one should be followed up for possible reclassification[1]. It is not "something bad has been found"; it is "nobody yet knows what that position means". A test result may also affect eligibility for certain drugs, and that part is in "After metastasis, genomic testing is about finding a drug".

The guideline says everyone aged 65 or under; the NHI pays for triple-negative disease

The guideline side is set out above. The NHI side: under the next-generation sequencing items of the fee schedule, item 30301B, BRCA1/2 gene testing, is paid at 10,000 points, germline testing uses a blood specimen, it is restricted to regional hospitals and above or hospitals accredited for cancer care quality, a molecular tumour board is required, and it is reimbursed once in a lifetime per person per cancer type. In the appendix listing the cancers covered, the only breast cancer listed is triple-negative breast cancer, with the indications in two groups — locally advanced or metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (having had preoperative, adjuvant or metastatic chemotherapy, or being unable to have chemotherapy), and early triple-negative breast cancer (either no preoperative chemotherapy with a tumour larger than two centimetres or axillary nodal metastasis, or no pathological complete response after preoperative chemotherapy); and ER, PR and HER2 immunohistochemistry must all be negative before it can be claimed[10]. This reimbursement came into effect on 1 May 2024[11].

Putting the two side by side: taking the subtype distribution in the American SEER data, triple-negative disease accounts for about 12.2%[12], which is to say roughly 87% of breast cancer patients fall outside this reimbursement — for hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative patients and for HER2-positive patients, this item is not covered by the NHI. I could not find citable statistics on the subtype distribution in Taiwan, so that estimate can only be read as an order of magnitude. How the difference is worked out, and whether there is any other route, is to be confirmed with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office. I am not going to tell you that this is not much money.

Two further items I could not find provisions for: there is no separate procedure item for "genetic counselling" in the NHI fee schedule, and I could not find the basis for reimbursing testing of unaffected relatives either. These two are for your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office — do not work them out from a price list found online. The logic of deciding to pay for a test yourself, across all these items, is in "This test is self-pay. Is it worth it?".

On the family side, the gap is wider than you would think

When a variant is found, its use is often not for you. A meta-analysis of 30 studies shows that in hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndrome families, only 33% of relatives take up cascade testing (95% confidence interval 25% to 42%), with female relatives higher than male and first-degree higher than second-degree[13]. One in three.

And surveillance for a carrier relative looks nothing like surveillance for other women. The American College of Radiology's 2023 updated recommendations are that women at genetically increased risk, women whose calculated lifetime risk is 20% or more, and women who had chest radiotherapy when young should start annual breast magnetic resonance imaging at 25 to 30 years of age, with annual mammography as well (starting between 25 and 40 depending on the type of risk); and that all women should have a risk assessment completed by age 25[14]. Annual magnetic resonance imaging starting in her twenties is not something she will know about unless somebody tells her.

At the surgical consultation, get these three sentences out

Timing matters more than content here. If you have not yet had the operation, this is the visit:

  • "At my age and with my subtype, would the guideline say I should be offered germline BRCA testing? If so, how is that arranged here?"

  • "How long from the blood draw to the result? Is there time before the surgical decision? If there is not, how will that be handled?"

  • "Does my situation meet that NHI provision? If it does not, how much is it privately, where do I pay, and is there any other route?"

If the result is that you are a carrier, save a fourth sentence for the next visit: "Which relatives should I tell? Where do they go, and from what age do they start being followed?" Keep asking until you have a name, a specialty and an age. That is what this report is really for.

From the physician

Being a carrier or not changes your options; it does not decide the extent of your surgery for you. If you have not yet had the operation, get "do I need this test, is there time, and do I meet that NHI provision" said out loud at this visit; and if you already know you are a carrier, the next step is to ask which relatives should go, and from what age they start being followed.
