WHEN YOU ARE THE FAMILY

Caring for someone: divide the work first

Agreeing that the eldest brother will speak for everyone is a family arrangement, not a legal power. Only 48.61% had anybody to take over.

Situation Guides · When You Are the Family · 7 references
#Family#Caregivers#Law & rights#Support services

Dividing the work is not a question of who has more free time. Families that cannot keep it up usually have enough people; what they lack is a fixed person for a few jobs — who listens in clinic, who keeps the records and the drugs, who builds the rota, who answers relatives, who deals with the social work office.

After the diagnosis most families do the same thing: everybody comes at once. The first fortnight holds. Around week six it comes apart — one cannot get leave, one has slept on the ward's fold-out bed until their back will not straighten, two have stopped speaking over who is doing more. What I can set out is the size of this job, who in the Taiwanese data carries it, and what having somebody to take over is connected with.

Look at the size of the job before you argue about who does it

Caring can be counted. A US national survey of cancer caregivers, 2003 to 2006, estimated informal caring time in the two years after diagnosis across nine cancers plus non-Hodgkin lymphoma: an average of 8.3 hours a day for 13.7 months, with both daily hours and months significantly higher where the disease had spread to distant sites[1]. "The cost that isn't on the receipt" cites this study; the cell worth the extra look here is the metastatic one.

Taiwan's official number is higher, but the population is a different one. The Ministry of Health and Welfare's Survey of the Living Conditions of the Elderly — Report on Principal Family Carers (106 年老人狀況調查—主要家庭照顧者調查報告) covered people aged 65 and over with difficulty in at least one activity of daily living or instrumental activity of daily living, cared for mainly by family; 469 completed interviews, 60.67% response: 11.06 hours of care a day on average, 48.40% giving ten hours or more, 7.80 years of caring on average. The report states that this describes those 469 questionnaires and is not an estimate for the population[2].

The two cannot be converted into each other; many cancer patients do not sit inside that frame of older people with functional impairment. Taiwan has no national hours-of-care statistic taken over principal family carers of cancer patients, so "the average Taiwanese cancer family gives so many hours a day" is a claim I cannot source. Both point one way: this is work on the scale of a full-time job.

Who does this work has a very clear shape in the data

Of those 469 principal family carers, 60.98% were women and 39.02% men; by relationship, 44.35% were the patient's children, 35.18% the spouse or cohabiting partner, 16.63% daughters-in-law or sons-in-law, 3.84% other. The report adds a line of its own under that third category: of those 78 people, 1 was a son-in-law and 77 were daughters-in-law[2].

Leaving work has a direction too. 31.77% were in work at the time of the survey, against 41.15% before caring began, 9.38 percentage points lower; of the 193 who had been working before, 35.23% gave up the job because of caring — women 43.93%, men 24.42%; of the 149 still working, 32.21% said the work had been affected[2].

I am copying these down. I am not going to tell your family how to divide this; that is not my place. Whether to divide it along the shape in the data is your family's business.

The rota is connected in the data with whether the carer sees a doctor

In the same survey, only 48.61% of principal family carers had anybody who could take over, and the figure fell with age (under 45, 63.04%; 45 to 64, 54.22%; 65 and over, 36.78%). Then the cell I think most deserves to be seen: 21.32% had, because of caring, not seen a doctor when they were unwell themselves; among those with nobody to take over it was 25.73%, among those with somebody, 16.67%[2].

That is a cross-tabulation of two variables in one cross-sectional survey, adjusted for nothing, and it could run the other way (a sicker patient makes a relief carer harder to find and leaves the carer less time). So all I will say is that in the data those two sit together. A study on the oncology ward of a Taiwanese medical centre, 137 patient–carer pairs, also listed lack of relief support among the factors associated with overall burden, the other three being psychological symptoms, daily hours of care and the patient's age[3]. Single institution, cross-sectional.

How the carer's own health risk has been measured, and where the line falls, is in "The carer breaks first".

"Point of contact" is not a position the law empowers

Many families nominate one person as the point of contact for the team. Useful in practice, but its nature is worth knowing. Articles 63, 64 and 81 of the Medical Care Act (醫療法) all read "the patient or their statutory agent, spouse, relatives or person with a relevant connection" — the statute lists these categories side by side, in no order, and empowers nobody to name a single representative[4] (current text amended 115-05-08, promulgated 115-05-15, verified 30 August 2026). Two things read alongside it. A relative signs a consent form only on the precondition in Article 63 II: "where the patient is a minor or is unable to sign in person". And Article 4 II of the Patient Right to Autonomy Act (病人自主權利法) provides that a person with a relevant connection shall not obstruct the institution or physician acting on the patient's own decision about medical options. While the patient can still express himself and has said he does not want something, a family member's signature cannot stand in place of what he wants.

So agreeing at home that the eldest brother will speak for everyone is a family arrangement; it does not mean he can decide for the patient in law. Agency with legal effect is a separate set of things with tight conditions, in "When he cannot decide, who decides".

What the person keeping the records should have in hand

A German study of 188 pairs of cancer patients and their principal carers found the carers' distress and anxiety scores both significantly higher than the patients' own, with 43.6% of carers reporting 10 or more unmet needs, mostly in medical services and information, and only then emotional and psychological[5]. What families are short of is often not comfort but information they can read.

"Keeping the records" is a real role: return dates, which specialty is doing what, the current medications, the last blood test, when the next scan is booked. One thing to be clear about first — Article 71 of the Medical Care Act says an institution "shall, at the request of the patient it has treated, provide a copy of the medical records and, where necessary, a Chinese-language summary"[4]. The subject is the patient, not the family. Families do it on his behalf all the time, but what to bring and what it costs differ by hospital, and I could not find a central rule; ask the medical records office (病歷室) first.

Caring for two people at once: the system already treats that as high burden

In that 106 survey, counting in other family members with functional impairment or children under 3, each principal family carer was looking after 1.14 people on average, highest among those under 45 (1.24); 91.68% lived with the person they cared for[2]. The Ministry's Screening Indicators for High-Burden Family Carers (高負荷家庭照顧者初篩指標), written for professionals (second revision 112-12-22), turn this into an operational definition: item 4, "no relief carer", means 20 hours a week or more of primary care work with no help from family, relatives or care resources, or difficulty seeking help because of traditional cultural or gender factors; item 5, "caring for two or more people", expressly includes having to divide time to care for a child under 3[6] (verified 30 August 2026). A patient above and a small child below, on one person, is something the system's documents expect. Talking to a child at home is in "How you tell a child matters more than whether".

What this piece leaves alone, and one threshold to read first

Money — leave, carer fees, grants — is in "The cost that isn't on the receipt". Asking in clinic and at a family meeting is in "Those ten minutes in clinic: what the family should ask". The signals that the carer is going under are in "The carer breaks first". Dividing the work across cities or countries is in "Caring from another city, or another country". The last stretch is in "The last few weeks: what actually happens". The head and neck specifics are in "What the person sitting next to the patient can do".

And one threshold I would rather you knew now than after the phone call: long-term care admission requires assessment by the county or city long-term care management centre (長期照顧管理中心) at long-term care need level 2 or above, plus falling into one of five categories, and none of those five is "has cancer"[7] — the full five, and the two routes that remain open, are in "The carer breaks first". A patient in his forties on chemoradiotherapy, very weak but without a disability certificate, mostly will not qualify. Eligibility changes quickly; the centre's actual assessment governs (verified 30 August 2026).

Write a name after every line

Take a sheet of paper, one line for each thing somebody has to do on a fixed basis — who listens in clinic, who keeps the records and the medications, who builds the rota, who gives relatives one single answer, who deals with the medical social work office (社工室) or the case manager (個管師). Then write after each line the name of whoever is actually doing it now. After that sheet there is usually no more discussion about who is working hardest, because the same name is on most of the lines.

One more to ask out loud at the next visit: what this hospital's social work office and case managers can actually take on. I could not find a national document defining what they cover, hospitals differ a good deal, and it can only be asked face to face.

From the physician

Long-term care admission needs the county or city management centre to assess need level 2 or above, plus one of five categories — and none of those five is having cancer. Knowing that threshold now beats finding out after the phone call. Eligibility changes quickly, and the centre's actual assessment is what counts.
