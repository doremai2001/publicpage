HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

Treatment is a combination, not a menu

From surgery first to definitive chemoradiation, why chemotherapy is added after surgery, and where protons and hyperthermia stand, with the evidence and cost of each option.

Head & Neck Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 16 references
#Decisions#Surgery#Chemotherapy#Proton Therapy#Hyperthermia#NHI Coverage

Nobody is handed all five treatments and asked to pick. What is worth knowing is how strong the evidence behind each combination is and what each one asks of you, and that is what this page goes through, option by option.

A lot of people walk into clinic holding a sheet of paper: surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, laid out like a menu waiting to be ticked. The first thing I usually do is take the sheet away. How those five things get combined is decided by where the tumour sits, the stage, whether it can be removed with clear margins, whether you will still be able to speak and swallow afterwards, and whether your body can take the treatment. Head and neck cancer in Taiwan is dominated by oral cavity disease — in the Ministry of Health and Welfare (衛生福利部) analysis of the 2023 national cancer registry, oral cavity cancer (including oropharynx and hypopharynx) ranks fourth by age-standardised incidence in men, with a median age at diagnosis of 60, five years earlier than the 65 for all cancers combined[1]. Most of the people in front of me are still working, still supporting a household, and still earning a living with their mouth. All of that goes into the discussion.

Surgery first, or straight to definitive chemoradiation?

Oral cavity cancer is usually surgery-led: the tumour comes out, and the pathology report then decides whether radiotherapy or concurrent chemoradiation is added. Larynx, hypopharynx and oropharynx cancers often have a second road — definitive concurrent chemoradiation, keeping the organ in place. Neither road is the more advanced one. They have different indications. The NCCN head and neck guideline, revised several times a year, handles oral cavity, oropharynx, hypopharynx, larynx, nasopharynx, sinus and salivary gland in separate chapters[2], precisely because the answer for each site is a different answer.

So the multidisciplinary team meeting is not a formality. Before a decision is made you want head and neck surgery, radiation oncology, medical oncology, pathology and dentistry around one table — especially for the question of how this person is going to eat afterwards. A surgeon alone will not think that through as completely as a room will.

Whether chemotherapy is added after surgery comes down to two lines in the pathology report

Whether concurrent chemotherapy is added to postoperative radiotherapy hangs on two findings: a positive margin, and extranodal extension (ENE) in a lymph node. This is not a preference of mine. EORTC 22931 enrolled 334 patients, postoperative radiotherapy with or without cisplatin: 5-year progression-free survival 47% versus 36%, 5-year overall survival 53% versus 40% (HR 0.70, p=0.02), locoregional relapse 18% versus 31%[3]. (A hazard ratio is a ratio of the risk of death; 0.70 means this group's risk was about seven-tenths of the other group's — an average across a whole population, not a number calculated for you. Read every HR in this article that way.) RTOG 9501 enrolled 459 patients: 2-year locoregional control 82% versus 72% (HR 0.61, p=0.01), disease-free survival also favouring chemoradiation, but overall survival was not significantly improved (HR 0.84, p=0.19)[4]. The honest way to say it is: both trials showed better local control, and the survival benefit was seen only in the European trial.

The price belongs in the same breath. In RTOG 9501, acute grade 3 or higher toxicity was 77% with chemoradiation against 34% with radiotherapy alone, and 4 patients died as a direct result of the combined treatment[4]. In EORTC 22931 it was 41% versus 21%[3]. That is why kidney function, hearing and performance status get checked and rechecked, and it is why "should I ask for chemotherapy to be added" is not a good question.

The pooled analysis of the two trials is more specific. Extranodal extension and/or a microscopically involved margin were the only risk factors significant in both trials; patients whose only risk factor was two or more involved nodes without extranodal extension did not appear to benefit from added chemotherapy[5]. Stage III–IV disease, perineural invasion and vascular embolism showed a trend only. So if someone tells you that involved lymph nodes automatically mean chemotherapy, that sentence has travelled further than the evidence.

Before radiotherapy, during it, or after it — the timing is the whole benefit

There is a large answer to this one. MACH-NC covered 101 trials and 18,951 patients, median follow-up 6.5 years: concurrent chemotherapy gave a hazard ratio for death of 0.83 and an absolute survival benefit of 6.5% at 5 years, 3.6% at 10 years; induction chemotherapy HR 0.96 and adjuvant chemotherapy HR 1.02, neither statistically significant[6]. The same analysis showed the benefit shrinking as age rises. So the value of chemotherapy is not in whether a drug is given, but in whether it is given at the same time as radiotherapy.

I know that paragraph is full of names. Only one line has to survive it: chemotherapy has to run alongside radiotherapy to buy survival. Given before it or after it, the numbers do not win.

Induction chemotherapy has been tested on its own terms. PARADIGM compared TPF induction then chemoradiation against chemoradiation alone: 3-year survival 73% versus 78%, HR 1.09, p=0.77, febrile neutropenia in 16 patients versus 1[7]. It is still used today, but for shrinking bulky disease quickly or selecting patients for organ preservation — not to live longer.

Larynx preservation is the classic case here. RTOG 91-11, 520 patients followed to 10.8 years, found that both induction chemotherapy followed by radiotherapy and concurrent cisplatin gave better laryngectomy-free survival than radiotherapy alone; and between the two, concurrent cisplatin preserved significantly more larynxes (HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.37–0.89, p=0.0050), while overall survival did not differ and there was a trend towards worse survival in the concurrent arm (HR 1.25, p=0.08)[8]. Keeping the organ and living longer are not the same objective, and that is worth saying out loud before a decision is made.

If you cannot have cisplatin, targeted therapy is a fallback, not an upgrade

The Bonner trial compared radiotherapy plus cetuximab with radiotherapy alone: median locoregional control 24.4 versus 14.9 months, median survival 49.0 versus 29.3 months[9], and 5-year survival 45.6% versus 36.4%[10]. Patients read that result in the press and ask to switch to targeted therapy. Two things need saying. First, the comparator was radiotherapy alone, not concurrent chemoradiation. Second, in Taiwan, National Health Insurance (健保) drug reimbursement rule §9.27 covers cetuximab in head and neck cancer only for recurrent and/or metastatic squamous cell carcinoma not amenable to local therapy, in patients who have not previously claimed it, with prior authorisation, a total course capped at 18 weeks and applications made every 9 weeks, and cetuximab and immune checkpoint inhibitors may only be used one or the other, with no switching if treatment fails[11]. The NHI index of covered cancer drugs lists cetuximab under that same §9.27 condition[12]. In other words, cetuximab given concurrently with definitive radiotherapy is not a reimbursed use. This misunderstanding comes up constantly in clinic, and knowing it early saves a wasted trip.

How far the proton evidence actually goes

A multicentre randomised phase 3 trial comparing protons with photons, published in 2026, enrolled 440 patients with stage III–IV oropharyngeal cancer. Protons met non-inferiority for progression-free survival (3-year 82.5% versus 83.0%) and were better on toxicity — dysphagia 31% versus 49%, xerostomia 33% versus 45%, gastrostomy-tube feeding 26.8% versus 40.2%; 5-year overall survival was 90.9% versus 81.0% (HR 0.58, p=0.045)[13].

That survival figure has to be read carefully. It is a secondary endpoint, the trial closed early at an interim analysis, and only 72% of the proton arm and 62% of the photon arm actually received the modality they were assigned — a high crossover rate[13]. The most I can say: a randomised trial showed protons matched photons for disease control, with less severe dysphagia and fewer feeding tubes, and a survival signal that still needs confirming. Not "protons cure more people." In Taiwan proton therapy is self-pay, and the NHI's own policy page puts a course at around NT$900,000[14]. Whether that money is worth spending has to be worked out against your tumour location and the dose to your normal tissue, not settled with "newer is better."

Hyperthermia has Taiwanese data for exactly one situation

A single-arm phase II trial from Shin Kong Wu Ho-Su Memorial Hospital (新光醫院) in Taipei enrolled 35 patients with previously irradiated, unresectable recurrent head and neck cancer, giving salvage re-irradiation to 50 Gy with weekly hyperthermia and low-dose chemotherapy: objective response rate 82.9%, median survival 32.8 months, acute grade 3 mucositis only 5.7%, no burns[15]. For a re-irradiation population those are good numbers.

But in the same paper, 12 of the 35 patients developed osteonecrosis[15]. Single-arm, 35 patients, re-irradiation only, no randomised comparison, and no evidence at all in first-line treatment. Hyperthermia in Taiwan is self-pay, and one medical centre's radiation oncology department puts it plainly: a self-funded medical service, not covered by National Health Insurance or by any commercial health insurance, roughly NT$15,000 a session, positioned explicitly as an adjunct[16]. It is not something that replaces standard treatment.

Before you decide, read those two lines out loud

First: what does my pathology report say about the margin and about extranodal extension? That question decides whether chemotherapy is added, and it is worth asking your doctor to read it to you.

Second: is the goal of my treatment combination to clear the tumour, to preserve organ function, or to control symptoms? The trade-offs are completely different for each.

Third: if my kidney function or hearing rules out cisplatin, what is the alternative, is it covered by National Health Insurance, and what would I pay out of pocket? Getting those numbers straight beats an evening of searching online.

From the physician

These numbers are population averages. Your own combination turns on the margins and extranodal extension in the pathology report, on your kidney function and hearing, and on whether speech or swallowing matters more to you. Write those few items on a sheet of paper and bring it to clinic; the conversation goes much faster.
