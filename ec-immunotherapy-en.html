WHAT THE EVIDENCE SAYS FOR EACH TREATMENT

Who does immunotherapy actually help

Evidence exists in only three situations: residual tumour after surgery, first-line metastatic disease stratified by PD-L1, and chemoradiotherapy plus immunotherapy, where most trials were negative. The NHI clauses are attached word for word.

Oesophageal Cancer Guide · What the Evidence Says for Each Treatment · 18 references
#Immunotherapy#Biomarkers#NHI Coverage#Evidence

The same drug is a randomised-trial-backed standard in one situation and, in another, a hypothesis that three phase III trials have failed to confirm. This article puts CheckMate 577, KEYNOTE-590, CheckMate 648 and the three chemoradiotherapy-plus-immunotherapy trials back into their own situations, then pastes in the NHI text.

"Doctor, can I have immunotherapy?" That is now the most frequent question in the oesophageal cancer clinic, and it comes from people just diagnosed and from people who have already had their operation. My answer has to be split into three situations before it makes sense: in oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma, immunotherapy has evidence in only three very specific situations — people whose pathology report after surgery still shows residual tumour, people with metastatic disease starting first-line systemic treatment, and "definitive chemoradiotherapy plus immunotherapy", which is still being tested in trials. The first two are backed by randomised trials; for the third, of the three phase III trials that had read out by 2026, none met its primary endpoint — one of them left a signal that needs re-testing, which I come to below. Which situation you are in decides whether this drug means anything for you.

My position first: concurrent chemoradiotherapy and proton therapy for oesophageal cancer are treatments I deliver myself, every week; the other side of every comparison in this topic is my thoracic-surgery colleagues' operation and my gastroenterology colleagues' endoscopy. That is why every comparison comes with its primary source, so you can take it to the surgical and gastroenterology clinics for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

Situation one: the operation is done, and the pathology report still shows tumour

The evidence for immunotherapy after surgery comes from CheckMate 577. This trial, published in NEJM in 2021, enrolled 794 patients with oesophageal or gastro-oesophageal junction cancer (squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma both included), stage II to III, who had completed preoperative chemoradiotherapy and then surgery with clear margins but still had residual tumour on the pathology report, and randomised them to a year of nivolumab or placebo. Median disease-free survival 22.4 against 11.0 months, hazard ratio 0.69. A hazard ratio divides the speed at which events occur in one group by the speed in the other; 0.69 means recurrence or death came about 30% slower. The price was grade 3 to 4 treatment-related adverse events in 13% against 6%, and discontinuation for adverse events in 9% against 3%[1]. Two things need spelling out. First, the primary endpoint was disease-free survival, not overall survival; I could not find a formal overall survival paper, only a second-hand statement in the introduction of a 2026 Dutch real-world study: the overall survival benefit in CheckMate 577 "did not reach statistical significance"[2]. That same Dutch nationwide matched dataset (311 against 311, 2020 to 2023) saw two-year overall survival of 66.8% against 58.8%, hazard ratio 0.75, but the authors themselves write that follow-up time and event numbers are still limited and the result should be read conservatively[2]. Second, the precondition for this situation is "residual tumour still present" — the Japan Esophageal Society's 2022 guideline, while strongly recommending postoperative nivolumab, lists its own reservations: in people who achieved a pathological complete response the efficacy is not established, and in people who had preoperative chemotherapy only, without radiotherapy, it is not established either[3]. So if your pathology report after surgery says "no residual tumour", the evidence here does not cover you.

Situation two: already metastatic, first-line with immunotherapy added — PD-L1 decides how big the gain is

For metastatic or incurable oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma there are two large randomised trials of first-line chemotherapy plus immunotherapy. KEYNOTE-590 (749 patients, squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma, pembrolizumab plus cisplatin/5-FU): in people with squamous cell carcinoma and PD-L1 CPS of 10 or above, median overall survival 13.9 against 8.8 months, hazard ratio 0.57; in the whole randomised population 12.4 against 9.8 months, hazard ratio 0.73; grade 3 or higher treatment-related adverse events 72% against 68%[4]. The 2025 five-year follow-up makes it more concrete: five-year survival in the whole population 10.6% against 3.0%, hazard ratio 0.72[5]: one person in ten alive past five years, against one in thirty. The difference is real, but it is not cure. CheckMate 648 (970 patients, all squamous cell carcinoma): in people with PD-L1 TPS of 1% or above, median overall survival with nivolumab plus chemotherapy 15.4 against 9.1 months, hazard ratio 0.54; whole population 13.2 against 10.7 months, hazard ratio 0.74; grade 3 to 4 treatment-related adverse events 47% with nivolumab plus chemotherapy, 32% with nivolumab plus ipilimumab, 36% with chemotherapy[6]. The 2026 five-year follow-up points the same way: hazard ratio 0.62 for TPS 1% or above, 0.77 for the whole population[7].

The correct way to read the PD-L1 number is "the higher it is, the bigger the gain" — not "low means useless". The whole-population hazard ratios are significant too; it is just that people with low expression gain less and their confidence intervals are wider. The 2026 update of the American Society of Clinical Oncology guideline says exactly that: for people with PD-L1 expression of 1 or above, immunotherapy plus doublet chemotherapy is recommended, and the higher the expression the more likely the benefit[8]. Four Chinese first-line trials in squamous cell carcinoma (camrelizumab, toripalimab, sintilimab, tislelizumab) all showed longer survival, but I could not find any provision on the availability or reimbursement of these four drugs in Taiwan, so I am not writing about them. Second line has its own evidence: ATTRACTION-3 (419 patients with squamous cell carcinoma, including Taiwanese centres, regardless of PD-L1), nivolumab alone against chemotherapy, median overall survival 10.9 against 8.4 months, hazard ratio 0.77, grade 3 to 4 treatment-related adverse events 18% against 63%[9].

Situation three: definitive chemoradiotherapy plus immunotherapy — negative is negative

This is the situation most people want and the one with the weakest evidence. For a locally advanced patient having definitive chemoradiotherapy, "adding immunotherapy while we're at it" sounds very reasonable, and three phase III trials have read out. KEYNOTE-975 (703 patients, squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma, pembrolizumab concurrent with chemoradiotherapy and then as consolidation): the manufacturer's April 2026 financial filing states in its own words that the primary endpoint of event-free survival "did not demonstrate a statistically significant improvement"[10]; the trial is completed, there is no paper, and the numbers do not exist anywhere that can be cited[11]. RATIONALE-311 (370 Chinese patients with squamous cell carcinoma, tislelizumab concurrent with chemoradiotherapy and then as consolidation): the results posted on the registry, median progression-free survival 29.0 against 28.9 months, hazard ratio 0.92 (p=0.30); overall survival hazard ratio 1.10[12]. Negative.

SKYSCRAPER-07 (760 patients with squamous cell carcinoma, consolidation after chemoradiotherapy with atezolizumab with or without tiragolumab, against placebo) has to be written from both sides. The primary endpoint was atezolizumab plus tiragolumab against placebo: progression-free survival hazard ratio 0.82 (p=0.095), overall survival hazard ratio 0.91, not met; but the atezolizumab-alone arm against placebo had an overall survival hazard ratio of 0.69 (95% confidence interval 0.52 to 0.91), and its p value can only be taken as a reference — that comparison sat third in the trial's pre-specified testing order, and once the earlier ones fail, the later ones do not count as formal results; treatment-related adverse events in the three arms were 74.8%, 65.2% and 55.4%, and treatment-related deaths 1.2%, 0.8% and 1.6%[13]. Writing "consolidation immunotherapy has been proven effective" goes too far; writing "all negative" is not honest either; the correct sentence is "a trial that did not meet its primary endpoint, leaving a signal that needs to be tested again". Still awaited: durvalumab's KUNLUN, for which the manufacturer's appendix gives an expected data readout in 2027[14]; camrelizumab's ESCORT-CRT, whose registry status is unknown and has not been updated since 2021[15]. So in clinic today, definitive chemoradiotherapy plus immunotherapy is not standard treatment; if someone suggests it to you, the right question is "which clinical trial is this in?"

The NHI clauses, copied here word for word

The NHI Administration's Drug Reimbursement Regulations, Section 9, item 9.69 immune checkpoint inhibitors, August 2026 consolidated edition, contain two clauses for oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma. Second line, single agent (my translation of the clause): "Oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma: nivolumab only, for patients with unresectable advanced or recurrent oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma that has progressed after combination chemotherapy containing a platinum and a fluoropyrimidine. (113/4/1, 113/6/1, 115/2/1)" — those are ROC-calendar dates, i.e. 1 April 2024, 1 June 2024 and 1 February 2026[16][17]. First line with chemotherapy, a new clause effective 1 February 2026 (my translation): "Oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma: nivolumab only, in combination with a fluoropyrimidine and cisplatin or oxaliplatin, for the first-line treatment of adult patients with advanced or metastatic oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma who are not candidates for curative treatment such as chemoradiotherapy or surgical resection. (115/2/1)"[16][18]. The PD-L1 threshold for both clauses is TC≧1%, and the shared conditions (again my translation) are: "the patient is in good general condition (ECOG≦1)", "use requires prior approval through pre-review", "each application is limited to 12 weeks", "reimbursement period limit: 2 years from the date of the first prescription", and "each patient is reimbursed for only one immune checkpoint inhibitor per indication, and switching is not permitted"[16].

Two more things that are not reimbursed. In the same biomarker table, pembrolizumab's entries for first- and second-line oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma both read "this drug is not yet reimbursed for this indication"[16] — the drug behind the KEYNOTE-590 numbers is not currently paid for by the NHI. And for situation one, adjuvant nivolumab after surgery, there is no clause anywhere in the full text of Section 9 for postoperative or adjuvant use[16]; if you meet the criteria for that situation, it is self-pay or a clinical trial — ask your hospital's pharmacy department or your case manager, and I am not going to guess at prices here.

Benefit and cost, which I insist on keeping in the same paragraph

It is no coincidence that in every situation above I wrote the adverse event rates next to the survival numbers. Immunotherapy's side effects have a different shape from chemotherapy's: it is the immune system attacking your own organs, and the lungs, liver, bowel, thyroid and pituitary can all be involved. In CheckMate 648, grade 3 to 4 treatment-related adverse events with nivolumab plus chemotherapy were 47%, higher than the 36% with chemotherapy alone[6]; once an endocrine organ has been knocked out, some people need hormone replacement for life. Which symptoms mean coming back the same day is written in "When to come back the same day"; how to judge a fever during treatment is already on the site in "The fever that cannot wait until morning". How the whole road runs after recurrence or spread, including the options other than immunotherapy, is in "After recurrence or spread, what roads remain".

Find that one line on the pathology report first, then find the PD-L1

The first sheet is the pathology report. If you have had surgery, find the "treatment response" line: whether there is residual tumour decides whether you are in situation one. The second sheet is the PD-L1 staining report: look at whether it says TC, TPS or CPS, what the number is, and which staining system was used — the NHI clause recognises TC≧1%, the trials used TPS or CPS, and the three terms do not measure quite the same thing, which is something to have your oncologist explain with the report in front of you. With those two sheets, ask three questions: which of the three situations am I in? Which side of the NHI threshold is my PD-L1 on? If this is self-pay, which trial is the evidence for this decision, and what was the hazard ratio? If the answer you get is "it's a new drug, everyone is using it", that is not an answer.

From the physician

Before asking about immunotherapy, find two reports: the "treatment response" line on the postoperative pathology, and the PD-L1 stain's number and system. Take them and ask: which situation am I in, which side of the NHI threshold is my PD-L1 on, and if this is self-pay, which trial is it based on. Working out which of the three situations you are in is more useful than asking "can I have it".
