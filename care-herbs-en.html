THE DECISIONS THAT ARE YOURS

Which supplements really do collide with your drugs

The few that collide are short: St John's wort, grapefruit, and a couple that depend on your drug — and where the antioxidant evidence stops.

Patient Education · The Decisions That Are Yours · 22 references
#Supplements#Nutrition#Decisions

This is not a ban list, and it does not tell you that combining Chinese and Western medicine works. Only a handful of things have human evidence of shaving a drug's effect. The antioxidant question has evidence too thin on both sides; that is section four, and it ends without a conclusion.

When I ask in clinic, "are you taking anything else?", most people shake their head, then three seconds later add "only a few tonics". Those three seconds are what this article is about. What you take is your decision; I only need to know, because a handful of things really will knock a treatment sideways, and I cannot check something I was never told about.

Roughly two in three do not say, and the reason at the top of the list is that the doctor never asked

A systematic review of 86 observational studies, 14 pooled in a meta-analysis: among people taking "biologically based" supplements, only 33% (95% CI 24–43%) tell a health professional, individual studies ranging 7% to 80%[1]. The reasons for not telling, in the same review, are headed not by mistrust but by the doctor not asking; then fear of disapproval, thinking it unimportant, assuming the doctor would not know, and "I thought it was safe".

Cancer patients are no different: a review of 21 studies found use running 11% to 95%, of whom 20% to 77% had not told their doctor, for the same reason[2]; a review of 30 studies and 8,721 people calls it "a gap in care with potentially serious medical consequences"[3]. In the US NHANES survey, 70.4% of 2,772 cancer survivors were using dietary supplements and 46.1% had consulted no health professional about it[4].

Those are all overseas figures. Whether anyone in Taiwan has counted how many people do not tell their doctor, I could find no citable formal data. What can be found is use: 82.4% of 106 patients in a questionnaire at the breast centre of a medical centre in southern Taiwan[5]; and 74,620 adult cancer patients in the National Health Insurance (健保) catastrophic illness database have consulted a traditional Chinese medicine practitioner[6].

The ones that genuinely knock a treatment out come to a short list

St John's wort is the one to remember. In a randomised crossover study, 5 cancer patients took it for 18 consecutive days and blood levels of SN-38, irinotecan's active metabolite, fell by 42% (95% CI 14% to 70%); myelosuppression was distinctly heavier when it was not being taken, so the drug's effect had been shaved off[7]. Five people is very few, but the direction holds: in 12 healthy volunteers, imatinib clearance rose 43% after 14 days[8]. It induces the CYP3A4 pathway and P-glycoprotein, and that reaches beyond anticancer drugs — a review of clinical observations lists ciclosporin, oral contraceptives and warfarin as also weakened, with case reports of contraception failing and an unintended pregnancy[9].

Green tea extract depends on the drug. Cell and animal work shows green tea polyphenols, EGCG in particular, react directly with bortezomib and seal off its proteasome-inhibiting function, blocking its antitumour effect[10]. That is not a human trial, so I will not say green tea makes treatment fail. I will say: on bortezomib, bring the jar of concentrated extract capsules in and ask.

Grapefruit is simpler. The drug-interaction literature explains that grapefruit juice selectively suppresses CYP3A4 in the intestinal wall, not the liver, raising absorption of certain oral drugs; the effect of one glass can last 24 hours, and repeated drinking accumulates[11]. Which is why spacing the juice a few hours from the tablet is not safe — the gap is nowhere near long enough.

Since those drug names are here, one thing unrelated to supplements that matters more the same evening. On a cytotoxic or targeted drug of the irinotecan or bortezomib kind, a single reading of 38.0°C or above on an ear or axillary thermometer, chills and shaking, sudden breathlessness or chest pain, swelling and pain in one calf, black stools or heavy bleeding all mean the emergency department the same day. On an immune checkpoint inhibitor, add: new diarrhoea, rash, cough, breathlessness, unusual tiredness or palpitations should not be handled by you as a cold or gastroenteritis — contact the team the same day. None of that is something a supplement can deal with, and none of it is the moment for "let me take something and see".

On bleeding the mechanism is plausible, and the research is startlingly thin

Ginkgo, fish oil, turmeric, garlic and ginseng are the group surgical and anaesthetic teams ask about most. A systematic review of supplements and coagulation notes that surgical patients use supplements at a significantly higher rate than the general population, and that supplements can affect coagulation both pharmacodynamically and pharmacokinetically; the authors also concede plainly that the volume of research in this area is itself very limited[12]. So what I can give is not a quantified risk but an action: say it out loud when surgery or an invasive procedure is coming, or when your platelets are low. For the "stop ginkgo seven days before surgery" figure circulating online I could find no official or guideline-level document in Taiwan. Ask the surgical or anaesthetic team; do not stop things on your own.

"Do antioxidants during chemotherapy wreck the treatment?" I have no definite answer

This has been written up as a prohibition too many times, so both sides go on the table. The worrying side is a prospective observational study attached to SWOG S0221, in 1,134 women having chemotherapy for breast cancer: those using antioxidants (vitamin A, C, E, carotenoids, coenzyme Q10) both before and during treatment had an adjusted hazard ratio for recurrence of 1.41 (95% CI 0.98 to 2.04, P=.06) and for death of 1.40 (0.90 to 2.18, P=.14)[13]. A hazard ratio of 1.41 means recurrences arrived about forty per cent faster, not that the chance of recurrence was forty per cent higher; and both intervals cross 1 — neither result reached statistical significance, and the difference could be chance.

Evidence of the same grade points the other way. The LACE cohort followed 2,264 women with early breast cancer: frequent vitamin C users had a lower recurrence risk (0.73, 0.55 to 0.97), vitamin E likewise (0.71, 0.54 to 0.94); frequent users of combination carotenoids did have raised breast cancer mortality[14]. Both are observational, both limited by the fact that people who buy supplements were never the same as people who do not.

The guidelines: the 2024 ASCO and SIO update on cancer-related fatigue (evidence base 113 randomised trials) says other dietary supplements did not improve fatigue during or after treatment — no help, not harm[15]; the 2017 SIO breast cancer integrative guideline, endorsed by ASCO in 2018, says there is no strong evidence supporting oral dietary supplements for managing the side effects of breast cancer treatment[16].

The honest position: the concern is theoretical, the evidence is too thin to conclude from, and so this is not a ban. A review of supplement use in cancer care puts the consequence plainly: a blanket "avoid all dietary supplements" only teaches patients to see the doctor as a negative, and then they stop telling you anything[17]. I would rather take them one at a time.

Chinese medicine has a formal NHI pathway in Taiwan, and it is built to be added, not swapped in

The Integrated Programme for Enhanced Traditional Chinese Medicine Care of Cancer Patients under National Health Insurance (全民健康保險中醫癌症病人加強照護整合方案) is a formal National Health Insurance Administration (健保署) programme with three sub-programmes: adjunctive Chinese medicine for inpatients under Western care, extended Chinese medicine outpatient care, and enhanced Chinese medicine outpatient care[18]. "Adjunctive" and "enhanced" are not decoration; the design puts them on top of the Western course. Nor does turning up get you in: extended care requires at least two symptoms at grade 2 or above on a CTCAE assessment form; enhanced care lists 13 cancer types and requires a Catastrophic Illness Certificate (重大傷病證明) held at the same time, within one year of surgery or during anticancer treatment. The conditions get revised — ask the Chinese medicine clinic or your case manager (個管師).

You often hear that combining Chinese and Western medicine lets you live longer. I do not write that sentence: everything available to support it is observational and cannot rule out biases of the "only people still walking and still able to afford it get as far as a Chinese medicine clinic" kind — an association is not effectiveness. What does carry guideline backing is something else: the SIO breast cancer guideline recommends acupressure and acupuncture for reducing chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting[16]. Find a qualified Chinese medicine physician, and ask the oncology team first if your platelets are low or you are neutropenic.

One more confusion. Preparations from a pharmacy or a Chinese medicine clinic hold licences, raw herbal materials have statutory heavy metal limits (arsenic 3.0, lead 5.0, cadmium 1.0, mercury 0.2 ppm or below)[19], and legality can be checked in the licence enquiry system of the Department of Chinese Medicine and Pharmacy, Ministry of Health and Welfare (衛生福利部中醫藥司)[20]. What is bought online, bought through an agent, or carried back from abroad is not inside that system. An analysis of 1,773 herbal products worldwide found 30.51% over the limit for at least one heavy metal by the Chinese Pharmacopoeia standard[21]; an audit of 26 Chinese medicine products found undeclared plant or animal DNA in 50% and undeclared pharmaceutical ingredients in 50%[22]. Neither is Taiwanese sampling, so neither gives a proportion for here, but both make one thing clear: what the label leaves out, you will not know as you swallow it.

How to write the list, and who to put it in front of

  • Bring the bottles, the whole bag, or photograph them. Describing them rarely matches up — plenty of products share a Chinese name and differ inside.

  • Include what other people gave you — the category most often missed: what a relative brought back from abroad, what came from the temple, what the person in the next bed recommended, what arrived in a festival gift box.

  • Include the things you file under "it's only food": concentrated turmeric, high-dose fish oil, green tea extract, ginseng drinks.

  • Write down when you started and how often. Whether it overlaps your course decides whether it needs dealing with.

With the list on the table, a few questions are worth saying out loud. Do any of my drugs go through CYP3A4? That decides whether grapefruit and St John's wort have to be avoided. Is surgery or an invasive procedure coming up, and should any of these stop beforehand, for how long? Given my cancer type and stage of treatment, do I meet the entry conditions for the enhanced Chinese medicine care programme?

Ordinary diet and food restrictions are not in this piece; the site covers them in "What should I eat during treatment? Any foods to avoid, or supplements?", and glutamine has its own piece in "Should I take L-glutamine?". This one wants to change a single thing: at your next visit, get the list out.

From the physician

Whether your drugs run through CYP3A4 decides if grapefruit and St John's wort have to go, and only your team can look that up. Next visit, bring the whole bag — including what other people gave you and the bottles you file under food. Say it before surgery or when platelets are low; do not stop anything on your own.
