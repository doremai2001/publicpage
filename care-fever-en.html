WHAT CANNOT WAIT

The fever that cannot wait until morning

A fever on chemotherapy is an emergency. What the threshold is, why an antipyretic is the wrong move, and what to say at the desk.

Patient Education · What Cannot Wait · 19 references
#Urgent#Chemotherapy#Side Effects

If you are running a fever right now, do not finish this article — the threshold is in the first section; read that and leave. The rest is for before it happens: know which days your counts are lowest, and keep the emergency department address and your case manager's number by the door.

There is one thing to do about a fever on chemotherapy: go to the emergency department now. Not take something for it first, not re-check the temperature to see whether it settles, not wait for morning clinic. Nothing here will teach you to watch it at home. Febrile neutropenia is an emergency, and the hard part is that for the first few hours people look fine.

One reading of 38.0°C, and you do not take a second one

The 2018 antimicrobial prophylaxis guideline from ASCO and the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) defines fever in this population in Box 1: a single oral temperature of 38.3°C or above, or 38.0°C or above sustained over one hour. Neutropenia is graded by the absolute neutrophil count (ANC — the white cells that deal with bacteria): below 1,000/µL, severe below 500/µL, profound below 100/µL[1].

At home you do not know your ANC, and you should not spend an hour proving your temperature is "sustained". A Korean group testing that guideline rewrote the definition as a tympanic or axillary reading of 38.0°C or above, at least once — because antipyretics may shorten the duration of sustained fever; they also preferred tympanic or axillary readings to oral[2]. With mucositis, an oral reading was never accurate anyway.

So keep one sentence: during chemotherapy, one reading of 38.0°C on an ear or axillary thermometer — go. Do not re-measure, do not wait for it to persist, do not wait for 38.3°C.

Why you must not take something for it first

Because your temperature is often the only visible signal, and an antipyretic flattens it. In a double-blind randomised feasibility trial, 22 haemato-oncology patients who developed febrile neutropenia and began treatment (13 paracetamol, 9 placebo) had a mean peak temperature over the first 24 hours of 38.2°C against 38.9°C — a difference of 0.78°C (95% CI −1.38 to −0.18, p=0.013)[3]. The interval in brackets is where that difference plausibly lies. The authors are careful: what antipyretics do to the outcome of febrile neutropenia has not been settled by any trial.

That does not say antipyretics will kill you; that has not been tested. It says you will see nearly a degree less than is there. At two in the morning, 37.8°C and 38.6°C produce completely different decisions — and 0.8 degrees can be one tablet.

The same goes for antibiotics left in the cupboard. At National Taiwan University Hospital, 1996 to 2001, 738 isolates from the bloodstream of febrile neutropenic patients with haematological malignancy were 57% Gram-negative, and 33% of the E. coli were ciprofloxacin-resistant[4]. Those data are over twenty years old and cannot stand for today's proportions, but the direction is clear: the tablet you swallow at home may not treat the thing, and will make every judgement after it harder.

"Antibiotics within the hour" is softer than you think — and it changes nothing about what you do

The rule is firm. The 2018 ASCO/IDSA guideline on outpatient management says patients with febrile neutropenia should receive the first dose of empirical antibacterial therapy within one hour of triage, and be monitored for at least four hours[5].

The evidence is not. The strongest single study behind "every hour counts" covers 307 febrile episodes in 169 adults at one Brazilian centre: each extra hour before antibiotics carried a hazard ratio of 1.18 for 28-day mortality (a hazard ratio compares event rates in two groups, 1 meaning the same rate; 95% CI 1.10–1.26), and mortality at 28 days was 3.0% within 30 minutes against 18.1% at 31 to 60 minutes[6]. One centre, three hundred-odd episodes.

The data pointing the other way are larger. Across 3,219 adult admissions for febrile neutropenia at the Mayo Clinic, 2006 to 2016, delays in time to antibiotic of up to three hours did not affect outcomes[7]. In 244 febrile events in haematology patients, median time to antibiotics was 120 minutes in the episodes that ended badly and 102 minutes in those that did not (p=0.93)[8].

So, honestly: one hour is a guideline consensus, not the conclusion of a randomised trial — nobody will randomise patients to later antibiotics. It changes nothing for you: that whole argument is about patients already at the hospital. Not one study supports waiting at home. And this is not a small illness: among 41,779 adult cancer patients at 115 US medical centres, in-hospital mortality was 9.5% — 2.6% with no major comorbidity, 10.3% with one, 21.4% or higher with more than one[9].

What to say at the emergency department desk

Say this: "I am on chemotherapy and I have a fever." Hand over the date of your last treatment and your case manager's (個管師) card. I could not find a nationwide Taiwanese rule giving cancer patients with fever priority at triage, so I will not promise you will be called first; but that sentence changes your triage category and the first tests ordered.

A recent vaccination does not change any of this — you still go, and you tell the doctor the date and which vaccine. Do not attribute the fever to it yourself. Reactions after vaccination are common, but that judgement is not made at home; "A fever the night after a vaccine" has the full version.

Fever is also not the only presentation. The IDSA's 2011 guideline on antimicrobial agents in neutropenic patients with cancer stratifies risk by presenting signs and symptoms, and stresses that clinical vigilance and immediate treatment are the keys to this care[10]. Shaking chills, sudden weakness that stops you standing, confusion, breathlessness, a blood pressure that drops — on chemotherapy those are the same emergency with no fever at all.

The scores used in the emergency department are not for you. The MASCC risk index, built on 756 patients, marks low risk at 21 or above: positive predictive value 91%, sensitivity 71%[11]. But pooled across 26 studies and 6,617 patients, "below 21" catches those who go on to serious complications with a sensitivity of only 55.6%[12] — nearly half of those who run into trouble are missed — and in a validation study of 227 Chinese patients, 12.5% of the low-risk group still had complications and 1.9% still died[13]. That score is for a physician who has already assessed you, deciding whether you can go home on tablets.

If you are having radiotherapy alone, this article weighs differently for you

This section is for a different group; I do not want to frighten you along with everybody else. JCOG0301, run by the Japan Clinical Oncology Group, randomised 200 elderly patients with unresectable stage III non-small-cell lung cancer to chemoradiotherapy or radiotherapy alone, 60 Gy. Grade 3–4 neutropenia: 57.3% in the chemoradiotherapy arm, none in the radiotherapy arm; leucopenia 63.5% against none; grade 3 infection 12.5% against 4.1%[14].

In that trial, thoracic radiotherapy alone produced grade 3–4 neutropenia in not one patient. With a fever you should still phone the team and be assessed, but usually this is not the same urgency. Three caveats stay attached. Anyone receiving concurrent chemotherapy belongs in the chemotherapy group, not this one. Anyone whose field covers a wide volume of marrow — a large pelvic volume, say, or the spine — can still see their counts fall. And that is one trial, one field, one group of lung cancer patients: whether your own field suppresses your counts is a question for your own physician, and this passage is not a reason to relax.

When is it most dangerous, and a number that concerns patients in Taiwan

There is no safe window. In 75 patients with advanced lung cancer on platinum-containing regimens, the median day of the ANC nadir was day 12 after the first day of chemotherapy[15]. A small study in one cancer type: an order of magnitude, somewhere around the second week, not a calendar you can rely on. A fever on any day after treatment gets the same response.

A retrospective chart review across China, Hong Kong, Japan, Taiwan, Italy and the USA covered 227 breast cancer patients on adjuvant docetaxel and cyclophosphamide: grade 3 or worse neutropenia in 30.7% of the Asian patients against 4.0% of the Caucasian ones (p<0.001), with much the same proportion in each group on prophylactic growth factor (26.0% against 28.0%)[16]. A retrospective count of one drug combination, not generalisable to all chemotherapy — but applying Western incidence figures to yourself may be an underestimate.

Three things worth settling at your next appointment

First: roughly what is the febrile neutropenia risk of my regimen, and should I have prophylactic granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF)? ASCO's white blood cell growth factor guideline puts the threshold at a regimen risk of approximately 20% or higher, weighed with age, history, disease characteristics and myelotoxicity[17]. It is not a shield: across 17 randomised trials and 3,493 adults it cut febrile neutropenia by 46% (relative risk 0.54), at the price of bone and musculoskeletal pain rising from 10.4% to 19.6%[18]. National Health Insurance (健保) runs on different logic: rule 4.1.2 is written in terms of your own past blood counts, so usually they must fall below the threshold once before the next cycle is covered[19]. "Should I have it" and "will it be paid for" are two questions.

Second: which emergency department do I go to in the middle of the night? If the treating hospital is far away, ask whether somewhere nearer will do, whether to phone first, and whether you can carry a record summary.

Third: roughly which day after treatment is the nadir for my regimen? Not to make you anxious, but so you measure more often over those days. And remember: there is no safe window; 38.0°C on any day is handled the same way.

Tonight, write the emergency department address, your case manager's number and the date of your last treatment on one sheet of paper, and stick it by the door. When it happens you will not want to be looking things up.

From the physician

If you are having radiotherapy alone with no concurrent chemotherapy, this article weighs differently for you; if your field covers a wide volume of marrow, ask your own physician. Find out at your next visit which group your treatment is in, and roughly which day after treatment the nadir falls — write it on the same sheet as the emergency department address.
