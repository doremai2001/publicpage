HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

After chemoradiotherapy, do you still need surgery

Of people whose imaging and biopsies are both clean, about two-thirds still have residual tumour in the oesophagus; what the active surveillance trials have read out and what is still missing, with the evidence for both directions laid side by side.

Oesophageal Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 31 references
#Decisions#Surgery#Organ Preservation#Evidence

This article will displease people on both sides: those hoping to hear "a good response means no operation" will read the numbers on missed disease; those convinced "no operation means giving up" will read the randomised trials in which adding surgery did not add survival. I attach the literature for both sides so you can take it for a second opinion.

Chemoradiotherapy is finished, the endoscopic biopsies show no cancer cells, the PET is clean too — and this is when patients ask me most often: "So do I still need the operation?" Conclusion first: our clinic still recommends operating, and below I explain why with numbers; how far "the road without surgery" has got is covered as well, because it is being tested. Both directions have to be guarded: a patient whose imaging is clean but who still has tumour, not operated on, is an irreversible error; reading definitive chemoradiotherapy as a second-rate option is an error too.

My position first: concurrent chemoradiotherapy and proton therapy for oesophageal cancer are treatments I deliver myself, every week; the other side of every comparison in this topic is my thoracic-surgery colleagues' operation and my gastroenterology colleagues' endoscopy. That is why every comparison comes with its primary source, so you can take it to the surgical and gastroenterology clinics for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

Chemoradiotherapy then surgery beats surgery alone: this is the ground the "operate" advice stands on

Two randomised trials settled this. The Dutch CROSS trial (366 patients, 75% adenocarcinoma, 23% squamous cell carcinoma): preoperative chemoradiotherapy plus surgery against surgery alone, median overall survival 49.4 against 24.0 months, in-hospital mortality 4% in both arms[1]; ten-year overall survival 38% against 25%[2]; the effect was especially large in the SCC subgroup, median survival 81.6 against 21.1 months, hazard ratio 0.48[3] — a hazard ratio divides the rate at which deaths occur in one group by the rate in the other, and 0.48 means deaths in the chemoradiotherapy-first group occurred at only half the rate. China's NEOCRTEC5010 (451 thoracic SCC patients): pathological complete response 43.2%[4], five-year overall survival 59.9% against 49.1%[5]. These two trials prove that "chemoradiotherapy plus surgery" beats "surgery alone"; neither had an arm of "chemoradiotherapy without surgery", so they cannot answer today's question. A footnote: China's CMISG1701 compared preoperative chemoradiotherapy with preoperative chemotherapy, both sides operated on; three-year survival 64.1% against 54.9%, no difference, yet pathological complete response 27.7% against 2.9%[6] — the extra pathological response from chemoradiotherapy did not convert into a survival difference.

"No tumour visible" is not "no tumour": how much biopsy and imaging miss

The Dutch preSANO study (207 patients, two assessments after the CROSS regimen and then surgery), with a target of "more than 10% residual tumour in the resected specimen": ordinary endoscopic biopsies plus fine-needle aspiration missed 31%; switching to bite-on-bite biopsies (two bites at the same spot, reaching into the submucosa) plus fine-needle aspiration, 10% missed; endoscopic ultrasound measuring wall thickness missed 28%; PET missed 15%[7]. Part of the missed tumour was hiding under normal-looking mucosa[8]. The squamous version, preSINO (China, Hong Kong, Taiwan; 309 patients; Linkou Chang Gung took part): the same combination had a false-negative rate of 13.5% for "more than 10% residual or node-positive"; for detecting any residual disease the negative predictive value was only 68.7% — of the people the biopsies said were clear, one in three actually had disease[9]. The hardest number comes from SANO itself: of the 101 patients judged a "clinical complete response" who went straight to surgery, only 36, 35.6%, had no tumour at all (pT0); critics working from the appendix found that 38 of them had residual disease as deep as T2 or T3[10]. The SANO authors replied that preSANO's 90% is a detection rate for "more than 10% residual" and cannot be compared directly with a pT0 proportion[11]. The clarification stands, but the conclusion does not change: of people whose imaging and biopsies are both clean, about two-thirds still have cancer cells in the oesophagus.

SANO read out two-year non-inferiority, and it has to be read with its labels on

Only one randomised trial has been completed and published: the Dutch SANO. By design, patients were assessed twice after chemoradiotherapy, at 4 to 6 weeks and again at around 12 weeks (PET, endoscopic biopsies, ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration), and only if both were clear did they count as a clinical complete response; the surveillance group was reassessed at intervals and operated on only when the tumour regrew locally without distant metastasis[12]. The result (Lancet Oncology 2025): 309 clinical complete responders (198 surveillance, 111 surgery), two-year overall survival 74% against 71%, meeting the trial's pre-set margin of "not worse than surgery by more than 15 percentage points" — this is called non-inferiority, and what it proves is "not much worse", not "as good"; median follow-up 38 months[13]. Four labels have to go on it. Three-quarters of its population was adenocarcinoma; SCC was only just over 20%[10]. Median overall survival was 43 against 53 months, hazard ratio 1.14 (95% confidence interval 0.74 to 1.78), no statistical difference but the direction favoured surgery; in the surveillance group 48% had local regrowth, 17% distant metastasis and 35% maintained a complete response[14], and at a median of 54 months only 25% still did[15]. The authors themselves wrote in 2026 that the distant metastasis rate was "a non-significant difference, in the direction of standard surgery", and that "a meaningful difference emerging with longer follow-up, and then showing up in overall survival, cannot be ruled out"[11]. A decision model built on SANO data (JAMA Surgery 2026): at five years the surgery group's quality-adjusted life years (years lived, discounted by quality of life) were 1.74 against 1.34, and surveillance was slightly ahead only at two years[16] — this is a model, not a trial.

The benefits on the surveillance side are real: at six months swallowing, fatigue and physical functioning were all better, and the swallowing advantage held to two years[17]; the risk of delayed surgery was comparable to immediate surgery[13]; and 46% never had an oesophagectomy[11].

Why our clinic still recommends surgery, and what the world is testing

The reasons our clinic recommends surgery: two-thirds of clinical complete responders have residual disease when operated on[10]; the negative predictive value of biopsies in SCC is 68.7%[9]; what SANO read out is two-year non-inferiority, in a mainly adenocarcinoma population, with the distant metastasis direction unfavourable and no five-year results[13][11]; and as of 2026, neither ESMO's 2025 interim update nor the Japan Esophageal Society's 2022 guideline has a box for "active surveillance after clinical complete response"[18][19]. This is my judgement as a radiation oncologist after reading the data, not the surgeons' position.

On the other side, this road is being tested. The Swedish-led NEEDS trial, restricted to SCC, compares "chemoradiotherapy plus planned surgery" with "definitive chemoradiotherapy plus surveillance, with salvage surgery as needed", non-inferiority margin 7.5%[20]; it is still recruiting, target 1,020 patients, estimated primary completion end of 2026, full completion estimated 2031[21]. France's ESOSTRATE was terminated for slow recruitment, having enrolled only 188, with no results published[22]. Japan's JCOG0909 gives the prospective numbers for the third road: 94 cStage II/III SCC patients, 50.4 Gy chemoradiotherapy, active salvage for residual or recurrent disease, three-year overall survival 74.2%[23]; in the final analysis, five-year overall survival 64.5%, oesophagus preserved in 54.9%, about a quarter (27% in the original paper[23]) went on to salvage surgery, 1 operative death; on this basis the Japanese guideline lists it as one of the effective options for patients who do not want a surgery-led approach[19]. Single-arm, no control: it proves this road can be walked; it does not prove the road equals or beats surgery.

The reverse: definitive chemoradiotherapy is not "the second-rate option for people who can't be operated on"

Two older randomised trials both enrolled locally advanced SCC. Germany's Stahl 2005 (172 patients): chemoradiotherapy then surgery, against chemoradiotherapy taken to a higher dose — overall survival equivalent; the surgery group's two-year local progression-free survival was better, 64.3% against 40.7%, but treatment-related mortality was 12.8% against 3.5%[24]. France's FFCD 9102 (259 patients, 88.8% SCC) randomised only those who "responded to chemoradiotherapy": two-year overall survival 34% against 40%, no difference; the surgery group had better local control and fewer needed a stent (5% against 32%); but death within three months was 9.3% against 0.8%[25]. Cochrane's 2017 pooling of the two trials (431 patients, 93% SCC): adding surgery made "little or no difference" to overall survival, hazard ratio 0.99 (0.79 to 1.24, high-quality evidence); local recurrence was reduced; treatment-related mortality was five times higher in the surgery arm (relative risk 5.11)[26]. Guideline wording, as written: the footnote to ESMO's 2025 algorithm says "Evidence suggests that neoadjuvant CRT followed by surgery and definitive CRT is equally effective with regard to OS", "For patients not willing to undergo oesophageal surgery or who are medically unfit for major surgery, definitive CRT should be preferred", and "Even many experienced centres prefer definitive CRT for oesophageal tumours with a very proximal/cervical location"[18]; ASCO 2020 writes, for SCC, chemoradiotherapy plus surgery "or" chemoradiotherapy without surgery, and that both should be offered[27]. For people medically unfit for major surgery and those who clearly decline it, definitive chemoradiotherapy is the road the guideline says "should be preferred"; for cervical oesophageal cancer, the guideline acknowledges that "even many experienced centres prefer" definitive chemoradiotherapy — neither is a consolation prize.

These two trials used pre-2000 response assessment, and they do not cover non-responders. They prove that "in responding SCC, adding surgery does not add survival and adds operative deaths"; they do not prove that today's tools can accurately pick out who has responded.

The Taiwanese data favour surgery, but who was sent to chemoradiotherapy has to be said

Five-year overall survival after matching, in 7,637 SCC patients in the registry from 2008 to 2016: definitive chemoradiotherapy 19.8%, preoperative chemoradiotherapy plus surgery 31.2%, surgery alone 30.5%[28]. That is a long way from Cochrane's hazard ratio of 0.99, and the direction has to be spelled out: in the Taiwanese data 54% of patients went down the definitive chemoradiotherapy road, and this group absorbed the patients who were less fit, whose tumour sat in an unfavourable position, and who declined surgery; matching can only level the variables that get measured, and everything unmeasured is charged to chemoradiotherapy. What these data can say is "the people sent to chemoradiotherapy did worse"; they cannot say "chemoradiotherapy is worse than surgery".

The operative mortality is real, and salvage surgery's is higher

The operative mortality is real: in a registry of high-volume centres, 90-day mortality after oesophagectomy was 4.5%[29] (the figures and risk factors are in "What surgery removes, and where the stomach goes"). Salvage surgery costs more: in-hospital mortality about 10%[30], and people who go to salvage because the tumour never cleared after chemoradiotherapy do worse than those in whom it cleared and then grew back[31] (the three datasets are laid side by side in "After recurrence or spread, what roads remain"). "Don't operate now, operate if something happens" is not a cost-free option.

Do not decide until all three specialties have spoken over your films

This decision has to wait until the surgeon, the gastroenterologist and the radiation oncologist have each spoken with your imaging and your biopsies in front of them; settling it in one specialty's clinic, whichever way it goes, is too early. Take these with you — the SANO main paper[13], the Cochrane meta-analysis[26], preSANO[7], preSINO[9] and the Japan Esophageal Society guideline[19]; the links are all at the end. In the clinic, ask first: "Was my response assessed with ordinary biopsies, or with bite-on-bite biopsies plus fine-needle aspiration?" If you are considering surgery, follow up with the 90-day mortality for someone of your age and fitness; if you are considering not operating, follow up with how the follow-up would be scheduled and the conditions for salvage surgery if the tumour regrows locally. Taiwan currently has no data on active surveillance after clinical complete response that I can cite; when there are, this article will be updated.

From the physician

Do not make this decision in a single specialty's clinic. Take the five papers at the end and have the surgeon, the gastroenterologist and the radiation oncologist each speak over your biopsies and imaging; and ask which biopsy method was used to assess your response. If you are considering not operating, know first how follow-up would be scheduled and the conditions and risks of salvage surgery.
