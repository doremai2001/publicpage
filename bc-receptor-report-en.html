What ER, PR and HER2 are telling you

Taking the receptor lines apart box by box: where the 1% threshold came from, why HER2 has to be tested again, and why a Ki-67 from one hospital cannot be held against another's.

Breast Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 12 references
#Staging#Biomarkers#Getting Ready

ER-positive covers the whole stretch from 1% to 100%, and if the report says "ER Low Positive", that is a box the guideline deliberately kept. As for the HER2-low so many people ask about, in 2026 there is still no such box on the pathology report — it is a category the treatment side uses.

When the pathology report lands in your hands, most people go looking for numbers first: how many centimetres, how many nodes. But what actually decides which tablets you take and which injections you have for the next several years is the three lines of letters in the middle — ER, PR, HER2. This article takes those three lines apart box by box, along with Ki-67 and grade: what each one measures, where its reading threshold came from, and which boxes are less solid than you think.

ER counts as positive from 1%, and that threshold is written down

ER is the oestrogen receptor. The 2020 ASCO and College of American Pathologists guideline on oestrogen and progesterone receptor testing puts it bluntly: ER is read on validated immunohistochemistry, that is the standard method for predicting who will benefit from endocrine therapy, and the guideline recommends no other assay to replace it; nuclear staining in 1% to 100% of tumour cells is reported as ER-positive, and below 1% or 0% as ER-negative[1]. PR is the progesterone receptor, and the same guideline puts its main use in the prognosis of ER-positive cancers; for ductal carcinoma in situ it recommends ER testing to assess the benefit of endocrine therapy in reducing future breast cancer risk, with PR optional[1].

So the words "ER-positive" cover everything from 1% to 100%. It is not a switch. It is a very long spectrum.

If the report says "ER Low Positive", that is a box the guideline deliberately kept

The same guideline concedes something: for cancers staining 1% to 10%, the data on how much benefit endocrine therapy gives are limited. It therefore created a separate reporting category, "ER Low Positive", with a recommended comment attached, and it requires that cases staining 0% to 10% be reported together with the status of the controls, with laboratories holding a standard operating procedure for confirming or adjudicating such results[1]. When a guideline sets aside a separate box for one result, it usually means the evidence under that box has not yet settled.

The molecular data point the same way. In 465 primary breast cancers compared by immunohistochemistry and gene array, among the three ER-positive groups the proportion positive for ESR1 messenger RNA was 24% in the 1% to 9% group (25 cases), 67% in the exactly 10% group (6 cases) and 92% in the group above 10% (251 cases) — the 1% to 9% group at only 24%, close to the ER-negative group; within that group 48% were basal-like and only 8% luminal B, and overall survival sat between the ER-positive and ER-negative groups[2]. The authors' conclusion is that most 1% to 9% tumours look molecularly more like ER-negative disease.

"Then can I stop worrying about the anti-hormone tablets?" — the most dangerous sentence here

Two sets of data have to be read in the same paragraph. In 5,930 patients operated on for invasive breast cancer at a single Korean institution, median follow-up 80.9 months, ER-low (1% to 10%) accounted for only 2.0%, that is 117 people; five-year disease-free and overall survival were 94.0% and 98.6% for ER high-positive, HER2-negative disease, 85.7% and 92.1% for ER-low, HER2-negative disease, and 81.3% and 90.1% for triple-negative disease[3]. Disease-free survival counts the stretch from surgery to recurrence, a new primary cancer or death, whichever arrives first. On the face of it, ER-low does sit closer to the triple-negative end.

But among 7,018 high-risk patients in the US National Cancer Database with stage I to III, ER 1% to 10% disease who received chemotherapy, median follow-up 3 years and 586 deaths: at 12 months 42% had still not started endocrine therapy, and in multivariable analysis omitting endocrine therapy was associated with a higher risk of death, hazard ratio 1.23, 95% confidence interval 1.04 to 1.46; for ER 6% to 10% the hazard ratio was 1.42 (1.00 to 2.02), and for ER 1% to 5% it did not reach significance (1.15, 0.91 to 1.45)[4]. A hazard ratio of 1.23 means deaths were occurring in that group at roughly two-tenths above the rate in the other; it does not mean the mortality rates differ by 23 percentage points. These are observational data, and no prospective trial answers this question. So ER-low is a result to take into clinic and discuss, not a conclusion you can draw on your own.

When HER2 is sent back for a second test, that is usually the process working

The 2018 ASCO and College of American Pathologists HER2 testing guideline defines immunohistochemistry 2+ as more than 10% of the invasive tumour cells showing weak to moderate complete membrane staining. If the initial HER2 result on the core biopsy is negative, then under specified clinical conditions it "may" — not "must" — be repeated on the surgical specimen. Dual-probe in situ hybridisation also has several uncommon patterns making up about 5% of cases: a ratio of 2.0 or more with an average copy number below 4.0; a ratio below 2.0 with a copy number of 6.0 or more; and a ratio below 2.0 with a copy number between 4.0 and 6.0. All three have to be taken back to the immunohistochemistry and read alongside it before a final positive or negative call can be made[5].

The Taiwanese provision matches that design: NHI item 12195B, in situ hybridisation, is limited to breast cancers with a HER2 immunohistochemistry score of 2+, and states that it is an adjunct to the immunohistochemistry result and must not be used on its own[6]. So the HER2 box arriving a few days later is a sequence the process laid out.

There is no "HER2-low" box on the report, and in 2026 there still isn't

I am asked about this more and more often. The 2023 ASCO and College of American Pathologists update ran a systematic review, retrieved 173 abstracts and reviewed 5, found no signal that constituted grounds for changing the recommendations, and therefore confirmed the 2018 recommendations. The update acknowledges that new drugs are aimed at tumours that are immunohistochemistry 1+ or 2+ without amplification, but it states in writing that establishing new result categories such as HER2-Low or HER2-Ultra-Low now would be premature, and that current data do not support treating immunohistochemistry 0 and 1+ as a new prognostic or predictive threshold. What the update genuinely added was a reporting comment noting that the distinction between 0 and 1+ now has clinical significance[7]. On the College of American Pathologists' current guidelines page, the only breast items are still the 2020 ER/PR version and the 2023 HER2 version[8].

And the line between 0 and 1+ is itself hard to draw. A College of American Pathologists proficiency testing survey found that in 19% of cases interlaboratory concordance on 0 versus 1+ was 70% or lower; at Yale, 18 pathologists scoring 170 slides on the four-category scale agreed on 0 versus 1+ only 26% of the time, against 58% for 2+ versus 3+[9]. HER2-low is a category the treatment side uses; it is not a grade on the pathology report — and why that line came to matter again is written up in "After metastasis, genomic testing is about finding a drug".

The trouble with Ki-67 is the Ki-67 number itself

The 2021 consensus of the International Ki67 in Breast Cancer Working Group opens with the words "because of the questionable analytical validity, its value for treatment decisions is limited". It accepts that Ki-67 immunohistochemistry has clinical validity as a prognostic marker, but concludes that clinical utility is clear only in anatomically favourable ER-positive, HER2-negative patients, for identifying those who do not need adjuvant chemotherapy; and in the T1-2, N0-1 group, the consensus is that Ki-67 of 5% or less, or 30% or more, can be used to estimate prognosis — which implies that the whole stretch in between cannot[10].

Why it cannot has a quantified answer. Thirty European pathology laboratories stained the same set of tissue microarrays using their own in-house processes, with 70 paired samples read centrally by one observer: the median Ki-67 labelling index by laboratory ranged from 0.65% to 33.0%, and the proportion of the same batch of tumours classified as luminal A ranged from 17% to 57%, both with P values below 0.0001; the differences persisted even among laboratories using the same antibody[11]. Same tumours, different laboratory, different classification. So taking hospital A's Ki-67 and holding it against hospital B's threshold is something you cannot do.

The grade line is three separate scores added together

Histological grade is not an impression. The Nottingham method scores three morphological features semi-quantitatively and adds them up: the percentage of tubule formation, the degree of nuclear pleomorphism, and the mitotic count over a defined field area; the total is then divided into three grades. Among 1,831 patients with primary operable breast cancer recruited from 1973 with a gradable tumour, survival in grade 1 was significantly better than in grades 2 and 3, P below 0.0001[12]. Along with tumour size and nodal status, it is one of the few lines on the report that genuinely enters the decision.

At the next visit, ask for these four boxes to be read out one by one

The report is long, but there is not much to ask about. Turn it to the immunohistochemistry page and ask for each item to be read aloud:

  • What is the ER percentage. If it falls between 1% and 10%, ask straight out: "Does this box count as ER Low Positive, and what would you advise about endocrine therapy?"

  • What is the HER2 score. If it is 2+, ask when the in situ hybridisation is scheduled and whether the result would change the plan.

  • Which laboratory did the Ki-67, and what is that laboratory's reference range. Asking that out loud is worth more than memorising a percentage.

  • What is the grade, and what did each of the three components score.

Only once those boxes are assembled does "so what happens next" have anything to stand on. How subtype divides the whole route is in "Three breast cancers, three different roads"; which lines of the report really change treatment and which do not is in "Which lines of the report actually change treatment".

From the physician

The same report can produce very different Ki-67 values in different laboratories, so please do not hold someone else's number against your own. At the next visit, ask your doctor to read out the ER percentage, the HER2 score, the laboratory and reference range for the Ki-67, and the three component scores behind the grade, one item at a time, and write them down.
