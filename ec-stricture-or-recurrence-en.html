Swallowing worse again: stricture or recurrence

How common anastomotic stricture after surgery and stricture after chemoradiotherapy are, and how many dilatations they take; how common recurrence is, and how to tell them apart — the conclusion being that neither side can be guessed, only biopsied.

Oesophageal Cancer Guide · After Treatment · 17 references
#Swallowing#Recurrence#Endoscopy#Follow-up

Forty per cent of patients with a neck anastomosis will need dilatation, and 40 to 60% narrow after chemoradiotherapy too; but over the same period, people whose disease was never cleared or has recurred are not few either. This article is not about which is more likely; it is about which symptoms a stricture will not give you, and what endoscopy and PET can each answer.

"Doctor, food is getting stuck again." Whoever says this a few months after surgery or chemoradiotherapy is thinking the same thing: has it come back? My answer in clinic is always the same: don't guess yet. Swallowing gets worse for two broad reasons — the join or the irradiated segment has scarred and narrowed, or the tumour was never cleared or has grown back — both are common, the symptoms are almost identical, and the only thing that separates them is endoscopy with biopsy. So I am not going to answer you with "it's probably nothing" or "it's probably recurrence". How common each side is, read on.

Stuck again two or three months after surgery: look at the anastomosis first

The place where the oesophagus is joined to the gastric conduit is the anastomosis, and it scars and narrows. Prospective data from Amsterdam on 607 oesophagectomy patients with a neck anastomosis: 41.7% developed a benign stricture needing dilatation, at a median of 74 days after surgery, with a median of 5 dilatations; refractory strictures needing more than 10 dilatations were associated with onset within 90 days, prior chemoradiotherapy and a previous leak[1]. Forty per cent is not a small number, and it is not over after one dilatation. Who is prone to stricture? A meta-analysis of 14 studies and 5,987 patients: anastomotic leak, odds ratio 2.75 (an odds ratio divides one group's odds of the event — occurrences against non-occurrences — by the other's, and is not a multiple of probability), cardiovascular disease 1.62, diabetes 1.62; preoperative chemoradiotherapy or chemotherapy (odds ratio 0.78) was not associated with stricture[2]. In other words, the biggest factor is "did it leak", not "did you have chemoradiotherapy first". Why does a neck anastomosis narrow so often? Because it leaks more: in the Dutch randomised trial, the leak rate for a neck anastomosis was nearly three times that of an intrathoracic one[3] (the numbers are in "What surgery removes, and where the stomach goes") — more leaks, more strictures. The way the join is made matters too: in a meta-analysis of 12 randomised trials, circular stapling produced about two-thirds more strictures than hand-sewing (relative risk 1.67), with no difference in leaks[4]; a network meta-analysis showed linear stapling had the fewest strictures (odds ratio 0.32 against hand-sewn)[5]. This is my surgical colleagues' territory; I raise it so you know that the words "neck anastomosis, postoperative leak" on your operation record amount to a forecast that dilatation is quite likely later. Dilatation itself carries a risk of perforation; the gastroenterologist doing it will explain the details.

People who had no surgery, only chemoradiotherapy, narrow too

While radiation is making the tumour disappear, the oesophageal wall fibroses. Twenty years of data from New Zealand on 77 squamous cell carcinoma patients (60 chemoradiotherapy, 17 radiotherapy alone): stricture in 58%, 32% had been dilated and 18% had had a stent[6]. In a Japanese single-centre series of 93 T2 to T4 squamous cell carcinoma patients with a complete response after definitive chemoradiotherapy, clinically significant stricture occurred in 44.1%; the predictors were an endoscope that could not pass before treatment (odds ratio 18.3), a longer tumour and a higher proportion of the lumen obstructed — not the chemotherapy regimen or the radiation dose[7]; data from Kyushu likewise point to T3 to T4, circumferential lesions and a thick wall as having more frequent and more severe strictures[8]. Timing: in an Indian cohort of 79 patients, stricture developed at a median of 4.5 months after completing treatment[9]. So if swallowing gets worse within six months of finishing chemoradiotherapy, stricture is a real possibility; but the next section tells you why, even so, I still biopsy.

How common "never cleared" and "grew back" are after chemoradiotherapy

The unwelcome part first. Long-term follow-up of RTOG 85-01 (T1 to T3 squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma, chemoradiotherapy to 50 Gy with cisplatin/5-FU): five-year overall survival in the chemoradiotherapy arm was 26%, and the most common mode of failure was "persistent disease" — in 26% of patients the tumour never cleared at all[10]. INT 0123 compared 50.4 Gy with 64.8 Gy: locoregional failure plus local persistence 52% against 56% — the higher dose was no better[11] (dose is discussed in "Radiation dose: why higher is not better"). On the Japanese JCOG0909 road, which wrote salvage surgery into the treatment plan, about 40% of patients ended up needing salvage[12] (the full figures are in "After chemoradiotherapy, do you still need surgery") — a "complete response" after chemoradiotherapy cannot be read as "cured". This is exactly why imaging that "looks clean" at the end of chemoradiotherapy is not the moment to relax; the complete discussion of that road is in "After chemoradiotherapy, do you still need surgery".

Endoscopy, biopsy, CT, PET: what each one can answer

For patients with a complete response after definitive chemoradiotherapy, the ESMO 2022 oesophageal cancer guideline recommends endoscopy with biopsy plus CT every three months, the aim being to catch a recurrence early enough for salvage surgery (evidence level IV, B — level IV means retrospective cohort or case-control studies)[13]. Diagnosing recurrence needs biopsies from several sites — the guideline says at least six[13]. A stricture often carries a radiation ulcer and can coexist with recurrence, so one negative biopsy does not mean there is nothing there — which is the logic behind the guideline's every-three-months repetition. And PET? A meta-analysis of 8 studies and 486 patients: pooled sensitivity for detecting recurrence 96%, specificity 78%[14]. In plain language: PET almost never misses, but some of its positives are inflammation or radiation reaction, so a PET that lights up still has to be proven by biopsy; PET cannot replace the biopsy. Tumour markers (SCC-Ag, CEA) rising a little does not mean recurrence, and not rising does not mean all is well — they miss 60% of recurrences and give 80% false alarms[15]; the numbers are in "How follow-up is scheduled, and the second cancer people forget". What decides whether to do an endoscopy is the symptom, not that number.

The symptoms a stricture will not give you

The typical stricture is solids sticking, slowly getting worse, with water still going down. There are a few symptoms a stricture will not give you, and if they appear you should come in early. Data from the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences on 193 squamous cell carcinoma patients with recurrence after surgery: nodal recurrence was most often supraclavicular (25.8%) and mediastinal (44.4%, of which upper mediastinal 36.4%)[16] — upper mediastinal and supraclavicular nodes press on the recurrent laryngeal nerve, and the voice suddenly goes hoarse; pressing on the trachea gives a new, persistent cough. The ESMO guideline lists the situations that call for an upper endoscopy: new dysphagia, gastrointestinal bleeding, recurrent aspiration (choking on water), vomiting, weight loss or loss of appetite[13]. As for timing, about 90% of recurrences occur within two years[13], and after the sixth year they are rare[17] (the curve is in "How follow-up is scheduled, and the second cancer people forget"); so "it's been two years since my operation" is not a reason to ignore a new symptom — only a reason the odds are lower. Swallowing suddenly and completely blocked, choking on water, sudden severe chest or back pain with fever — these are not for the next appointment, they are for today; see "When to come back the same day".

Before the appointment, have these three answers ready

Think through three things before you come to clinic, and you will save us a lot of wasted steps. One, what gets stuck: solids, porridge, or water? When did it start, and how much worse has it got over how many weeks? Two, any signals beyond a stricture: a changed voice, a new cough, how many kilograms lost, any black stools or vomiting blood? Three, your treatment record: neck or intrathoracic anastomosis, any leak after surgery, whether the endoscope could pass before chemoradiotherapy — these three pieces of information directly shape our judgement of how likely a stricture is. Then accept one conclusion: whichever side we guess, the next step is endoscopy with biopsy. Placement of a metal stent has a procedure item in the NHI fee schedule (the clause is quoted in "Can't swallow, weight falling: a feeding tube first?"); whether the stent itself is reimbursed as a special material, and how the number of dilatations is counted — I could not find a clause I could cite, so ask the medical affairs office. What roads remain once a recurrence is confirmed is in "After recurrence or spread, what roads remain".

From the physician

When swallowing gets worse again, the last thing I want to see is a patient deciding for himself "it's probably just a narrowing" and letting two months go by. Whichever side we guess, the next step is endoscopy with biopsy; before the visit, write down what gets stuck, since when, and whether there has been hoarseness or black stools, and bring your operation record.
