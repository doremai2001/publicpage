IS LETTING GO EASIER

The last few weeks: what actually happens

Eight indications for an emergency visit are written into the NHI home hospice schedule. That list is the "when to phone" list.

Situation Guides · Is Letting Go Easier · 11 references
#End of life#Family#Palliative care#How to ask

One phone call is worth making today rather than in those days: the process after a death at home — who issues the certificate, what the family does. I obtained no official document, so I describe no process; ask your home hospice team. Several other questions here I could not answer either, and each is marked.

This one is written for the family. Some people will want to read it first, some will want to skip it. Either is fine. I do not write dramatic detail and I do not describe the final moment. There are several things here I could not find a citable source for, and where that is so I say so plainly.

What you will see, and what it means

Two prospective studies recorded the same group of 357 patients with advanced cancer, admitted to acute palliative care units at two cancer centres, one in the United States and one in Brazil, with researchers recording physical signs every twelve hours; 203 of them died during the admission[1]. The things a family will see, and will recognise, are roughly these:

  • Sleeping more and more, rousable but soon asleep again, in bed almost all day and needing full assistance; later, less response to voices as well[1][2].

  • Swallowing becoming difficult, choking on fluids. This one appears relatively early, and what it means in practice is: do not force feeds[1].

  • Less urine, and darker; a changed breathing pattern, uneven in depth, with pauses and then resumption; a rattling sound in the throat; hands and feet turning cool, fingertips and lips darkening. These appear relatively late[1].

Three things go together here. First, that study was done in acute palliative care units, not at home, and it is data from abroad; I could not find Taiwanese data on symptoms at the end of life. Second, these signs describe what appears "relatively late". They are not a countdown, and should not be used to count days. Third: these changes are part of the process. They are not evidence that someone did something wrong. The medical team will see more detail than this, and that is their job; what a family needs is to recognise things, not to judge them.

Food and drips: the answer is less settled than people assume

This is the question families find most painful, and the one most likely to start an argument. The evidence first.

On the drip side there is a randomised trial: 129 patients with advanced cancer from six hospice organisations, randomised to one litre of intravenous fluid a day or a hundred millilitres a day of placebo infusion, with the primary endpoint the change from baseline at day four in the total score for four dehydration-related symptoms (fatigue, myoclonus, sedation, hallucinations). The result was −3.3 versus −2.8, p=0.77, no difference; the other symptom scales showed no difference either, and overall survival likewise showed no difference (median 21 days versus 15 days, p=0.83)[3]. Cochrane assembled six studies and could not pool them because of heterogeneity: one found better sedation and myoclonus in those given fluid, another found more marked dehydration without fluid but more pleural effusion, peripheral oedema and ascites with it, and the remaining four found no difference. The authors concluded that the evidence is insufficient to make a definite clinical recommendation[4].

On the tube-feeding side it is blunter: the same authors' other Cochrane review states "we found no randomised controlled trials or prospective controlled trials that met the inclusion criteria", and concludes that the available studies are not of sufficient quality to support any practice recommendation[5]. Both reviews stopped searching in 2014; remember the year.

So this is not "should be given" or "should not be given". It is "what does he need these few days" — which is something to decide together with the team, and the decision can be changed at any time. One thing has to be said honestly: whether a dying person feels hungry or thirsty — I could not find citable evidence, so I make no claim about what he subjectively experiences. How to care for a dry mouth: I could not find a citable source for the technique, so I give no steps — ask the nurse or the home team to show you once.

The rattling sound in the throat can be treated

That sound is frightening. Whether the patient himself is distressed by it — again I could not find citable evidence, so I do not answer for him. What can be said is that the symptom is treatable: in a double-blind, placebo-controlled randomised trial across six Dutch hospice organisations, 157 patients entering the dying phase received either preventive treatment or placebo, and the rate of moderate or worse rattle was 13% (10/79) against 27% (21/78), a difference of 14 percentage points (2 to 27), p=0.02[6]. Which drug, and whether to use one, is the team's decision, and I name no drug. What a family can do is turn him onto his side, and tell the team what you heard.

When breathing is hard, oxygen is not necessarily the answer

A randomised trial tested this directly. 239 adults with a life-limiting illness and refractory breathlessness, whose blood oxygen was not low (arterial oxygen tension above 7.3 kPa), at nine centres in Australia, the United States and the United Kingdom, were randomised to oxygen or room air by nasal cannula, 2 litres a minute, at least 15 hours a day, for 7 days. The change in breathlessness on the morning of day six was −0.9 versus −0.7 (p=0.504), and in the evening −0.3 versus −0.5 (p=0.554): no difference detected. The frequency of side effects did not differ either[7].

Read it together with the entry criterion: that trial enrolled people whose blood oxygen was not low. People with low blood oxygen were not in it, so this does not mean oxygen is useless. What it supports is this: if the team says "adding oxygen now will not make him more comfortable", there is a randomised trial behind that sentence.

When there is agitation, the first move is not another drug

A randomised, placebo-controlled trial at 11 Australian hospice or palliative care services enrolled 247 participants (88.3% of them cancer patients) into two antipsychotic arms or placebo, with all three arms receiving supportive care and individualised management of the precipitants of delirium at the same time, and rescue sedative medication available in all three where needed. On day three, the delirium symptom scores in both drug arms were higher — that is, worse — than in the placebo arm: by 0.48 (0.09 to 0.86, p=0.02) and 0.24 (0.06 to 0.42, p=0.009) respectively, with more extrapyramidal effects[8].

This is not saying "do not use drugs". What it says is: look for the cause first (pain, urinary retention, constipation, medication, unfamiliar surroundings), together with familiar people nearby and the light and noise kept simple — which is what the trial actually did in every arm.

Whether painkillers make someone go sooner: somebody measured it

In an American study of 1,306 hospice patients, 725 were using opioids and had at least one dose adjustment before death, with a mean interval of 12.46 days from the last adjustment to death. In the multivariable models, several factors were associated with shorter survival (including higher dose, a cancer diagnosis and unresponsiveness), but no model explained more than 10% of the variance in survival time, and the authors concluded that concern about hastening death is not a sufficient reason to withhold opioid analgesia[9]. In a Spanish hospital-at-home unit, among 223 cancer patients who died at home, those on higher doses survived longer (6 days versus 2 days), but the difference disappeared after adjustment (hazard ratio 0.86, 0.62 to 1.18, p=0.338)[10]. A hazard ratio of 1.0 means the two groups die at the same rate; 0.86 looks low, but the confidence interval crosses 1, which means the difference does not stand up. Both are retrospective or observational and cannot show cause. What they jointly support is that fear of hastening death is not a sufficient reason to withhold pain treatment — not that opioids definitely do not affect survival. Hurting for fear of addiction is in "How long have you hurt for fear of addiction?".

When to phone: the NHI has written a list

The NHI fee schedule for home-based hospice care sets out the indications for an emergency visit: 1. unstable vital signs; 2. persistent unrelieved breathlessness; 3. delirium or an unstable level of consciousness; 4. an acute episode of pain; 5. fever or sudden instability of body temperature; 6. acute diarrhoea; 7. a tube problem needing immediate attention; 8. any other situation a physician assesses as requiring an immediate visit[11].

The provision that makes phoning possible is this: an institution or nursing organisation claiming under this chapter must provide a 24-hour telephone consultation service staffed by hospice professionals. In the same schedule, the terminal patient visit fee expressly states that the service content must include the terminal visit, preparation for a good death, and bereavement support for the family[11] — bereavement support is written into the covered item, not a favour to be asked for. These provisions were checked on 2026-08-30; for how your own hospital does it, ask your case manager (個管師) or the medical social work office (社工室).

And one thing I could not find out, which you will certainly need: the administrative process after a death at home — who issues the death certificate, what the family does at the time. I obtained no official document, so I describe no process. Ask your home hospice team about this before it happens. That is the phone call I would make today.

What a family can do

Do not force feeds; moisten his mouth the way the team showed you. Turn him onto his side and the rattle will be quieter. Put your hand in his and talk to him — less response to voices does not mean he cannot hear. Less urine, breathlessness, agitation, sudden pain: write it down and phone, without waiting for morning.

One last thing. The person most easily overlooked in these days is you. Taking shifts, eating, sleeping are not selfish; they are the conditions for lasting to the end. Carer exhaustion is in "The carer breaks first". Whether to tell him everything about his illness is in "Whether to tell him, and what the law says"; who speaks for him is in "When he cannot decide, who decides".
