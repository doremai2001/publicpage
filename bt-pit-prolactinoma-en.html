PITUITARY ADENOMA

Prolactinoma: the drug comes first, not the knife

Prolactinoma is the only one where the drug leads; the first line for somatotroph adenoma and for Cushing's disease is still surgery. And "the drug first" does not mean never operated on — how well it works, what it costs, pregnancy, and the recurrence rates after stopping have to be read together.

The passage written most carefully in this article is the one about stopping. The guideline's own words are "can be considered", evidence level very low; and once it is stopped, in the meta-analysis only about two in ten keep a normal prolactin. Whether to stop and when is something the doctor is watching, not you.

Prolactinoma is the one kind of pituitary adenoma whose first-line treatment is a drug rather than a knife. That is easy to remember, and it is also the sentence most easily remembered as "pituitary tumours are treated with drugs first" — which is wrong: the first line for somatotroph adenoma and for Cushing's disease is surgery. And prolactinoma has its own exception inside it: for the Knosp grade 0 to 1, well-circumscribed group, surgery is also one of the first-line options. Same gland, different secretion, different first step. Which type is which is in "First, find out whether it is secreting".

The guideline cut its hole for this one tumour only

The Endocrine Society's pituitary incidentaloma guideline lists a string of conditions that should be referred for surgery, and the last item reads, word for word, "Hypersecreting tumors other than prolactinomas"[1]. In plain words: as a rule, adenomas that secrete are operated on, and prolactinoma is explicitly singled out as the exception — the first line for somatotroph adenoma and for Cushing's disease is surgery.

"A prolactinoma is treated with drugs first" does not mean "a prolactinoma is never operated on". The 2025 prolactinoma consensus (a Chinese one, not the Pituitary Society's) draws its line on the shape of the tumour: for Knosp grade 0 to 1, well-demarcated micro- and macroadenomas, surgery and medical therapy can both stand as first-line, with a multidisciplinary team setting out both sides and then deciding together with you — and the same document says it a second time further on. It is only at Knosp grade 2 and above, in invasive macroadenomas and giant adenomas, that what is written is drug-first for prolactinoma[2]. Which road you go down comes out of the imaging, the margins and the team's assessment; it is not something for you to judge for yourself. And the first line for somatotroph adenoma and for Cushing's disease is surgery, which is not part of this discussion at all.

What the drug can do, and the denominator you have to read it against

There is only one piece of head-to-head randomised data: in 1994, 459 women with hyperprolactinaemic amenorrhoea were randomised double-blind to cabergoline or bromocriptine. Stable normalisation of prolactin was 83% against 59%, ovulation or pregnancy 72% against 52%, and stopping the drug because the side effects were intolerable 3% against 12%[3]. The label that matters is in the population: of those 459, 279 had a microprolactinoma and 167 had idiopathic hyperprolactinaemia, and only 3 had a macroprolactinoma. That 83% is a statement about microadenomas; it cannot be carried across to macroadenomas.

The numbers for macroadenomas have to be found elsewhere. The 2025 consensus pooled 1,362 prolactinoma patients from the literature between 2000 and 2018: with cabergoline the overall biochemical remission rate was 79.7%, and within that, 84.0% in 541 people with microadenomas and 76.9% in 821 with macroadenomas, with 73.9% showing some reduction in tumour volume; for bromocriptine it was 68% for normalisation of prolactin and 62% for tumour shrinkage[2]. A 498-patient retrospective set points the same way (normalisation of prolactin 87.4% against 41.4%), but the two groups were 450 against 48[4]. It is not a randomised comparison, and I discount it accordingly when I read it.

What if it is already on the optic nerve, or already four centimetres?

A retrospective cohort of 150 macroprolactinomas followed for a median of six years: at diagnosis 40 people (26.7%) had a visual field defect, and of the 39 who had a follow-up field test by the end, 24 (61.5%) recovered completely. The ones that recovered well had smaller tumours[5]. Note that the cohort I am quoting mixes medical and surgical treatment; it is not a drug-only group.

For giant prolactinomas of four centimetres and above, the first line is still a drug — and, saying it again, that is a separate matter from the first line for somatotroph adenoma and Cushing's disease being surgery, and a separate matter again from "in the Knosp grade 0 to 1, well-circumscribed group, surgery is also one of the first-line options". A single-centre cohort of 33 people spanning twenty-three years is very plain about it: among those whose first line was medical, two-thirds needed nothing but the drug from beginning to end; 9 (27%) went to surgery because of complications, 4 were able to come off treatment after ten to twenty years on it, and the remaining 29 are still on it[6]. A systematic review of 10 papers and 104 giant prolactinomas found normalisation of prolactin in 69.4% with cabergoline against 31.7% with bromocriptine, while tumour shrinkage and visual field improvement showed no difference between the two drugs[7].

The price of the tablets is not just feeling a bit sick

On the gastrointestinal side, in the randomised data nausea ran at about 50% with bromocriptine against 31% with cabergoline and vomiting at 10% against 4%, with headache and dizziness much the same for both[2]. What gets raised much less often is impulse control disorder. A multicentre cross-sectional study questioned 200 patients with pituitary adenomas who were on a dopamine agonist: 52% reported at least one, with compulsive shopping at 28.5% and hypersexuality at 24.5%; risk was a great deal higher in those with a psychiatric history[8]. This is a questionnaire survey in tertiary referral centres, so the proportions will run high; but if your family think you have "not been yourself lately", that is something to raise at the clinic.

There is one more complication that runs against intuition: when a large tumour is shrunk by the drug, a defect can be left in the floor of the sella. The consensus puts the incidence of cerebrospinal fluid rhinorrhoea during dopamine agonist treatment at about 6.1%, and it states in as many words that this needs immediate surgical repair[2]. Clear watery fluid running from one nostril while you are on the drug, worse when you bend your head forward, means going back to the clinic the same day. The emergency side of all this is in "Pituitary apoplexy, and not enough hormone".

Heart valves: the Parkinson's dose is not the endocrine dose

The signal starts in Parkinson's disease. A nested case-control study in a UK database (a cohort of 11,417) found an incidence rate ratio of 4.9 for new cardiac valve regurgitation in Parkinson's patients taking cabergoline (95% confidence interval 1.5 to 15.6; an incidence rate ratio divides the rate of new events in one group by the rate in the other, and a confidence interval is the range the true value plausibly sits in)[9]. That population is treated at doses far higher than the ones used for prolactinoma, and the label cannot be dropped.

Endocrine doses are a separate layer. A meta-analysis of 13 case-control studies found more tricuspid regurgitation, odds ratio 3.74 (95% confidence interval 1.79 to 7.8) — an odds ratio is the ratio of the "had it against did not have it" odds in the two groups, and cannot be read as a multiple of the probability; the same paper immediately goes on to say "In no patient was tricuspid valve dysfunction diagnosed as a result of clinical symptoms", no other valve showed a significant increase, and the authors' conclusion is that the clinical significance is unclear[10]. The consensus wording, copied across: an echocardiogram is done at baseline before treatment; people on long-term higher doses, treated for more than five years, over fifty, or with a murmur on auscultation are followed up regularly, at intervals of two to three years thereafter (evidence level low, strength of recommendation weak)[2].

Stopping the drug: this is what the doctor is weighing up

Let me put this at the front: whether the drug stops is the doctor's judgement, not yours. What the consensus writes is that in patients who have been on a low maintenance dose for more than two years, with a normal prolactin and a clearly shrunken tumour or no visible mass on MRI, stopping "can be considered" — evidence level very low, strength of recommendation weak[2]. Those three items are what the doctor is looking at; they are not a checklist you can run against yourself. And even with all three in place, that does not mean it will hold — of the three sets of data in the next paragraph, only 21% stayed off it successfully, and the recurrences measured in the other two were 54% and 63%.

Once it is stopped, most people come back. A meta-analysis of 19 papers and 743 people worked out a pooled proportion of only 21% in whom prolactin stayed normal after withdrawal, stratified as 32% for idiopathic hyperprolactinaemia, 21% for microprolactinoma and 16% for macroprolactinoma[11]. Another study, which selected its patients strictly against criteria, had 46 people (31 microadenomas, 11 macroadenomas, 4 non-tumoural) with an overall recurrence of 54% and an estimated eighteen-month recurrence risk of 63%, with 91% of the recurrences happening within the first year after stopping[12]. A prospective study of 200 people gave recurrence two to five years after withdrawal of 24% (non-tumoural), 31% (microprolactinoma) and 36% (macroprolactinoma), with not one tumour regrowing; the authors point out themselves that the follow-up is not long enough to rule out delayed growth[13]. Stopping is not "the course has finished", it is switching to a different kind of follow-up: the consensus recommends a prolactin every three months for the first year after withdrawal and yearly after that, with a test any time there are symptoms[2]. When it starts, when it stops, and when it goes back on, are all decided by the doctor.

If you want to get pregnant, this is a conversation for beforehand

The consensus keeps microadenomas and macroadenomas well apart. Microprolactinoma: no need to stop the drug while trying to conceive, and stopping is advised once pregnancy is confirmed — after which the chance of the tumour growing enough to cause symptoms during the pregnancy is only 1% to 5%. Macroprolactinoma: the advice is to shrink the tumour to the size of a microadenoma before conceiving; those that are resistant or still growing have surgery before conceiving; and where control before pregnancy was not good, continuing the drug right through the pregnancy can be considered, because in an untreated macroadenoma the chance of growing enough to cause symptoms during pregnancy runs as high as 15% to 35%[2].

I am putting the data that point the other way in as well: in a meta-analysis of 12 papers and 1,387 pregnancies, live birth rates were lower in those who stayed on cabergoline through the pregnancy than in those who stopped as soon as it was confirmed (relative risk 0.81, 95% confidence interval 0.67 to 0.98 — a relative risk of 0.81 means the probability was 0.81 times that in the comparison group), while congenital malformation, preterm birth and low birth weight showed no difference[14]. This is an observational pooling, and the group that carried on with the drug were the people whose tumours were harder to control in the first place, so it cannot be read as the drug having harmed anybody. Every one of these is a pre-pregnancy clinic conversation, not a decision to be made once you are already pregnant.

When the drug stops moving it, that is when surgery comes up

The consensus defines resistance as treatment at the recommended dose for three to six months without normalisation of prolactin and without the tumour volume halving. Resistance runs at about 20% to 30% for bromocriptine and about 10% for cabergoline; when it will not move, the advice is to switch to cabergoline first. The situations the consensus lists for referral to surgery are: intolerance or resistance to the drug; and a prolactinoma that suffers pituitary apoplexy with rapidly deteriorating vision[2]. The consensus also gives the figures for the surgical road: in experienced centres, immediate postoperative biochemical remission runs 90% to 100% for well-circumscribed microadenomas and 66.7% to 81.0% for Knosp grade 0 to 1 macroadenomas. That side of it is in "Where endonasal surgery and radiotherapy fit".

On the Taiwan end I found only two clauses I can quote word for word. In the NHI drug reimbursement regulations, clause 5.4.7 on cabergoline reads "restricted to conditions caused by hyperprolactinaemia", with a note that suppression of lactation after childbirth is not reimbursed; clause 5.4.2 on bromocriptine consists of the single sentence "where it is used for suppression of normal lactation after childbirth, it shall not be reimbursed"[15] (the clauses are in Chinese; the wording here is my translation of them). The clauses say what they say — whether your own situation gets through is a question for the medical affairs office. The prolactin test used for follow-up is item 09120C in the fee schedule, at 150 points[16].

Three sentences to take to clinic: what Knosp grade is mine, and are the margins clear or not? Given my prolactin and the size now, how far am I from "we can start discussing stopping"? And if the day comes to stop, how are the blood tests over the following year going to be scheduled?

From the physician

If you are on a dopamine agonist, three things to hold on to: the drug is not something you decide to stop; if your family think your shopping or your impulses have changed lately, that is something to raise at the clinic; and clear watery fluid from one nostril while you are on it means going back the same day. One more thing to settle early: whether the margins of your tumour count as well-defined on the imaging — that decides whether you are in the small group for whom surgery can also be first-line.
