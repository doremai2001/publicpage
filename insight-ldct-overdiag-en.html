LUNG SCREENING

When screening finds too much

A Lancet Regional Health commentary flags overdiagnosis from LDCT screening in East Asian never-smokers. In Taiwan, early-stage diagnoses among women rose nearly sixfold from 2004 to 2018 while late-stage incidence and mortality barely moved.

Insights · Screening & Early Detection · 4 references
#Lung#Screening#Overdiagnosis#NHI Coverage

Almost nobody argues against catching cancer early. But when early diagnoses climb sixfold and late-stage incidence and mortality stay flat, it is worth asking who the extra diagnoses helped.

Lung cancer is Taiwan's leading cause of cancer death, killing 9,629 people in 2020, nearly one-fifth (19.2%) of all cancer deaths. But lung cancer in Taiwan doesn't quite match the textbook picture built in the West. Western screening logic was built around heavy smokers, yet Taiwan has a large population of patients who never smoked, especially women: only 5.9% of female lung cancer cases in Taiwan involve a smoking history. That's part of why Taiwan's National Health Administration launched, on July 1, 2022, what it describes as the world's first national low-dose CT (LDCT) lung cancer screening program covering two risk groups at once: people with a family history of lung cancer, and heavy smokers. Screening smokers alone would miss a large share of Taiwan's genuinely high-risk population. LDCT works by scanning the lungs with a much lower radiation dose than a standard CT, catching small nodules in theory before a tumor has grown large or spread. Five-year survival for stage 1 lung cancer runs around 90%, versus roughly 10% for stage 4, and that gap is the core argument behind screening policy. Taiwan's program has genuinely caught early cases since launch, and "early detection, early treatment" has become a near-unquestioned message in public health messaging. But does screening more, and catching cancer earlier, automatically mean saving more lives? Does earlier detection carry a cost of its own? A commentary published this year in The Lancet Regional Health – Western Pacific puts the brakes on that assumption, and it singles out East Asia, the region Taiwan belongs to.

The commentary was co-authored by Xuan Xu and Wanghong Xu of Fudan University School of Public Health, together with Haiquan Chen, a thoracic surgeon at Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center[1]. The authors note that non-smoking-related lung cancer accounts for only 15% to 20% of cases globally, but nearly 40% in East Asia, a distinct disease pattern that has driven rapid adoption of LDCT screening in general-risk populations across the region, often outpacing what international guidelines recommend. Most international guidelines, including the US Preventive Services Task Force, still limit their LDCT recommendations to heavy smokers, precisely because of concern that screening beyond that group could do more harm than good. The result of the broader push, the authors warn, is that large numbers of ground-glass nodules and indolent lesions are being detected, many of which would never grow, spread, or threaten life, while patients undergo unnecessary follow-up imaging, biopsies, or surgery, along with the anxiety and cost that come with them. They propose four priorities: building risk-stratification models specific to East Asian populations, reclassifying some indolent lesions as precancerous rather than cancer, identifying the optimal surgical window for early adenocarcinoma, and accelerating randomized controlled trials designed for East Asian populations rather than simply importing smoking-based Western criteria. None of this is speculative. Taiwan's own data raised a similar warning four years earlier.

A 2022 study in JAMA Internal Medicine, led by a Taipei Medical University team working with Brigham and Women's Hospital, analyzed Taiwan's national cancer registry and mortality statistics and found that between 2004 and 2018, early-stage (stage 0-1) lung cancer diagnoses among Taiwanese women rose nearly sixfold, from 2.3 to 14.4 cases per 100,000[2]. Over the same period, late-stage incidence barely moved, and lung cancer mortality only edged down from 17 to 16 deaths per 100,000. The team estimated that roughly 7,000 to 12,000 Taiwanese women were overdiagnosed during that window, meaning they were told they had cancer when the lesion in question would never have caused symptoms or death in their lifetime. Female smoking rates in Taiwan stayed under 5% throughout this period, so the surge in early diagnoses wasn't a byproduct of anti-smoking campaigns; it reflects screening finding things that never needed finding. H. Gilbert Welch, MD, of Harvard Medical School and the study's senior author, said in an interview that survival rates rise automatically whenever overdiagnosis is present, because the extra people being counted were never going to die of the disease in the first place, so a better survival statistic doesn't necessarily mean screening saved more lives[3]. That gap between rising early-stage diagnoses and flat mortality is exactly the concern at the center of the Lancet commentary: a sharp climb in early diagnoses without a matching drop in late-stage cases or deaths suggests a meaningful share of what's being caught was never going to be fatal.

None of this means LDCT screening is useless or that people should skip it; that reads far more into the commentary than it argues. The real point is that who gets screened matters more than whether screening happens at all. Taiwan's existing publicly funded LDCT program is itself a risk-stratified design: eligibility covers heavy smokers aged 50 to 74 with a 30-plus pack-year history (including those who quit within the past 15 years), plus men aged 50 to 74 and women aged 45 to 74 with a parent, child, or sibling diagnosed with lung cancer, screened once every two years, free of charge with an NHI card at a contracted facility[4]. Taiwan's own screening logic already echoes the risk stratification the Lancet authors are calling for. It doesn't screen everyone; it screens people at genuinely elevated risk, which makes this commentary less a rebuke of Taiwan's policy than a case for not expanding it further. The group that should take real note is anyone considering LDCT outside those eligibility criteria, paying out of pocket "just to check." With no smoking history and no first-degree relative with lung cancer, it's worth discussing actual individual risk factors with a doctor first (family history of other cancers, long-term cooking-oil fume exposure, radon, secondhand smoke, specific occupational exposures) rather than treating LDCT as a routine checkup item, or booking one because of reassuring advertising. A small nodule turning up isn't automatically a cancer diagnosis, either. Most ground-glass nodules under 6mm are managed with periodic imaging surveillance rather than immediate biopsy or surgery, a decision worth working through with a pulmonologist or thoracic surgeon rather than panicking, or demanding surgery the moment a report uses the word "abnormal." Screening still deserves a place for people who meet the eligibility criteria; the benefit is real. But the instinct that more screening is automatically better just took a real hit, and precision about who gets screened may matter more than how often. At your next visit, it's worth asking directly: given my situation, do I actually qualify for the free program? That question may do more good than searching online or booking a self-pay scan alone.

From the physician

The commentary does not say LDCT is useless; it says who gets screened matters more than whether screening happens. Taiwan's publicly funded program is already risk-stratified — heavy smokers aged 50 to 74 with 30 or more pack-years (including those who quit within 15 years), plus men aged 50 to 74 and women aged 45 to 74 with a first-degree relative diagnosed with lung cancer, every two years — so this reads less as a rebuke of Taiwan's policy than as a case against widening it. The group that should pause is anyone paying out of pocket to just check without a smoking history or a first-degree relative: discuss individual risk factors with a physician first, including family history of other cancers, long-term cooking-oil fumes, radon, secondhand smoke and occupational exposures. A small nodule on a report is not a diagnosis either; ground-glass nodules under 6 mm are usually followed with periodic imaging rather than biopsied or resected.
