Which cell actually has a drug in it

Neither of the drugs in the FGFR and HER2 boxes belongs in the first line. How many people have one, the nine things the companion diagnostic recognises, where Taiwan's licence and the NHI clauses part company, and one indication that has already been withdrawn.

One study measured that distance: of 208 patients who were tested, 21 had actually received the targeted drug by the data cut-off, and 6 of those stopped it because of toxicity. Between the line on the report and the drug reaching the body there are several boxes to cross.

"Could we do a gene test and see whether there is a drug I can have?" I hear that in clinic every week. The honest answer is not yes or no; it is that three conditions have to hold at the same time: your alteration happens to sit on a list that is fixed in advance, you happen to have already reached the line of therapy at which the drug is allowed to be used, and that drug can actually be obtained in Taiwan. Take one away and the line on the report stops on the paper. This article takes the three conditions apart. Verified 13 September 2026.

First, the box that is easiest to misread: the two markers that really do have a drug against them — FGFR and HER2 — are both out of the first line. The first line will not change because you had a gene test. How the first line runs is in "The first line after it has spread"; the upper tract has its own article in "It grows in the upper tract too".

FGFR: how many people have one, and the nine things the test is really looking for

A reflex-testing cohort supplies a denominator: 366 specimens collected prospectively at two Canadian medical centres between 2021 and 2025, with any FGFR1 to 4 alteration in 16.1% and an "actionable" one in 13.4% (33 FGFR3 mutations, 13 FGFR3 fusions, 3 FGFR2 mutations); upper tract 23.8% against lower tract 13.8%, P=0.007[1]. That cohort only tested specimens from metastatic or pT3/pT4 disease. The only general statement that holds up is this one: roughly one in every six or seven people with metastatic urothelial carcinoma carries an actionable FGFR alteration, and the upper tract has somewhat more of them than the bladder. This is molecular epidemiology, not Taiwanese incidence data.

More important than the proportion: "the report says there is an FGFR alteration" and "this alteration is on that list" are two different things. The companion diagnostic approved in the United States is a qualitative tissue RT-PCR test, and what it looks for is a list fixed in advance: the four FGFR3 hotspot mutations R248C, S249C, G370C and Y373C, plus the fusions FGFR3-TACC3, FGFR3-BAIAP2L1, FGFR2-BICC1 and FGFR2-CASP7[2]. Another prospective study counts that list as nine items, four hotspot mutations plus five fusions[3], and the same paper states outright that at present there is "no internationally standardised testing approach", so who gets selected and who can get hold of an FGFR-targeted drug varies by platform[3].

The same alteration, a different line of therapy, a different answer

THOR's first cohort enrolled people with a susceptible FGFR3/2 alteration whose disease had progressed after one or two lines of treatment, one of which included a PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor: 266 people (erdafitinib 136, physician's choice of chemotherapy 130), median follow-up 15.9 months, median overall survival 12.1 against 7.8 months, hazard ratio 0.64 (95% confidence interval 0.47 to 0.88; a confidence interval means that if you ran the same study many times over, the answer would land in that range), P=0.005; median progression-free survival 5.6 against 2.7 months[4]. The response rate was 45.6% against 11.5%[5]. After an interim analysis showed erdafitinib doing better, the chemotherapy arm was allowed to cross over to it[5].

The second cohort was negative, and the question it asked was precisely "can this be used earlier?": 351 people with the same FGFR alterations but who had not yet had immunotherapy, erdafitinib against pembrolizumab, median follow-up 33 months, median overall survival 10.9 against 11.1 months, hazard ratio 1.18 (0.92 to 1.51), P=0.18; response rate 40.0% against 21.6%, but the median duration of response 4.3 against 14.4 months[6]. A higher response rate, no difference in survival, and the responses were shorter — what the authors wrote themselves is that "outcomes with pembrolizumab were better than assumed"[6]. So "having an FGFR alteration" does not mean "this drug should go in now". The limitation-of-use sentence on both countries' labels comes straight out of that result: not recommended in patients who are eligible for but have not previously received a PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitor[2].

This drug wants you at the eye clinic every month

Erdafitinib has two characteristic toxicities. The eyes: central serous retinopathy or retinal pigment epithelial detachment in 22%, with a median time to first occurrence of 46 days; of the 104 people it happened to, 40% needed the drug held and 56% needed a dose reduction, and among the 24 who restarted after a hold, 67% had it recur or worsen, with 41% still ongoing at the last assessment[2]. The label requires an eye examination monthly for the first four months and every three months thereafter[2]. Blurred vision, or a dark or distorted patch in the centre of your field of view: contact the team that day, do not wait for the next appointment.

Phosphate: the label states plainly that a rise in blood phosphate is a pharmacodynamic effect of this drug rather than an accident, and 24% of patients used a phosphate binder during treatment[2]. In THOR's safety population (erdafitinib 135, chemotherapy 112) the figures were hyperphosphataemia 78.5% (grade 3 or worse 5.2%) and onycholysis 23.0%; dose reduction for an adverse event 68.9% and interruption 71.9%, against 24.1% and 31.3% in the chemotherapy arm[7]. Permanent discontinuation 21%, and the commonest reason for it is the eye problem[2].

The Taiwan box: there is a licence, and zero hits in the reimbursement clauses

As of 13 September 2026, erdafitinib has a Taiwan marketing authorisation: 盼樂膜衣錠 3/4/5 mg, 衛部藥輸字第027912、027913、027914號, and the indication recorded on the authorisation reads "for the treatment of adult patients with locally advanced or metastatic urothelial carcinoma (mUC) who meet the following conditions: 1. having a susceptible FGFR3 genetic alteration, and 2. having had disease progression during or following at least one line of prior systemic therapy"[8]. Note that 3: the indication has narrowed from FGFR2/3 to FGFR3. THOR enrolled on FGFR3/2, but people carrying an FGFR2 alteration are not inside the current Taiwanese or American label[8][2].

On the NHI side: a full-text search of the drug reimbursement regulations for "erdafitinib", "Balversa" and 「盼樂」 returns zero hits on all three search terms[9]. Zero hits means I could not find a listed item; I am not inferring in either direction, and I am not writing an amount. Ask the medical affairs office; how an application would be made is a question for them.

HER2 three-plus: the denominator is sixteen people, and it is an accelerated approval

DESTINY-PanTumor02 is a phase 2, open-label, single-arm trial across seven tumour types, 267 people in total. The definition of its bladder cohort takes in the renal pelvis, ureter, bladder and urethra, so these are not purely bladder figures[10]. That cohort had 41 people, with an investigator-assessed response rate of 39.0% (24.2 to 55.5); of those centrally confirmed as IHC 3+ there were only 16, with a response rate of 56.3% (29.9 to 80.2)[10] — a denominator of sixteen, and a confidence interval stretching from 29.9 to 80.2, a width that is itself the answer.

"HER2 3+" here is also not the thing you may take it for: the scoring in this trial used the ASCO/CAP criteria for gastric cancer, not the breast ones[10]. And on the same specimens, agreement between the local and the central laboratory was only 58.6% for IHC 3+; the authors' conclusion is that a "validated diagnostic test and standardised algorithm" are needed[11]. Change laboratory and you may change the answer. On safety: among the 267, grade 3 or worse drug-related adverse events were 40.8%, and interstitial lung disease adjudicated as drug-related was 10.5%, with three deaths among them[10]. A new dry cough, getting breathless walking, a fever: contact the team that day.

In the United States this pan-tumour indication is an accelerated approval: the label says so itself — approved on the basis of objective response rate and duration of response, with continued approval possibly contingent on verification of clinical benefit in a confirmatory trial[12]. Taiwan has a licence (優赫得凍晶注射劑, 衛部菌疫輸字第001179號, with the sixth item of its indications covering pan-tumour HER2-positive IHC 3+ solid tumours)[8]; NHI clause 9.115 has only the two breast cancer paragraphs, and urothelial carcinoma returns zero hits[9]. Again, no listed item that I could find; again, a question for the medical affairs office.

Accelerated approval is not confirmed efficacy: an indication that has already been withdrawn

The ready-made teaching example is sacituzumab govitecan. It once held an American accelerated approval, for metastatic urothelial carcinoma after platinum and immunotherapy. EAU records what followed: the confirmatory trial TROPiCS-04 did not significantly improve overall survival or progression-free survival, and the indication was withdrawn as a result; grade 3 or worse treatment-related adverse events were 67% against 35% and grade 5 treatment-emergent adverse events 7% against 2%, both higher than in the comparator arm of physician's choice of chemotherapy[13]. In today's American label the drug's indications are down to two kinds of breast cancer, with no urothelial carcinoma at all[14].

So what "accelerated approval" means is this: a response rate bought a ticket in, and the real report card has not been handed in yet.

PD-L1: one marker, three places, three identities

The United States: pembrolizumab's current first-line single-agent indication is written for people "not eligible for any platinum-containing chemotherapy", with no PD-L1 condition; the same label also records the failure of KEYNOTE-361 — in that 1,010-patient phase 3 trial, neither progression-free survival nor overall survival in the pembrolizumab-plus-chemotherapy arm met the major efficacy outcome measures, and the other endpoints "could not be formally tested"[15]. In atezolizumab's current American label, a search for a urothelial carcinoma indication returns zero hits[14]. The gate swapped from "a biomarker" to "whether the body can take platinum" — which is how this marker left the trials.

Europe: the definition EAU records is still tied to PD-L1 — for pembrolizumab, a CPS ≥ 10 on the Dako 22C3 platform; for atezolizumab, tumour-infiltrating immune cells ≥ 5% on Ventana SP142[13]. Taiwan: the biomarker table in NHI clause 9.69 still uses it to this day as the gate on payment, and word for word it is, for urothelial carcinoma, first-line treatment (single agent) pembrolizumab 「CPS≧10」, first-line treatment (combined with chemotherapy) nivolumab 「TC≥1%」, second-line treatment pembrolizumab 「CPS≧10」 and nivolumab 「TC≧5%」, with the avelumab maintenance box reading "no report need be attached"[9]. The testing platforms are tied down one by one in the table's header as well; not every stain counts[9].

So "PD-L1 no longer matters" is a sentence that cannot be said. It lost its selecting role in the trials, yet it is still there in the payment rules outside the United States. That number on your report may still decide today whether the NHI pays.

The test came back. How far is it from there to having the drug?

One study measured that distance: a prospective study across 12 Canadian centres from 2021 to 2024, in which 26% of the 208 analysable patients had an FGFR alteration detected in either tissue or ctDNA; by the data cut-off, 21 people had actually received erdafitinib, and 6 of those 21 (29%) stopped it because of toxicity[3]. Between "it is on the report" and "the body actually gets the drug", a great many people drop out.

Can a blood test replace a biopsy? In the same study, among the 125 people with both an interpretable ctDNA result and a matched tissue result, the two agreed 90% of the time and ctDNA had a sensitivity of 84% against the alterations detected in tissue; but there was also one patient whose upper tract biopsy carried an FGFR3-TACC3 fusion that was never detected in any blood sample[3]. The authors conclude that the two should be used "in combination", not one instead of the other. Patients already known to have no tissue available were never screened into the study at all[3].

Nor should you expect comprehensive genomic testing to hand you several boxes to choose between. Japan's national registry (1,014 people with metastatic urothelial carcinoma) found FGFR3 alterations in 15.5% and ERBB2 amplification in 14.8%, but only 1.3% had both, below the 2.3% expected if they were statistically independent (P=0.010)[16] — the two boxes are largely mutually exclusive. ERBB2 amplification and HER2 IHC 3+ are not the same thing either.

How long does it take? For this box I could not find a figure I could cite, so I am not writing a number of days: it varies by laboratory, ask your doctor or your case manager. As for when to test, EAU places both erdafitinib and trastuzumab deruxtecan in the later lines[13]; but the testing itself can be done early — the authors of THOR's Japanese subgroup recommend that early FGFR testing be considered once metastatic urothelial carcinoma is diagnosed[7]. Testing early is not for changing the first line; it is so that the answer is already in hand when that box comes round.

Three sentences worth getting out loud at the next appointment: which list is my test looking for, was it done on tissue or on blood, and does this line of the report fall on that list? Given the line of therapy I am on, will this result change the treatment now? And if the drug it points to has no listed item in the NHI, who do I speak to about how an application is made? Every licence and every clause on this page stops at 13 September 2026; when you read it, check the current position first.

From the physician

When you take the test report into the clinic, three questions are enough: which list was it looking for, was it done on tissue or on blood, and will this result change the treatment at the line of therapy I am on now. If the answer is that it only comes into play further down the line, note it and leave it there; there is no need to rush into adding self-paid tests. Licences and reimbursement both move, and this page stops at 13 September 2026.
