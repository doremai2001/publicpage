Active surveillance is neither stalling nor gambling

Who it is for, what the schedule involves, when to come off — and why ProtecT's 9.4% cannot be moved onto a modern programme.

Prostate Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 15 references
#Prostate#Active Surveillance#Decisions#Quality of Life#Clinical Trials

This one has to block two mistakes at once, so it does not read comfortably in either direction. Nothing here qualifies anyone to decline treatment, and nothing here is an argument for treating a low-risk cancer.

"Doctor, so I don't need treatment? You mean we just leave it alone?" That is the commonest misunderstanding of active surveillance, and it rolls two things into one.

This is the piece I have written most carefully. Misreadings either way cause harm: a high-risk man decides to "wait and see" and loses the window in which he was curable; a low-risk man is frightened into radical treatment he never needed, and lives with the leakage and sexual dysfunction.

Get the name right first: active surveillance is not leaving it alone

Active surveillance and watchful waiting are two different things. EAU: active surveillance has curative intent and a pre-arranged schedule (rectal examination, PSA, repeat biopsy, MRI), for life expectancy over 10 years; watchful waiting is palliative, followed up individually, for life expectancy under 10 years[1]. NCCN's patient version agrees: observation needs no regular biopsies, just a physical and a PSA once or twice a year[2].

Active surveillance is not a deferred decision but an option with indications. AUA/ASTRO Statement 11: for low-risk prostate cancer, clinicians should recommend active surveillance as the preferred management option (Strong Recommendation, Evidence Level Grade A); Statement 13 requires that favorable intermediate-risk men have all three options discussed[3].

ProtecT at fifteen years: prostate cancer mortality did not separate the three arms

ProtecT is the only large randomised trial here: 1,643 men with localised prostate cancer randomised to active monitoring (545), radical prostatectomy (553) or radiotherapy (545), median follow-up 15 years, 98% complete.

  • Forty-five men (2.7%) died of prostate cancer: 3.1% monitoring, 2.2% surgery, 2.9% radiotherapy, P = 0.53 overall.

  • 24.4% of the monitoring arm were alive at the end of follow-up having never had any prostate cancer treatment[4].

I am not sliding past that P = 0.53. It means "this trial did not detect a difference between the three arms", not "the three are proved equally good". Fifteen years and 45 events give limited power. And something gets skipped: of those 1,643 men, more than a third were already intermediate or high risk at diagnosis[4].

The other half of the same men: more metastases, more progression on monitoring

Writing only that section would be this piece's biggest failure. Same trial, same men, same report:

  • Metastases: 9.4% in the monitoring arm, 4.7% with surgery, 5.0% with radiotherapy.

  • Clinical progression: 25.9% monitoring, 10.5% surgery, 11.0% radiotherapy.

  • Long-term hormone therapy required: 12.7% monitoring, 7.2% surgery, 7.7% radiotherapy[4].

The gap did not appear only in the final years. At 10 years, metastases were 33 against 13 against 16 (P=0.004), progression 112 against 46 against 46 (P<0.001)[5].

The honest way to put it: in ProtecT, monitoring did not buy a higher prostate cancer mortality, but it did buy more metastases, more progression and more men on hormone therapy. That is a trade you make.

But ProtecT's monitoring is not today's active surveillance

Leave this out and that 9.4% gets transplanted onto modern programmes. ProtecT's protocol was designed in 1999 and its arm was called active monitoring: PSA-led follow-up, no routine MRI, no routine repeat biopsy. Modern surveillance is a different system. EAU recommends PSA at least every 6 months, rectal examination at least yearly, repeat biopsies at intervals[1]. NCCN's patient version schedules PSA twice a year, rectal examination once a year, MRI every 1 to 2 years, biopsy every 1 to 3 years, and states that all patients should have a confirmatory biopsy within 1 to 2 years of the first[2].

Confirmatory biopsy is no formality. A meta-analysis of 6,039 men on active surveillance found a pooled upgrade rate at first confirmatory biopsy of 20%[6]. One in five had a grade that was an underestimate.

Both halves need saying: ProtecT's 9.4% cannot be moved onto a modern programme with MRI, confirmatory biopsy and PSA density triage; and that programme has no 15-year randomised trial of its own to set against it.

What gets you in, and what takes you out

EAU inclusion: ISUP Grade Group 1, cT1c or cT2a, PSA under 10 ng/mL, PSA density under 0.15 ng/mL/cc; favorable intermediate may be considered, but only low-volume Grade Group 2 with PSA under 10, low density, ≤cT2a[1]. The EAU systematic review defines low volume as ≤3 positive cores with ≤50% involvement of each; men with ISUP Grade Group 3 should be excluded[7].

Know the exit criteria too. EAU: reclassification to ISUP Grade Group ≥3, or cribriform or intraductal patterns appearing on biopsy[1]. A long-running Canadian cohort of 993 men used other triggers: PSA doubling time under 3 years, Gleason upgrading, or unequivocal clinical progression[8]. The two do not match, so ask: which exit criteria does my hospital use?

In those same 993 men, 15 (1.5%) died of prostate cancer, 15-year cancer-specific survival was 94.3%, and the cumulative probability of dying of something else was 9.2 times that of prostate cancer[8].

The Johns Hopkins cohort of 1,298 men gives the other side: cumulative incidence of radical treatment was 50% at 10 years, 57% at 15[9]. Active surveillance buys time, not exemption.

Who it does not suit, written out in full

EAU's listed exclusions: cribriform, predominantly ductal, sarcomatoid, small cell, plus extraprostatic extension or lymphovascular invasion already present; men with life expectancy under 10 years the guideline steers to observation instead[1].

What most needs writing down is how often a biopsy misses those two patterns. Of 641 men with a Grade Group 2 to 3 biopsy who went to surgery, biopsy found cribriform in only 5.5% and intraductal carcinoma in 3.4%, against 18.6% and 11.1% in the surgical specimens; MRI-assisted biopsy had a sensitivity of only 21.8% for cribriform, 26.8% for intraductal[10]. Both are associated with worse extraprostatic extension, biochemical recurrence and cancer death[11].

So "my report doesn't say cribriform" is not much reassurance. Not seeing it on a biopsy is not the same as it not being there, which is why active surveillance is a schedule, not a one-off verdict.

Grade Group 1 can be monitored because of one fact from radical specimens: Gleason ≤6 hardly ever metastasises[12]. But that is final pathology, not the biopsy in your hand — back to the 20% upgrade rate[6]. The full evidence and its trap is in "The few lines on the report that change treatment".

Two randomised trials also belong here. SPCG-4 randomised 695 men whose localised prostate cancer was found clinically, not by screening, to watchful waiting or radical surgery, over 29 years: 71 prostate cancer deaths against 110, relative risk 0.55 (95% confidence interval 0.41–0.74), the surgery arm living 2.9 years longer on average[13]. A relative risk divides one group's proportion by the other's: 0.55 means the proportion dying of prostate cancer in the surgery arm was about 55% of the observation arm's, not "risk reduced by 55%"; the bracketed interval is the range the true value plausibly sits in. Those men were at a later stage than the ones I meet today, so 0.55 cannot frighten a Grade Group 1 man found by PSA testing — and cannot be pretended away.

PIVOT enrolled a PSA-era population, 731 men followed 19.5 years, prostate cancer or treatment-related death 7.4% against 11.4%. In subgroups, the absolute difference in all-cause mortality for surgery against observation was 14.5 percentage points (2.8–25.6) in intermediate-risk men and only 0.7 points (−10.5 to 11.8) in the low-risk group, interaction P=0.08; incontinence and erectile dysfunction were both more common in the surgery arm through 10 years[14]. That interaction is exploratory, so 14.5 is only a signal; the direction is clear — the higher the risk, the more not treating costs, and at the low-risk end too small to see.

Does anyone in Taiwan actually do this

Yes, with follow-up data. National Taiwan University Hospital and Linkou Chang Gung enrolled 405 consecutive non-high-risk men from 2012 to 2023, median follow-up 64.6 months. Five-year reclassification fell between 18.6% and 27.0%, conversion to treatment between 20.4% and 43.6%; PSA density ≥0.15 predicted reclassification (hazard ratio 1.84, 1.17–2.88; a hazard ratio compares the relative rate of reclassification over the same period, not a multiple of probability). In 2,141 person-years, 1 man developed metastasis and none died of prostate cancer[15].

Be careful with that last sentence: 405 men, five years, 1 metastasis and 0 deaths is nowhere near enough for any conclusion about survival. All it supports is this — men in Taiwan do this too, and the medium-term reclassification and conversion rates look like those abroad.

On money I found no citable regulation: whether the PSA tests, MRIs and repeat biopsies during surveillance count as "related treatment" under the Catastrophic Illness Certificate (重大傷病證明), and are exempt from co-payment, ask your case manager (個管師) or the hospital billing office (醫務課). How to apply is in "This first month: order matters more than speed".

The two things I least want this piece to cause

The first is a man reading it and telling his doctor "I don't want treatment, I want to watch it". Nothing here gives him that standing. "Prostate cancer grows slowly, there's no rush" is true for some men and lethal for others, and what tells them apart is not how you feel but the grade on your report, your PSA density, your imaging and your life expectancy. Entry and exit criteria are defined; the threshold is not your willingness.

The second is a man frightened by that 9.4% metastasis figure into radical treatment he did not need. At 15 years in ProtecT, prostate cancer mortality in all three arms sat between 2% and 3% with no difference detected, and in PIVOT's low-risk group the absolute difference in all-cause mortality between surgery and observation was 0.7 percentage points. Pushing a low-risk man into radical treatment with a metastasis figure is this piece's other failure. The leakage and sexual cost of surgery and radiotherapy are real, and both sit side by side in "Surgery or radiotherapy: different bills".

Three questions to have asked before you nod. Which inclusion criteria do I meet, and what is my PSA density? What does the test schedule look like for the next three years, and who books it? And what would make you take me off surveillance and start treatment?

Getting a concrete answer to the third before you decide is safer than deciding first and fitting in around it.

From the physician

Whether surveillance fits you turns on the grade, the PSA density, the imaging and your life expectancy, not on how well you feel. Bring three questions to the next visit: which inclusion criteria do I meet, what is the test schedule for the next three years, and what exactly would take me off surveillance? A programme with no written answer to the third is not surveillance.
