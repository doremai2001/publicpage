WHAT COMES AFTER

How follow-up is scheduled, and when it stops

Three schedules from three societies in three different years, two of which run all the way to the end without a stopping point; and the price of one more scan, which the guideline writes into itself.

Benign Brain Tumour Guide · What comes after · 12 references
#Follow-up#Imaging#Active Surveillance#Evidence

A follow-up table is not an itinerary the hospital drew up for you. It is a recommendation written out by one guideline or another, and each of them covers only one of these diseases. Knowing which society wrote your table, in which year, and which tumour it covers is what lets you ask "why am I having this particular scan".

"So how long do I have to keep coming back for?" I usually hear this at the third or fourth appointment. It is really three questions with three different answers: meningioma has a national guideline that writes the stopping condition down, the vestibular schwannoma (also called acoustic neuroma) guideline runs to the end without ever giving a stopping point, and for the non-functioning pituitary adenoma the official answer is "long-term surveillance is recommended, but the evidence is insufficient to make a recommendation on how long or how often"[1]. Mixing any two of those three tables together is the commonest mistake on this question.

Meningioma: the last box on the table says "consider discharge"

The UK's NICE guideline NG99 (published July 2018, last updated January 2021) has a meningioma follow-up table laid out by grade. The table's own title says "possible", and the verb it uses is "consider", not "offer" — it is a suggestion, not a scheduling system[2]. Counting from the end of treatment, it goes like this:

  • Grade 1, completely excised: a scan at three months, then annually from year one to year three, every two years from year three to year nine, and after year nine consider discharge.

  • Grade 1 with residual tumour: a scan at three months, then annually up to year five, every two years from year five to year nine, and after year nine consider discharge.

  • Grade 2: a scan at three months, then another at 6 to 12 months; annually from year one to year five, every two years from year five to year nine, and after year nine consider discharge.

  • Grade 3: every 3 to 6 months at first, every 6 to 12 months after year two, annually after year five — this row has no "consider discharge" box at all[2].

Asymptomatic, incidentally found meningiomas have a line of their own: scan at 12 months, and if nothing has changed, consider discharge, or rescan at five years. There is another line under the table that settles a question a lot of people have: whether there is residual tumour can only be pinned down on the scan at three months after surgery — which is why the first follow-up scan is not booked before you go home[2]. All four schedules have the same shape: dense for the first three years, loosening afterwards, and the last box reading "consider discharge" — of the three diseases, meningioma is the only one whose guideline can write a stopping condition at all.

Two limits, stated first. NG99 covers glioma, meningioma and brain metastases; it does not cover vestibular schwannoma or pituitary tumours. And it is a UK national guideline, not an international settlement: the 2024 international meningioma consensus still lists "the appropriate surveillance intervals and modality" among the unresolved items in its future-directions section[3]. How it is scheduled in Taiwan is your own consultant's judgement.

Vestibular schwannoma: three situations, and not one box says "stop"

The 2026 imaging guideline update from the Congress of Neurological Surgeons splits sporadic vestibular schwannoma into three situations, all of them level III recommendations[4]: for people under observation, annually for at least the first three years after diagnosis and at least every three to five years thereafter, adjusted for age, size and previous growth rate; after surgery the first scan goes at three months or later, denser after subtotal or near-total resection (for example 3, 12 and 24 months) and sparser after gross total resection (for example 6, 18 and 36 months); after stereotactic radiosurgery, at 12, 24 and 36 months. Not one sentence anywhere says when you can stop scanning.

The 2018 version in the same series was worded differently: MRI every year for five years, and then, if stable, lengthen the interval[5]. Five years against three years plus every three to five — the two versions do not give the same numbers, and I am writing both. The reason is in the 2026 document itself: their own verdict on the whole evidence base underneath it is "broad, diverse, low certainty, and low quality"[4].

Pituitary adenoma: the official answer is "long-term surveillance is recommended, but the duration and frequency are not settled"

For an incidentally found pituitary lesion the Endocrine Society's 2011 guideline is the most detailed: rescan a macroadenoma six months after the first scan and a microadenoma after a year (1|⊕⊕○○); in people whose size has not changed, a macroadenoma annually for the next three years and a microadenoma every one to two years, lengthening gradually thereafter (2|⊕⊕○○)[6]. That string of symbols in the brackets is GRADE: 1 is a strong recommendation, 2 a weak one, and the number of circles stands for the quality of the evidence. Note the last two words — "lengthening gradually". It does not say stop. And this is a fifteen-year-old document.

For non-functioning adenomas after treatment, the 2016 guideline recommends long-term imaging, endocrine and ophthalmological monitoring, and then writes this down outright: "there is insufficient evidence to make a recommendation on the duration and frequency of surveillance"; the same document also says the first scan for assessing the extent of resection should be at three months after surgery or later[1].

Several years of clean scans — does that mean you can relax? A set of data on 148 people says no. All of them had gross total resection; over a median 91 months of follow-up, 12 recurred (8.1%), with a median time to recurrence of 80 months and a range of 36 to 156 months. And in people who had accumulated several years of recurrence-free imaging, the probability of staying recurrence-free did not climb accordingly — the odds of recurring rose linearly by 1.07% per year. The authors' concluding sentence is: patients require lifelong periodic imaging[7].

Follow-up is not only about that one scan

Visual fields have two recommendations pointing in opposite directions, both from the same pituitary guideline: test the fields when the lesion on the follow-up scan has come up against or is compressing the optic nerve or optic chiasm (1|⊕⊕⊕⊕); do not test them routinely in people whose lesion is not near the chiasm, who have no new symptoms and who are being followed closely by MRI (2|⊕○○○)[6]. Hormones are separated out too: reassess a macroadenoma six months after the first set of tests and annually thereafter; for a microadenoma with no change clinically, in the history or on MRI, there is no need to test for hypopituitarism[6]. What is watched in a functioning adenoma is chiefly the biochemistry rather than the imaging, and that sorting is written in "First, find out whether it is secreting".

On hearing I will be straight with you: I could not find any guideline that states how often a pure-tone audiogram should be done during observation. All I can find is the outcome side, and it can only be used to say one thing. The concluding sentence of the 2026 hearing-preservation guideline is: whatever the management, fewer than half of the patients with sporadic vestibular schwannoma who still have serviceable hearing in the affected ear at diagnosis retain it ten years later[8]. It sits here for one purpose only: hearing goes downhill with time, and it is going downhill on the observation path as well, so hearing follow-up and imaging follow-up are two separate things to schedule. The preservation rates for the three paths only make sense with their scoring system, years of follow-up, number of patients and treatment attached, and that table is in "Watching, radiosurgery, surgery"; how a hearing test is read is in "Hearing is what this decision is really about".

It got bigger after radiosurgery, and reading that is not yours to do

This is common in vestibular schwannoma. In 100 patients followed prospectively after radiosurgery, tumour volume increased by an average of 23% at three months and 27% at six, and on average came back to its original size by twelve months; the average expansion at its peak was 47% above the original, and 13 of them at one point more than doubled[9]. Another volumetric analysis of 259 people sorted the trajectories into four kinds: early pseudoprogression 35.5%, late pseudoprogression 11.2%, stable 41.3%, true progression 12%[10]; solid tumours show it more often than cystic ones, 55% against 31%[11].

The guideline's way of handling this is to stretch the time out, not to hand the reading over to the moment: imaging at 12, 24 and 36 months, and no further treatment before three consecutive scans have shown deterioration, unless the clinical symptoms are changing worryingly at the same time[4]. "The scan looks worse. The disease may not be." on this site takes the same position: the phenomenon exists and has denominators, but no imaging feature and no symptom can separate it from real deterioration in the moment. It belongs to your doctor and a run of consecutive scans; it is not a waiting period you can take home with you.

The Taiwanese schedule lists the item, but not how often

The NHI fee schedule splits magnetic resonance imaging into two items: 33084B without contrast, 6,500 points, and 33085B with contrast, 11,500 points. Word for word, the regulation column of both carries only two sentences — limited to institutions approved by the insurer, and the report must be attached when the claim is filed — with nothing at all about intervals or numbers of scans[12]. On hearing, the pure-tone audiogram (22001C) and speech discrimination testing (22011B) are both on the table[12] — but how often may they be done? I could not find a frequency provision in the whole schedule that I could cite. Not finding a listed item does not mean there is no coverage; how to file a claim is a question for the medical affairs office.

One more scan is not free, so the question next time is "why this scan"

NG99 writes the price of extra imaging into the guideline as well, word for word: there is no definite evidence that detecting recurrence earlier improves outcomes; changes of unclear significance on a scan may increase anxiety; and some people find more frequent imaging and appointments a burden and an intrusion — they feel their life revolves around their most recent scan[2]. The 2024 international consensus writes it too: surveillance imaging itself can bring significant anxiety and a drop in quality of life[3]. If the days either side of a scan are especially hard, "Treatment ended. Why am I more afraid?" on this site was written for you.

So at your next appointment, get three questions out loud: which guideline this is based on and when the next scan is due; what else besides the scan is being followed (fields, hormones, hearing, each at its own frequency); and under what circumstances I should not wait for the scheduled date. NG99 gives the general rule for that last one: meningioma patients — including those found incidentally — should be offered an appointment and appropriate imaging at any time if new or changing neurological symptoms appear[2]. The date on the table is a default, and symptoms override it.

From the physician

Two kinds of patient worry me most: the one who books an extra scan for himself every few months, and the one who thinks five years means it is over. Neither is follow-up. Follow-up is knowing which document your own box rests on, and under what circumstances you do not have to wait for the date on it.
