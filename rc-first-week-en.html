AFTER THE DIAGNOSIS

Just diagnosed, and nobody has touched the tumour yet

What the weeks between diagnosis and a treatment plan are actually deciding, why the team meeting is a Taiwanese accreditation rule, and whether the waiting costs you anything.

Rectal Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 17 references
#Getting Ready#Treatment Pathway#Tumour Board#Screening#Decisions

Here is a conclusion you may not expect: in rectal cancer, the two or three extra weeks the hospital spends on scheduling has a far weaker link to survival than you would guess. The delay the data do identify sits between the first symptom and the clinic door.

In the first month after a rectal cancer diagnosis you give blood, repeat the colonoscopy, book an MRI, and wait for a meeting you will not attend. The tumour has been seen, the report says malignant, and three weeks pass with nobody doing anything to it. From where you sit, that is nothing happening.

What those weeks decide, what cannot be skipped, and one thing unlike head and neck cancer: here the evidence for starting sooner is much weaker than people assume.

Start with the Taiwanese numbers, because they explain everything after this

The 2023 national cancer registry recorded 138,051 new cancers in Taiwan, the top ten by number running lung, colorectal, female breast, liver, prostate, oral cavity and so on; colorectal cancer, overtaken by lung in recent years, now sits second[1]. The 2022 itemised figures: 17,643 new colorectal cancers, age-standardised incidence 41.1 per 100,000, median age 67[2]. The options are many, and the price is that the information must be complete before any can be chosen.

"Isn't three weeks too long?" The data are not on anxiety's side

In head and neck clinic I tell people time matters. In colorectal cancer I cannot, because the evidence looks different.

A systematic review of 5 studies and 13,514 colon cancer patients compared the diagnosis-to-surgery interval against overall survival: four found no association, the fifth one with no clinical meaning[3].

In the US National Cancer Database, 514,103 colon cancer patients give a U-shaped curve against a reference of surgery at 7 to 30 days: within 7 days, hazard ratio for death 1.56; 31 to 60 days 1.13; 61 to 90 days 1.49; 91 to 120 days 2.28; 121 to 180 days 2.46[4]. Look at that first cell: if delay itself did the harm, those operated on inside a week should be safest, yet they resemble those who waited three months. They are the patients with obstruction, perforation or heavy bleeding and no choice — the disease decided the timing, not the reverse.

The same database across six cancers and 3.67 million patients agrees: in early-stage breast, lung, renal and pancreatic cancer later treatment meant worse survival, but in colorectal cancer only stage I reached significance, with per-week hazard ratios of 1.005 to 1.030[5].

The one rectal-specific signal sits elsewhere. A Danish population study of 740 patients split delay into symptom-to-care, referral from primary care, and hospital scheduling. Total delay over 60 days carried a hazard ratio of 1.69 (95% CI 1.01–2.83) for rectal cancer and none for colon cancer; referral delay and hospital delay each had no effect on survival[6]. Most of those 60 days go between first symptom and clinic door, not on the scan queue.

So: a few weeks finishing the work-up is time well spent; a few months is not. Those hazard ratios of 2.3 and 2.5 at 91 to 180 days are confounded, but not numbers to wave away[4]. The question worth chasing is which items are missing and what day they will all be in, not whether we can operate next week.

The meeting you will not attend is an accreditation rule in Taiwan

The Health Promotion Administration (國民健康署) has run cancer care quality accreditation since 2008, and more than 84% of Taiwan's cancer patients are treated at accredited hospitals[7].

The standards make the team meeting a hard requirement: monthly for hospitals with fewer than 50 new cancers a year, twice monthly for 50 to 499, weekly for 500 or more. At least 20% of new patients in each cancer type must go to the meeting, at least 15% (of that cancer's total) before treatment starts. Core specialty attendance must reach 80%, and colorectal core members explicitly include imaging, pathology, colorectal surgery, medical oncology and radiation oncology[8].

Does the meeting do anything? Across 11 studies and 30,814 colorectal cancer patients, discussion by a team carried an overall survival hazard ratio of 0.81 (95% CI 0.69–0.94), 0.73 in the stage IV subgroup, with no difference in postoperative mortality[9]. The honest part: all 11 were observational, and patients taken to the meeting were often younger and earlier-stage — a selection not cleaned out. So: an association, not "the meeting saved you".

What is actually being done in these weeks

The colorectal cancer guideline of the Taipei Medical University healthcare system (臺北醫學大學醫療體系), which includes Wan Fang Hospital (萬芳醫院), makes CEA, colonoscopy with biopsy, chest and abdominal CT, pelvic CT or MRI and MMR/MSI mandatory; pelvic MRI, PET and endorectal ultrasound are optional[10].

  • The whole colon has to be seen, not just as far as the tumour. In a Dutch study of 13,683 patients, 3.9% (about one in 25) had a second colorectal cancer at the same time, and 34% sat in a different surgical resection segment from the main one[11]. Missing one means operating again later. If the tumour blocks the lumen and the scope cannot pass, this moves to after surgery.

  • The pretreatment CEA. In 266 rectal cancer patients at Kaohsiung Chang Gung, a level of 7 ng/mL or above predicted a lower pathological complete response rate and worse overall survival, distant metastasis and locoregional recurrence, in both the long-course chemoradiation and short-course radiotherapy groups[12]. It is not a diagnostic test but your personal baseline; every blood test after this is read against it.

  • The pelvic MRI. This is the scan that decides rectal cancer treatment; why it is not the CT is in "The one scan that decides your treatment".

  • PET is covered by National Health Insurance (健保) for colon and rectal cancer for staging and for suspected recurrence or restaging (36,500 points whole-body, 26,500 local); as a health check it is self-pay[13]. It answers whether there is disease at a distance, not the detail of the pelvis.

Being booked in with the stoma nurse does not mean the decision has been made

Almost every patient misreads this and lies awake all night. Preoperative stoma site marking is a preparation step, separate from whether you will need a stoma. Across 20 studies, patients whose site was marked beforehand had odds ratios of 0.47 (95% CI 0.36–0.62) for stoma complications and 0.34 for difficulty with self-care, and clearly better quality of life[14].

A stoma in the wrong place — in a skin crease, against bone, out of sight — leaks, breaks the skin down, and cannot be changed by you. The mark must be made while you stand, sit and bend forwards, so only before the operation. Whether you will need one, temporary or permanent, is in "Will I need a stoma, and when will anyone know".

One thing for your family: 62% and 10%

The Health Promotion Administration's faecal immunochemical test (FIT) programme expanded on 1 January 2025 from ages 50 to 74 to ages 45 to 74, and now also covers ages 40 to 44 for anyone with a first-degree relative (parent, child or sibling) with colorectal cancer; biennial, publicly funded[15]. Official wording: screening every two years reduces colorectal cancer mortality by 40% and late-stage disease by 34%[1].

There is a gap here worth saying out loud. In Taiwan's One Million screening programme, 1.16 million of 5.41 million eligible people (21.4%) had been screened by 2009. Screened against unscreened, the relative risk of dying of colorectal cancer was 0.38 (95% CI 0.35–0.42), a 62% reduction. The authors then adjusted for the fact that people who turn up for screening look after themselves better anyway, and the population-level effect fell to a 10% reduction (RR 0.90, 0.84–0.95)[16].

Both are true, answering different questions: what happened to the people who went, and how many fewer people died because the programme exists. I give you both, because if I quoted only the flattering one you would have no reason to trust any other number I hand you. Another analysis of 77,169 patients with colorectal adenocarcinoma, linking National Health Insurance records with the cancer registry, is more concrete: the disease costs women an average of 6.0 years of life and men 5.1, and screen-detected cases recovered 2.9 years (women) and 2.7 (men)[17].

These numbers are no use to you now — you have been diagnosed. But your siblings and children are eligible today, and that call can be made today.

The few things this month that are genuinely in your hands

First: which staging investigations am I still missing, and what date will they all be in? A list beats asking how much longer. Second: which multidisciplinary meeting is my case going to, and when after it can I have the plan? Third: is the mesorectal fascia (MRF) clear on my pelvic MRI report? That one line largely settles whether chemoradiation comes before surgery; what the codes mean is in "What T3, MRF and EMVI actually mean on your report". Fourth: if I do need a stoma, can the stoma nurse (造口護理師) see me before the operation? That need not wait for the surgery type to be settled.

From the physician

The figures here are population averages. Your own timetable depends on where the tumour sits, whether the bowel is obstructed, and how the scans queue. Take a list to your next visit: which investigations are still outstanding, what date they will all be in, and which team meeting your case goes to.
