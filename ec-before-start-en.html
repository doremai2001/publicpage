DURING TREATMENT

Things to settle before treatment starts

Quitting alcohol, betel quid and tobacco; the dietitian; teeth; simulation; fitness training; mood; the catastrophic illness certificate; the family's jobs — what should be done between the treatment plan being settled and the first treatment.

Oesophageal Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 16 references
#Getting Ready#Quitting#Dental Care#Caregivers

For oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma patients in Taiwan, drinking is the strongest predictor of survival after stage, and the prospective evidence for quitting alcohol is firmer than for quitting smoking. I write quitting into the treatment plan, on the same sheet as the chemotherapy dates — not as a moral lesson, but as part of the course.

Between the treatment plan being settled and the first treatment, there is usually a week or two. Many people treat that time as waiting; I treat it as the first stage of the course — because there are a few things for which doing them here, rather than once treatment is under way, makes a big difference. Below are the items I go through one by one in the clinic visit before the first treatment: quitting, the dietitian, teeth, simulation, fitness, mood, the catastrophic illness certificate, and dividing up the family's jobs — each with its reason for being done now.

Quitting alcohol, betel quid and tobacco: I count it as part of the treatment

Let me be clear first: I am not going over how you came to have this disease. Tobacco, alcohol and betel quid are the main sources of oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma in Taiwan, and that part is written up in "Squamous and adenocarcinoma are two different diseases"; what I want to cover here is what they will do to the treatment ahead. In Taiwanese multicentre data on 718 oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma patients (a Kaohsiung Medical University team, 70% stage III or IV), habitual drinking was the strongest predictor of survival after stage, followed by betel quid and tobacco; for people using all three, the adjusted hazard ratio for early death was 1.52 (95% confidence interval 1.02 to 2.27). A hazard ratio divides the rate at which events happen in one group by the rate in the other; 1.52 is a group average for "dying about half again as fast", not a verdict on you personally; and the more substances used, the worse the outlook[1]. Among 286 squamous cell carcinoma patients at Kaohsiung Chang Gung, betel quid chewers were diagnosed four years younger, and in the group who received preoperative chemoradiotherapy, chewers had a lower pathological complete response rate and worse survival[2] — a single-centre retrospective study, which cannot be stated as cause and effect, but that betel quid may make chemoradiotherapy work less well is worth knowing before you start.

Does quitting help? The most solid prospective data at present are Japanese: 331 patients with early oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma after endoscopic resection, all instructed to stop smoking and drinking and then followed; in those who stopped drinking, the risk of multiple squamous cell carcinomas in the oesophagus fell by half (adjusted hazard ratio 0.47, 95% confidence interval 0.25 to 0.91), while stopping smoking did not reach statistical significance in the same dataset[3]. I write this as it is: in oesophageal cancer's own evidence, quitting alcohol is on firmer ground than quitting smoking. How alcohol figures in follow-up after treatment ends is written up in "How follow-up is scheduled, and the second cancer people forget". People who flush when they drink need to take extra care: for the same amount of alcohol, the cost to your oesophagus is far higher than for others — the reason why is in "Squamous and adenocarcinoma are two different diseases". For those heading for surgery there is one more reason: in a meta-analysis of 202 studies on risk factors for anastomotic leak, the relative risk was 1.25 for current smokers and 1.7 for frequent drinkers[4].

The resources are already there. The Ministry of Health and Welfare's free quitline is 0800-636363, and there are about two thousand smoking-cessation clinics nationwide[5]; betel quid, with or without lime and inflorescence, is an IARC Group 1 carcinogen, and anyone aged 30 or over who has chewed betel quid or smoked is entitled to a free oral mucosal examination every two years[6]. For alcohol I could not find an official page I could cite — tell me or your case manager directly in clinic and we will refer you. The point of this section is one sentence: quitting goes into the treatment plan, on the same sheet of paper as the chemotherapy dates.

The dietitian is not someone you see only once you have lost weight

The ESPEN guideline asks for regular assessment of intake, weight change and BMI from the day of diagnosis, with a ladder of management running from counselling to oral supplements to enteral nutrition to parenteral nutrition[7]. Reality is often much slower than the guideline: in data from a British cancer centre on 920 gastrointestinal cancer patients between 2002 and 2004, a third of those who had lost more than 10% of their weight were still not referred to a dietitian even after admission, and outpatient referrals made up only 9%[8] — old data, from far away, but "referred too late" is something I see in Taiwan just the same. For those heading for surgery, the ESPEN surgical guideline lists weight loss of more than 10% to 15% within six months, BMI below 18.5 and albumin below 30 as severe nutritional risk, and recommends seven to fourteen days of nutritional support before major surgery[9]. Whether to place a tube first belongs to another article, "Can't swallow, weight falling: a feeding tube first?"; the point here is that the date of your first dietitian appointment has to come before the date of your first treatment.

If the field reaches the neck, I send you to the dentist first

There is no literature to cite for this section; it is my practice. For cervical or upper thoracic oesophageal tumours, the radiation field reaches the lower jaw and the salivary glands, and having a tooth out after radiotherapy is a high-risk business, so decayed teeth, gum disease and loose teeth have to be dealt with before radiotherapy begins; for a lower oesophageal tumour the field is a long way from the mouth and this is not urgent. Which segment your tumour sits in decides whether the dentist goes on your pre-treatment list — ask your radiation oncologist directly.

Why simulation day includes an extra CT scan of your breathing

The oesophagus moves with breathing, and the lower down, the more it moves. A study in Amsterdam placed markers endoscopically at the edges of the tumour in 20 patients and then performed four-dimensional CT (4D-CT); the median head-to-foot displacement caused by breathing was 2.9 mm in the upper oesophagus, 3.7 mm in the middle, 5.4 mm in the lower oesophagus and 8.2 mm in the proximal stomach[10]. This is why, on simulation day, you will be asked to breathe normally on the machine while an extra set of images is taken: it is not a repeated test, it is to measure how far your tumour moves, so that the edges of the field can be drawn just right. One honest sentence: whether respiratory management (deep-inspiration breath-hold, for instance) improves outcomes in oesophageal cancer is something for which I could not find a comparative trial; all that can be said at present is the size of the movement.

Training before surgery: useful, but do not expect it to keep pneumonia away

People heading for surgery often ask whether they should train first. A meta-analysis of 16 randomised trials and 1,479 patients undergoing gastric or oesophageal cancer surgery found an odds ratio of 0.43 for post-operative complications in the prehabilitation group (95% confidence interval 0.31 to 0.58), and fewer readmissions, but no difference in death or severe complications[11]. What has to sit beside it is the largest single randomised trial: the Dutch PREPARE trial enrolled 241 oesophageal cancer surgery patients for inspiratory muscle training before surgery; inspiratory muscle strength did increase, but post-operative pneumonia was 39.2% against 35.5% — no reduction[12]. So I will encourage you to walk, to do breathing exercises, to stretch the distance you can walk in six minutes — this is about going into the operating theatre in somewhat better shape; I will not tell you it will prevent pneumonia.

Anxiety and depression are common, and saying so is part of the treatment

Among 106 patients awaiting oesophageal cancer surgery at a London hospital, screened with the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale, 34% had anxiety and 23% depression[13]. One in three — this is not you being unusually fragile. In clinic I ask straight out "how have you been sleeping these last few days", because not sleeping, not eating and not wanting to do anything will directly affect whether you get through the six weeks ahead. Every hospital's cancer resource centre and psychologists can be referred to; one word to your case manager is enough.

The catastrophic illness certificate: five years, how to apply, what it waives

Oesophageal cancer falls under the NHI catastrophic illness category "cancers requiring active or long-term treatment", as "other malignant neoplasms", and the certificate is valid for five years[14]. The application can be made by you, by someone you authorise, or by the hospital on your behalf; the documents are the application form, a diagnosis certificate issued within thirty days that carries the diagnosis code, and proof of identity — at most hospitals the case manager submits it for you[15]. The scope of the co-payment waiver is (my translation of the provision) "the illness stated on the catastrophic illness certificate, or treatment that the attending physician determines to be related to that illness"[16] — that is, the co-payments for chemoradiotherapy, surgery and the related admissions. Two things to keep distinct: five years is the validity of the certificate, and it has to be renewed when it expires — it is not the point at which reimbursement stops; and waiving the co-payment does not mean every item is free: drugs, special materials or treatments that were self-paid to begin with are outside this scope — ask the medical affairs office item by item.

Four jobs the family can divide up today

During treatment the patient's energy has to be saved for eating and sleeping, so I ask the family to divide four jobs before it starts. First, the weight and swallowing record: weigh on the same day each week and note what could be swallowed that week — this notebook is the most useful thing at every clinic visit. Second, operating the tube feeds: if a tube is placed, the family has to learn alongside — nearly 30% of people who have a tube placed never use it regularly (the figure is in "Can't swallow, weight falling: a feeding tube first?"), and a tube placed but not used is the same as no tube. Third, the medication list and the warning-signs sheet: write every drug name on one sheet of paper, and print a copy of the warning signs from "When to come back the same day" for the fridge. Fourth, a rota for accompanying visits and transport: five to six weeks of reporting in every day is more than one person can carry, so settle in advance who does which days. These four things can be divided up sitting down together tonight; there is no need to wait for the first treatment.

From the physician

You do not have to remember all eight items yourself — ask your case manager to turn them into your pre-treatment checklist and tick them off one by one. The smoking quitline 0800-636363 can be called today; most hospitals will submit the catastrophic illness certificate for you, and it has to be renewed when its five years run out. The four family jobs can be talked through sitting down together tonight.
