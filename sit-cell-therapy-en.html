BEYOND STANDARD TREATMENT

The regulator approved it — approved what, exactly?

"Approved for performance" approves a hospital, a doctor, a facility and a plan: a permit under the Medical Care Act, not a drug licence.

Situation Guides · Beyond Standard Treatment · 10 references
#Cell therapy#Regulation#Cost & benefits#Reading evidence#Newer therapies

Stage IV patients often hear about this while first-line treatment is still running, because the third line of the indication list says only "stage IV solid cancer". So two things are set out in detail: what happens to your existing course during the weeks of culture, and why those published survival figures compare with nothing.

"Doctor, the Ministry of Health and Welfare has approved this." I hear that most months, and the person saying it usually believes those words mean "proved to work".

What the permit approves is a hospital and a doctor, not a result

The rule is the Regulations Governing Specific Medical Techniques (特管辦法), made under Article 62(2) of the Medical Care Act (醫療法); cell therapy was brought in by the amendment of 民國107年9月6日 (6 September 2018), and the current version took effect on 1 January 2026. Article 3: a medical institution performing a non-trial cell therapy technique must file proof of the operating doctor's qualifications, a certificate for the cell preparation facility, and the written plan required by Article 13, obtain approval from the central competent authority, then register with the local authority before it may proceed[1] (verified 2026-08-30).

So "approved for performance" (核准施行) means this, as precisely as the statute puts it: the central authority has approved this hospital, this named doctor, this cell preparation facility and this written plan, for one listed indication, for a fixed period that may be renewed (Article 15). It is a technical-practice permit under the Medical Care Act (醫療法), not a drug licence under the Pharmaceutical Affairs Act (藥事法), and it is not a finding of efficacy.

The regulation proves that about itself three times[1]:

  • Article 13, subparagraph 8 requires the applicant to file "published domestic and foreign literature reports" (已發表之國內、外相關文獻報告) — papers other people have already published, not data the applicant generated.

  • Article 20 requires the hospital to report case numbers, treatment effect and adverse events annually. Efficacy data starts being collected after the permit. The order is the wrong way round.

  • Article 19 requires each hospital to write its own consent form setting out the "known effects", risks, adverse reactions and remedies. A drug with an approved indication needs no such thing.

The Regenerative Medicine Act (再生醫療法) came into force on 1 January 2026 (Executive Yuan order of 30 December 2025, 院臺衛字第1141035478號). Article 7 requires a completed clinical trial before a regenerative technique is performed; what lets current practice continue is Article 8 I (2), which exempts techniques already approved before the Act took effect[2] (verified 2026-08-30). Any page telling you the Act has not been passed yet is out of date. Approved to perform, reimbursed, and shown to work sit on three different levels of the system — "Approved, covered, effective: three different things".

Six kinds of cell, filed as one item, and they are not one thing

Appendix 3 lists them as one item: autologous immune cell therapy (CIK, NK, DC, DC-CIK, TIL, gamma-delta T)[1]. One line on a list does not make them one treatment:

  • CIK (cytokine-induced killer cells) are peripheral blood mononuclear cells grown outside the body with cytokines and antibodies into a mixed killer population recognising no particular antigen; the commonest in Taiwan. NK cells are expanded natural killer cells, also antigen-unrestricted.

  • DC (dendritic cells) are monocytes matured into dendritic cells, loaded with tumour antigen and given back — a vaccine: what is being taught is your own T cells, not a killer being delivered. DC-CIK gives both. Gamma-delta T expands a T cell subset that recognises stressed cells without conventional antigen presentation.

  • TIL takes lymphocytes out of the tumour tissue itself, expands them and returns them; it needs lymphodepleting chemotherapy before and high-dose interleukin-2 after, which puts it in a different weight class.

What they share: none of the six has had its genes altered. Article 2(1) defines what 特管辦法 governs as cells produced by non-genetic-engineering manipulation[1]. Genetically modified cells are regenerative medicine products; by Article 3 of the Regenerative Medicine Products Act (再生醫療製劑條例) they are drugs under the Pharmaceutical Affairs Act and need a drug licence[3]. CAR-T goes down that road, and the standard of proof is entirely different — "CAR-T is not the same thing as cell therapy".

Blood out, culture, staged invoices — and where it goes wrong in the middle

The sequence: blood draw or apheresis → transport to the cell preparation facility → about two to four weeks of culture → release testing → infusion. The facility must meet good tissue practice standards and be accredited, and may be inspected without notice[1].

Charging is staged: consultation and screening → collection and culture → infusions → follow-up, CIK commonly four stages and six doses. Cultures do fail. The published fee terms of the approved plans set out three ways that can happen and provide for a full refund of that stage where the cause was not the patient's — but once the cell product has been manufactured, that charge is not refunded[4] (verified 2026-08-30).

One more thing, plainly: it is added on, not brought in to replace chemotherapy, radiotherapy or surgery. 特管辦法 does not prohibit concurrent use, and the indications are defined by stage and by the result of prior treatment[1].

Three lines of indication, and the third is not like the other two

Appendix 3, word for word: one, haematological malignancy in which standard treatment has failed. Two, stage I to III solid cancer in which standard treatment has failed. Three, stage IV solid cancer[1].

The third line does not say "in which standard treatment has failed". That is why a stage IV patient can be offered this while still on first-line treatment. On the doctor's side, Article 12 requires a specialist in the relevant disease field who has completed the announced training course or taken part in a clinical trial[1].

Taiwan's own outcome figures have no comparison group

As of 30 June 2026 the cumulative enrolment count was 1,996 (CIK 835, DC 605, DC-CIK 403, gamma-delta T 121, NK 32, TIL 0); the authority defines "enrolment count" as everyone who signed a consent form, including those already finished, those still in treatment and those never treated[5] (verified 2026-08-30).

On the same portal, a signed column by a named physician publishes survival for those who completed treatment before the end of 2020 — figures observed in one treated group, with no comparator anywhere in the dataset: stage IV colorectal cancer, 56 patients, median 677 days; liver cancer, 36 patients, 368 days; pancreatic cancer, 19 patients, 344 days; breast cancer, 24 patients, more than half still alive[6].

These figures have no control group. There is exactly one sentence they support: "this is the survival observed in this group of people who had this treatment." They cannot be set beside any survival figure for the same cancer from anywhere else — people who can afford it, and whose bodies hold up long enough to finish a course, live longer to begin with. This group selected itself, and no second group was ever compared with it, so these day counts can only say what happened to them afterwards. That step is the distance between new and better — "New does not mean better".

Comparative research from Taiwan: one study, a matched case-control in refractory metastatic colorectal cancer, 27 against 27, DC-CIK plus chemotherapy against chemotherapy alone, median overall survival 18.73 against 14.23 months (p=0.045), no difference in progression-free survival[7]. The controls were matched from records, not randomised, so selection bias cannot be excluded — what this level can do is generate a hypothesis. Internationally the largest comparative dataset for CIK is a colorectal meta-analysis, 70 studies and 6,743 patients, overall survival hazard ratio 0.59 (a hazard ratio compares how fast events accrue in two groups, not a multiple of probability; 0.59 means deaths arrived more slowly in the treated group, not "41% fewer deaths"); but the authors note most of the studies were published only in Chinese and most were not randomised[8]. Without randomisation you cannot rule out that the fitter, better-off patients were the ones selected into the treated group, so 0.59 supports "worth testing in a randomised trial", not "having it makes you better off".

Two results get borrowed here constantly, and neither can be. TIL does have a randomised phase 3 trial — 168 patients with melanoma, median progression-free survival 7.2 against 3.1 months, but grade 3 or worse treatment-related events in 100% of patients, from the lymphodepleting chemotherapy[9] — and there is not one approved TIL plan in Taiwan[4]. The strongest randomised evidence for CIK is adjuvant treatment after curative therapy for liver cancer (226 patients)[10] — people with no detectable tumour in the body, which is not the refractory and stage IV population the permits cover.

Money: this is the one item with an official published range

Article 13(7) forces every plan to publish its fees and how they are charged, so the figures exist. Across the 181 currently approved autologous immune cell therapy plans the fee bands are: 1 to 50萬 (NT$10,000–500,000), 55 plans; 51 to 100萬, 4; 101 to 200萬, 116; over 200萬, 6 (萬 is the Taiwanese unit of ten thousand, so 200萬 is NT$2,000,000); of the 83 plans stating a total, the range is 15萬 to 240萬, median about 160萬[4]. Two things travel with those numbers: some low figures are a single stage or a single dose, not a whole course; and the band column and the plan's own fee text do not always agree. National Health Insurance (健保) does not cover this (verified 2026-08-30).

One gap, stated as it is: Article 20, and Article 24 of the Regenerative Medicine Act, both require annual reporting and publication of treatment effect; but in the 286 plan records I downloaded on 30 August 2026, the annual outcome report field is empty in every one[4].

Before you sign anything, ask for the case number

  1. What is the case number of this plan? Every approved plan has one, and the indication, doctor, cell preparation facility, approval period and fee terms are all public. With the number you can check it yourself.

  2. If the cells do not grow, or fail release testing, how is the money handled? It needs to be written out for both cases — "not caused by the patient" and "manufacture already completed". How to get a written itemisation is in "How to ask about a self-pay item".

  3. During this course, will my standard treatment be delayed or changed? Two to four weeks of culture plus several infusions happen while the disease is active.

From the physician

The stage IV survival figures the Ministry publishes have no control group; all they support is what happened to that group of people. Any checking of your own starts from the case number — indication, doctor, cell preparation facility, approval period and fee terms are public. And settle in advance how your standard treatment runs through the two to four weeks of culture.
