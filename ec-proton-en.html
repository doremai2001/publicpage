Protons for oesophageal cancer: the case and where the trials stand

Photon IMRT is already very good; protons are an engineering upgrade of the same prescription, the only randomised trial won on total side effects with survival the same, and phase III waits until 2031.

In the only completed randomised comparison, protons pushed the composite score "total toxicity burden" to less than half of photons', and three-year survival was exactly the same in both arms. That is the second of three sentences; the first is that photons are already very good, the third is that the survival answer lies in NRG-GI006. Adult proton therapy is entirely self-pay, and every number that can be found is here.

"Are protons better?" In the oesophageal cancer clinic my answer is always the same three sentences: photons are already very good; protons have one randomised trial that won on the total amount of side effects; and whether survival differs has to wait until 2031. Each sentence has a dataset behind it, and I take them one at a time. The physics of protons and Taiwan's regulations are already on the site in "Protons: precise. Then what?" and "Is proton therapy actually better?"; I am not rewriting those here, only the oesophageal question.

My position first: concurrent chemoradiotherapy and proton therapy for oesophageal cancer are treatments I deliver myself, every week; the other side of every comparison in this topic is my thoracic-surgery colleagues' operation and my gastroenterology colleagues' endoscopy. That is why every comparison comes with its primary source, so you can take it to the surgical and gastroenterology clinics for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

The foundation first: photon IMRT is already very good

Before talking about an upgrade, the basic model has to stand up. Today's standard for oesophageal radiotherapy is photon intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), and its advance over the previous generation, three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3D-CRT), is documented. A propensity-score comparison of 676 chemoradiotherapy patients at MD Anderson (413 3D-CRT, 263 IMRT): overall survival was better with IMRT, adjusted hazard ratio 0.72 — a hazard ratio divides the speed at which deaths occur in one group by the speed in the other; locoregional recurrence was lower; deaths from the cancer itself did not differ, and the difference lay in cardiac deaths and deaths of "unrecorded cause", the latter 11.7% against 5.4% at five years, which the authors read as non-cancer deaths of cardiopulmonary origin[1]. This is retrospective data and cannot be written as "IMRT has been proven to reduce cardiac deaths", but the direction is consistent. An analysis of 444 trimodality (chemoradiotherapy followed by surgery) patients at the same centre: the odds ratio for postoperative pulmonary complications with 3D-CRT against IMRT was 2.0 — an odds ratio divides the ratio of "happened to did not happen" in one group by the same ratio in the other; it is not "twice the probability", but the direction is clear — and the difference could be entirely explained by mean lung dose[2]: that bringing dose down converts into clinical outcomes was already true in the photon era. Across three academic centres and 580 patients treated with preoperative chemoradiotherapy, length of stay was 13.2 days with 3D-CRT and 11.6 with IMRT[3]. More important still: the dose trials this topic cites — the Dutch ARTDECO, the Chinese 50 against 60 Gy — were all done in the IMRT era[4][5]; "50.4 Gy IMRT with chemotherapy" is the repeatedly validated basic model, and the dose question is written in full in "Radiation dose: why higher is not better". If photons already solve the problem in your box very well, protons are an engineering upgrade of the same treatment — same prescription, better dose distribution — and the value of the upgrade rests on the next point holding up.

The case for the upgrade: the low dose taken by heart, lungs and lymphocytes

The oesophagus sits in the very centre of the chest, with the heart in front and the lungs on both sides. Photons keep travelling after passing through the tumour, so the heart and lungs always take a bath of low dose; protons stop after depositing their energy at the end of their range, and that low-dose bath can be saved. MD Anderson compared plans on the four-dimensional CT scans of 15 patients with lower oesophageal cancer: relative to IMRT, a two-beam proton plan cut lung V5 (the volume of lung receiving 5 Gy or more) by 35.6% and mean lung dose by 5.1 Gy; but the same study honestly recorded that the two-beam proton plan's heart V40 was 41.8%, higher than IMRT's 35.7%, and it took a three-beam plan to bring the heart down to 27.7% — there is a trade-off between sparing lung and sparing heart, and diaphragm movement and gas in the stomach also affect proton dose[6]. A 2023 meta-analysis in JAMA Network Open (45 studies) links the dosimetric advantage to the clinic: protons significantly reduced grade 2 or higher radiation pneumonitis, pericardial effusion and grade 4 or higher lymphopenia; the pooled hazard ratio for overall survival was 1.31 (photons worse), with no difference in progression-free survival — but every study in it is non-randomised, and the patients who received protons were plainly selected[7]. The lymphocyte item has a matched dataset: 136 against 136 preoperative chemoradiotherapy patients, grade 4 lymphopenia 40.4% with IMRT against 17.6% with protons[8]. The relationship between lymphopenia and survival is a mechanistic hypothesis, and I am not going to write it as "therefore you live longer".

The only completed randomised comparison: won on total toxicity burden, survival the same

Photons against protons in oesophageal cancer has exactly one completed randomised trial, the MD Anderson randomised phase IIB published in JCO in 2020: 145 patients with locally advanced oesophageal cancer (squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma, stratified by resectable and unresectable), 50.4 Gy chemoradiotherapy, randomised to IMRT or protons, 107 evaluable. The co-primary endpoints were "total toxicity burden" (TTB) and progression-free survival; total toxicity burden is a composite score that weights 11 adverse events and postoperative complications by severity — not the rate of any single side effect. Results: the posterior mean total toxicity burden was 39.9 for IMRT and 17.4 for protons, the former 2.3 times the latter; the postoperative complication score 19.1 against 2.5, 7.6 times; three-year progression-free survival 50.8% against 51.2%, three-year overall survival 44.5% against 44.5%, exactly the same[9]. Two things belong together: 80% of the proton patients were treated with passive scattering, not today's pencil-beam scanning; and the trial was closed before its interim analysis because the phase III NRG-GI006 had opened[9]. So the one sentence this trial can support is: for the same prescription, protons lowered the total amount of side effects patients bore during treatment and surgery, and tumour control and survival did not differ. "Protons make you live longer" cannot be written, and I am not going to say it.

The answer on survival arrives in 2031

The phase III trial with overall survival as its direct primary endpoint, NRG-GI006, is in progress: protons against IMRT, an estimated 300 patients, primary endpoints overall survival and grade 3 or higher cardiopulmonary adverse events; the registry status is accrual closed, follow-up continuing, with an estimated primary completion date of November 2031[10]. The patient sitting across from me in 2026 will not get that answer in time. Thailand has a trial of a different design (high-dose protons against standard-dose IMRT, squamous cell carcinoma), for which only the design paper exists so far[11]. Until these read out, "protons prolong survival in oesophageal cancer" has no randomised evidence to cite. The lung cancer randomised comparison in which protons did not win has already been covered in the site's two proton articles.

Retrospective data and the Japanese experience, both to be read with their conditions attached

The retrospective data lean towards protons, and the labels cannot be dropped. MD Anderson's comparison in definitive chemoradiotherapy (132 proton, 211 IMRT): overall and progression-free survival were better with protons, with a hazard ratio for death of 1.45 for IMRT in multivariable analysis; five-year overall survival in stage III 34.6% against 25.0%, no visible difference in stage I to II, no difference in toxicity, and the authors themselves write that prospective confirmation is needed[12]. That three-centre dataset of 580 patients from earlier: postoperative length of stay 11.6 days with IMRT and 9.3 with protons; 90-day postoperative mortality 4.3% against 0.9%, not statistically significant (p=0.264)[3]; the odds ratio for postoperative pulmonary complications with 3D-CRT against protons was 3.2[2]. Four Japanese proton centres, 202 patients (2009 to 2013, including 90 inoperable, about half stage III to IV): three- and five-year overall survival 66.7% and 56.3%, five-year local control 64.4%; grade 3 pericardial effusion 1%, grade 3 pneumonitis 0.5%, and no grade 4 or higher cardiopulmonary toxicity[13]; from the same database, 54 squamous cell carcinoma patients aged 75 and over had five-year overall survival of 56.2%, with no grade 3 or higher toxicity apart from 3 cases of grade 3 oesophageal ulcer[14]. These are single-arm report cards with no control group; what they can say is "protons were safe and the results held steady in these patients", not "better than photons".

Cost and access: adults pay themselves, and everything I could find is here

From 1 January 2026 (ROC year 115) the NHI added three proton radiotherapy reimbursement items, but the scope of every one of them is "patients under nineteen years of age"[15] — for adult oesophageal cancer, proton therapy has zero NHI items and is entirely self-pay. The other "proton" entries in the full fee schedule are a set of zero-point self-pay reference codes (treatment per session, simulation, planning and so on), not reimbursement[16]. Only one price can be found officially: the fee schedule published by the Kaohsiung Chang Gung proton therapy centre (page marked as updated 27 March 2026): intensity-modulated proton therapy NT$21,750 per session, moderate complexity NT$26,000, complex NT$34,500, with respiratory gating adding NT$5,000 or NT$9,600 per session, plus separate charges for immobilisation devices, simulation imaging and treatment planning[17]. Three labels have to go on this: it is that hospital's published notice; the page has no oesophagus-specific bundle, so the total depends on number of sessions, complexity and whether gating is used; and it is not a Taiwan-wide rate — at other hospitals, ask the medical affairs office directly. The Ministry of Health and Welfare's equipment status page lists 13 proton facilities nationwide, 4 in operation (data dated December 2024)[18]. The American Society for Radiation Oncology's proton model policy places primary oesophageal cancer in its first group, recommended for routine coverage[19] — that is a judgement of the American insurance system, not Taiwan's reimbursement position, and the two are kept apart. Whether to pay that money for the gap "lower total side effects, survival currently the same" — the answer lies in your heart and lung condition and in your household's finances.

Before asking about protons, let the photon plan answer one question

First ask your radiation oncologist to produce the photon IMRT plan and answer one sentence: with your tumour's position and size, are the mean lung dose and the heart dose brought down low enough? If the photon plan is already very low, the low-dose region protons would save means little for you; if the tumour is extensive, your heart and lungs were poor to begin with, or surgery is to follow chemoradiotherapy, then that saved dose corresponds exactly to the postoperative complication score where the randomised trial differed most. Take three things to the discussion: the dose numbers from the photon plan, your heart and lung function reports, and the 2020 randomised trial listed at the end together with the current status of NRG-GI006. However far protons are upgraded, they remain an engineering refinement of the "concurrent chemoradiotherapy" road, not a second road; whether to take that road at all is in "The oesophageal cancer treatment map: which box are you in".

From the physician

Before asking about protons, ask your radiation oncologist to produce the photon IMRT plan and answer whether the lung and heart doses are brought down low enough. If they are, the low-dose region protons save means little for you; a large tumour, poor heart and lungs, or surgery after chemoradiotherapy is where that dose corresponds to the postoperative complications that differed most in the trial. Take the dose numbers and your heart and lung reports to the discussion.
