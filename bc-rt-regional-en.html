DURING TREATMENT

Should the axilla and supraclavicular area be treated too?

Regional nodal irradiation improves disease-free survival and breast cancer mortality, not overall survival; and after mastectomy with one to three positive nodes there are two pieces of evidence that will not meet.

Breast Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 15 references
#Breast Reconstruction#Surgery#Late Effects#Decisions#Proton Therapy

The timing of reconstruction is settled before surgery and it narrows the road afterwards: immediate or delayed, implant or autologous — there are data in both directions and they point opposite ways. This one also draws the boundary of the proton evidence: its primary cardiovascular endpoint has not read out.

"They already operated on my armpit — why does it have to be irradiated as well?" And: "I want reconstruction. Is radiotherapy going to wreck it?" Those two questions get asked in two different clinics, to two different doctors, and they are in fact two halves of one decision. Whether the axilla and the supraclavicular fossa are treated decides the size of the treated volume; the size of the treated volume then turns round and decides the timing and the method of reconstruction. This article is only about who needs those areas treated. How far the axillary surgery itself should go is another article's business, in "How much of the axilla has to go?".

Adding the regional nodes does not improve overall survival

This gets stated backwards very often, so let me put the numbers up first. MA.20 enrolled 1,832 women with node-positive or high-risk node-negative disease treated with breast-conserving surgery and adjuvant systemic therapy; at a median follow-up of 9.5 years, ten-year overall survival was 82.8% with regional nodal irradiation and 81.8% with whole-breast treatment alone, hazard ratio 0.91 (95% confidence interval 0.72–1.13, p=0.38) — a hazard ratio divides the speed at which events occur in one group by the speed in the other, and 0.91 looks low, but the interval crosses 1, so that difference will not stand up. What did improve was disease-free survival, 82.0% against 77.0%, a difference of 5.0 percentage points (hazard ratio 0.76, 0.61–0.94, p=0.01)[1]. EORTC 22922 enrolled 4,004 women[2] and followed them to fifteen years: overall survival 73.1% against 70.9%, hazard ratio 0.95 (0.84–1.06, p=0.36), while breast cancer mortality was 16.0% against 19.8% (hazard ratio 0.81, p=0.0055). The authors put their own conclusion very bluntly: the reduction in breast cancer mortality did not translate into an improvement in overall survival[3]. Both trials enrolled populations of mixed subtype, and the numbers cannot be broken out and applied to any single subtype.

The cost can be quantified: in MA.20, grade 2 or worse acute pneumonitis was 1.2% against 0.2%, and lymphoedema 8.4% against 4.5%[1]. Surgery and radiotherapy are both risk factors for lymphoedema, and the full account of the odds, the risk factors and what can be done about it is in "A swollen arm: the real odds of lymphoedema".

Internal mammary nodes: non-randomised, and hard to ignore all the same

The Danish DBCG IMN2 study used an unusual design: allocation was by which side the tumour was on, with internal mammary node irradiation given for right-sided disease and withheld for left-sided, precisely to sidestep the worry about cardiac dose on the left. Among 4,541 node-positive patients treated with taxane-based chemotherapy or an aromatase inhibitor or trastuzumab, plus 3D-planned radiotherapy, at a median follow-up of 13.7 years, fifteen-year survival was 65.0% in the irradiated group and 60.8% in the non-irradiated group, adjusted hazard ratio 0.85 (0.76–0.94, p=0.0016). In the same dataset, the fifteen-year cumulative incidence of death from ischaemic or valvular heart disease was 0.2% on the right and 0.7% on the left[4]. This is a prospective cohort study, not a randomised allocation, and the two sides may still differ in other ways. That list of drugs — taxanes, aromatase inhibitors, trastuzumab — is here only to describe the trial population; for anyone actually taking them, the symptoms to report the same day are set out drug by drug in "Which side effects mean phoning the same day".

After mastectomy with one to three positive nodes: two pieces of evidence that will not meet

This is the most awkward box on the sheet at the moment, and I am not going to bury it at the end. The EBCTCG pooled 22 trials and 8,135 women randomised between 1964 and 1986, among them 1,314 women with one to three positive nodes after axillary dissection: radiotherapy reduced overall recurrence (rate ratio 0.68, 0.57–0.82; a rate ratio, like a hazard ratio, is the ratio of the speed at which events happen in the two groups, and 0.68 means recurrences happened more slowly in the irradiated group, not that "30% fewer people recurred") and breast cancer mortality (rate ratio 0.80, 0.67–0.95). The EBCTCG adds its own caveat: for women whose recurrence risk is already lower today, the absolute benefit may be smaller[5].

SUPREMO, which read out in November 2025, redid the question with modern systemic therapy. It enrolled "intermediate risk" patients — pT1N1, pT2N1, pT3N0, or pT2N0 with grade 3 disease and/or lymphovascular invasion — who had had a mastectomy, axillary surgery and systemic therapy; 1,607 were randomised to chest wall irradiation or none, median follow-up 9.6 years. Ten-year overall survival was 81.4% against 81.9%, hazard ratio 1.04 (0.82–1.30, p=0.80): chest wall irradiation did not raise overall survival. Chest wall recurrence was 9/808 (1.1%) against 20/799 (2.5%), hazard ratio 0.45 (0.20–0.99)[6]. Its five-year quality-of-life substudy showed that at year five only "chest wall symptoms" were worse, with no significant difference in any other domain[7].

Put the two side by side: one says breast cancer mortality falls, the other says ten-year overall survival is no different but chest wall recurrence is cut by half. They cannot be combined into a single sentence. This is exactly how I put it in clinic — this box is not settled, the decision turns on your individual features (tumour size, number of positive nodes, grade, lymphovascular invasion, age, subtype), and you are entitled to ask your doctor to say out loud which of those he is weighing.

Cardiac dose: what the technique does, and how far the evidence goes

On the heart there is hard data. A population-based case-control study of 2,168 women who had radiotherapy for breast cancer in Sweden and Denmark between 1958 and 2001 found a mean whole-heart dose averaging 4.9 Gy, and that for every additional 1 Gy to the heart the rate of major coronary events was multiplied by 1.074 (a relative increase of 7.4%, 95% confidence interval 2.9% to 14.5%, p<0.001) — that is a proportional figure, not "7.4 more people in trouble per 1 Gy"; how high the absolute risk is to start with depends on your own cardiovascular condition. The study saw no obvious safe lower threshold, and the risk began to rise within five years of radiotherapy[8]. That was the technology of decades ago; what is done now is to push the cardiac dose down, for instance with a deep inspiration breath hold. A meta-analysis of 11 studies shows that in left-sided breast cancer a deep inspiration breath hold lowers the dose to the heart, the left anterior descending artery and the ipsilateral lung[9] — but those are dosimetric endpoints, not cardiac-event endpoints. The dose has come down; the data on long-term clinical events are still accumulating, and I am not going to say the risk has been brought to zero.

Protons are my own field, so I draw this line tighter, not looser. RadComp, the pragmatic randomised trial comparing protons with photons, is under way, and its primary endpoint is major cardiovascular events, but all that can be found so far is the trial protocol; the clinical results have not been published[10]. Until that result reads out, the sentence "protons are better or safer for breast cancer" has no evidence I can cite behind it, so I do not say it. On the Taiwan side: the draft amendment to the fee schedule published in the Executive Yuan Gazette on 29 October 2025 adds three proton therapy items, 36025B to 36027B, and the first note against all three is "claims restricted to those under nineteen years of age"[11]. That is a draft; I have not obtained a consolidated text of the provisions currently in force, and I could find no reimbursement provision for deep inspiration breath hold or for cardiac dose monitoring either — those are items to confirm with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.

The size of the treated volume changes how the bill is worked out

There is a practical matter here too. The indication written for the NHI's bundled breast hypofractionation codes 36022B and 36023B is treatment after breast-conserving surgery with the treated volume including the whole breast, and it states explicitly that the supraclavicular, axillary and internal mammary nodes are not included[12]. So anyone who needs regional nodal irradiation falls outside those two bundled codes, and the charging works differently. How the number of treatments is chosen is another article: "Five treatments, fifteen, or twenty-five".

Reconstruction timing: this decision narrows the road ahead

The MROC prospective multicentre cohort enrolled 622 reconstruction patients who had radiotherapy and 1,625 who did not. At least one breast complication within two years occurred in 38.9% with radiotherapy and implants and 25.6% with radiotherapy and autologous tissue; without radiotherapy the figures were 21.8% for implants and 28.3% for autologous. Within the irradiated group, autologous reconstruction carried a significantly lower complication risk (odds ratio 0.47, 0.27–0.82; an odds ratio compares the odds in the two groups rather than counting heads), while in the non-irradiated group there was no difference between the two operations[13]. Among 109 patients in Utrecht in the Netherlands who had skin-sparing mastectomy with immediate reconstruction followed by radiotherapy, reconstruction failure occurred in 17/80 (21%) with implants and 0/29 (0%) with autologous tissue[14].

But there are data pointing the other way, and this part often gets left out. Another Dutch group compared 372 immediate implant-based reconstructions with 18 delayed reconstructions, all with post-mastectomy radiotherapy: a mean of 2.24 operations against 3.72, and 14.3 months against 38.6 months to achieve a reconstructed breast shape. The wording those authors themselves recommend is that women who may need post-mastectomy radiotherapy should be told before surgery that immediate reconstruction plus radiotherapy may carry a 22% rate of serious complications and an 8% failure rate[15]. So I am not going to write that delayed is safer, and I am not going to write that autologous is better — autologous reconstruction has costs of its own, and in MROC the autologous complication rate in the non-irradiated group was in fact slightly higher[13]. A reconstructed breast will also not be the same as the original one, and that has to be said up front.

The timing matters here: the fork in the road for all these options is before surgery. Once immediate or delayed, implant or autologous, has been chosen, the paths still open afterwards are narrower. Which parts of breast reconstruction are covered in Taiwan and which are self-pay, I could not find a formal document I could cite, so go through it item by item with your plastic surgeon and the hospital's medical affairs office.

Before you walk into that three-specialty conversation, put the questions into writing

What you can prepare today is one sheet of paper to take to the pre-operative clinic. Write four sentences on it: "On my current imaging and biopsy results, roughly what is the chance I will need radiotherapy after surgery?" "If I do, will the supraclavicular fossa, the axilla or the internal mammary chain be treated?" "Does that change the timing and method of my reconstruction — should I talk to plastic surgery before the operation date is set?" "My tumour is on the left; how will the cardiac dose be handled?"

Then add one concrete request: ask that breast surgery, plastic surgery and radiation oncology settle the question of whether post-operative radiotherapy is likely in the same room, rather than each clinic saying its own piece separately. You are in a position to ask for that, and the time to ask is before the operation.

From the physician

The trade-off between treating and not treating turns on your individual features, and you are entitled to ask your doctor to say which ones he is weighing. Before surgery, ask breast surgery, plastic surgery and radiation oncology to settle in the same room whether post-operative radiotherapy is likely.
