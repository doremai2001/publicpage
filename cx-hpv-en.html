AFTER THE DIAGNOSIS

HPV: infection is common, cancer is rare

Infection close to universal, most of it cleared by the body, progression measured in decades — the natural-history numbers laid out, and why "who gave it to me" has no answer.

Cervical Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 12 references
#HPV & Vaccines#Screening#Quality of Life

From persistent infection to a precancerous lesion usually takes 5 to 10 years; from precancer to cancer, a national registry model puts the median at 23.5 years. This article is written for the person who wants to ask "who gave this to me" — and for the partner sitting alongside, not knowing what he is supposed to do.

My position first: definitive chemoradiation and brachytherapy are the treatments I deliver every day. So every sentence about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it — not more warmly.

For this article, I am assuming you are not reading alone — your husband or partner may be sitting beside you, and between you sits a question that has or has not been said out loud: "Who gave this to me?" I have seen what that question does to people in my clinic, so let me put the conclusion first: with today's testing, this question has no answer; and the timeline from infection to disease means that using today's diagnosis to infer what happened recently does not hold up biologically. Every sentence below has a source you can check.

How common infection is — close to the shared experience of having had sex at all

Researchers at the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention built a model from the distribution of lifetime partner numbers: for anyone who has had at least one opposite-sex partner, the modelled lifetime probability of acquiring HPV is about 84.6% for women and about 91.3% for men — a model estimate, not a person-by-person measurement, but its direction matches the measured cohorts[1]. The measured version looks like this: 608 American college women tested every six months — within three years, 43% had a detected infection[2]. Having had HPV is not the mark of any particular behaviour; it is close to the common experience of everyone who has ever had a sex life.

The great majority of infections, the body clears by itself

That same college cohort measured a median duration of new infections of just 8 months[2]. A Costa Rican cohort following 800 carcinogenic-type infections found 67% cleared within 12 months[3]; and in a two-year prospective study of 4,504 women with mildly abnormal cytology, 91% of prevalent infections cleared within 24 months[4]. The small group that genuinely needs watching is the one whose infection persists beyond a year: for infections persisting 12 months or more, the risk of being diagnosed with a high-grade precancerous lesion within 30 months is about 20%[3]. How that precancerous layer is graded and managed is written in "Before it is cancer: CIN and carcinoma in situ".

From infection to cancer is a road measured in decades

The 2007 Lancet review lays the natural history out clearly: persistent infection and precancerous lesions usually form within 5 to 10 years of infection, and in fewer than one in ten new infections; invasive cancer then takes many more years — even decades — to appear[5]. A mathematical model built on Dutch national registry data estimates the median time from high-grade precancer to invasive cancer at 23.5 years; only 1.6% complete that road within 10 years, and 2.4% for HPV16-positive lesions[6]. So the statement that stands up is "usually more than a decade" — not "always more than a decade". A very small number move faster, but that very small number is around one to two percent. This timeline is the foundation for the next section.

Why "who gave it to me" is a question without an answer

The US CDC's 2021 sexually transmitted infections treatment guidelines put it more directly than any words of comfort could: partners usually share HPV between them, and "it is not possible to determine which partner transmitted the original infection"; "having HPV does not mean that a person or their partner is having sex outside the relationship"; and the timing of infection "cannot be determined"[7]. The guidelines also record something most people do not know: an HPV test can turn positive many years after first exposure, because latent infection can reactivate — in men and in women alike[7]. This is not just theory: in a two-year follow-up study of 700 women aged 35 to 60, 85% of newly detected HPV occurred during periods of no sex or a single ongoing partner, and only 13% could be attributed to a new partner[8]. A positive result in midlife is mostly an infection from years ago becoming measurable again — not something someone recently brought home.

Let me say the whole of it, tilting neither way: infection itself is related to sexual activity — the number of partners is a risk factor for acquiring the infection, and the research says so plainly[1][2]. What does not hold is something else: reasoning backwards from today's disease to a partner's recent behaviour. The timeline does not permit that inference, and the testing does not support it. Both of those sentences can be true at once, and both need saying.

What does a partner need to do? The answer is shorter than you expect

First: he does not need to be tested. The guidelines state in so many words that sex partners do not need HPV testing, and no HPV test is approved for use in men[7]. Second: vaccination can be discussed, but the person it protects is him. In a phase 3 trial of the quadrivalent vaccine in 4,065 young men, per-protocol efficacy against external genital lesions caused by vaccine types was 90.4%[9]; the US Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices recommends catch-up vaccination up to age 26, and for ages 27 to 45 makes no routine recommendation, leaving it to shared decision-making — because most adults have already been exposed, and the vaccine has no treatment effect on an existing infection[10]. In Taiwan adult vaccination is self-paid, and the current nine-valent vaccine is licensed up to age 45[11]; prices differ between providers — confirm with the clinic or pharmacist. I could not find evidence that a partner's vaccination improves the patient's own prognosis, so do not frame it as a treatment done for your sake — it is his own protection.

There is one factor that is in your own hands

Smoking. An international collaborative reanalysis of 23 studies and 13,541 cases: women who currently smoke have 1.60 times the relative risk of cervical squamous cell carcinoma — that is, the rate in the smoking group is 1.6 times the rate in the non-smoking group — rising to 1.95 in analyses restricted to HPV-positive women, and the risk climbs with the amount smoked[12]. I am not putting this here to go back and prosecute anyone's smoking history — the main cause of this disease is, from start to finish, persistent infection; smoking is a co-factor. And these are data on the risk of developing the disease, not the risk of recurrence — quitting is not a treatment. But in an illness where most of the factors were never yours to decide, this is one of the few you can act on today, and most hospitals have a smoking-cessation clinic that can be scheduled alongside your treatment.

Three questions to ask at the next visit — together with your partner

  • "Do I need HPV genotyping in my situation? Would the result change my treatment or follow-up?"

  • "Does my partner need to do anything — testing, or vaccination?" (You can bring this article along and hear the answer together.)

  • "If I want to quit smoking, can you refer me to the cessation clinic so it runs alongside treatment?"

As for what comes after treatment — whether you should be vaccinated yourself, whether smears continue, how your daughter's publicly funded vaccine is arranged — that is written in "Follow-up, vaccines and smears from here on". That is the next stage's homework. Today's homework is to let this disease return to what it actually is — a very common infection that took a very rare, and very long, road.

From the physician

"Who gave this to me" has damaged many couples in clinic, and as a matter of laboratory testing it is unanswerable. If it is fermenting in your household, take this article and its sources to the appointment and have the doctor say it once with both of you in the room; a partner does not need testing, the vaccine is his own protection, and the schedule can be asked about on the spot.
