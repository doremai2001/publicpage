HOW THE TECHNIQUE IS CHOSEN

Not three machines — three layers

Modulation, image guidance and particles are three things that stack, not three products to choose one from; which cell you land in is decided by the indication, and the modulated radiotherapy the NHI already pays for has a string of numbers of its own, each with its cancer attached.

A handwritten note turns up on my desk in clinic most weeks, with three names copied onto it — intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), helical tomotherapy (TOMO), protons — strung together with arrows, and then the question: is each one a step better than the one before? Those three names are not on the same line. Modulation is how the machine cuts the dose up and sends it in; image guidance is checking each day that you are lying where the plan says; protons change the particle that goes in at all. The three of them stack. They are not a choice of one from three.

My position first: pelvic radiotherapy is what I do every day, so every good word about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it. Implants such as the rectal spacer are not something my department provides; I write about them as an option you can go and ask about, and for cost and for who performs them, please ask that department and the medical affairs office.

Modulation, image guidance, particles: three things that stack

Modulation is a division of labour inside one family. IMRT delivered from fixed angles, volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) turning around you, and TOMO treating in the helical tomotherapy way are all intensity-modulated; what differs is the delivery. Image guidance is not a fourth technique but a layer laid on top — the European cervical cancer protocol EMBRACE-II writes "use IMRT or VMAT, with daily image guidance" as a single requirement[1]; the 2026 ESTRO technical guideline for rectal cancer puts the two side by side in the same way, saying in its own words that they "have become an inseparable part of standard care", while noting honestly that its recommendations rest on low to moderate quality evidence and expert consensus[2]. The proton layer changes the particle: a comparison of 10 plans each showed that in the pelvic field for advanced prostate cancer, intensity-modulated proton therapy left roughly half as much tissue volume receiving low dose (within 10 CGE) as photon modulation did, with no discount on target coverage[3] — that is a conclusion about dose distribution, not about side effects, and this topic keeps those two labels apart from beginning to end.

What ordinary intensity-modulated radiotherapy already does by itself

The ASTRO clinical guideline on radiotherapy for cervical cancer says in its own words that "IMRT is recommended for postoperative radiotherapy to reduce acute and late toxicity", and is conditionally recommended for definitive radiotherapy[4]. In the American SEER-Medicare prostate cancer data, use of IMRT relative to three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy went from 0.15% in 2000 to 95.9% in 2008[5]. It is the default setting, not an upgrade option. Every number below carries its cancer, its dose and its endpoint.

I normally quote only one of these in clinic — the one for the cancer you actually have. Cervical cancer, postoperative, 50 Gy in 25 fractions, a randomised trial of 300 people: grade 2 or worse late gastrointestinal toxicity at 3 years was 21.1% against 42.4%, hazard ratio 0.46 (95% confidence interval 0.29 to 0.73) — a hazard ratio below 1 means fewer events over time, and a confidence interval is the range within which the true value plausibly sits; any grade 2 or worse late toxicity was 28.1% against 48.9%[6]. The experimental arm of that trial is written in the registry entry as "image-guided radiotherapy delivered by helical tomotherapy"[7], so the correct label is "image-guided intensity-modulated radiotherapy against three-dimensional conformal", not "TOMO against ordinary IMRT". A randomised trial in endometrial and cervical cancer after surgery (278 eligible) used patient-completed scores as its primary endpoint: at the end of treatment, 33.7% against 51.9% reported "frequent or almost constant diarrhoea", and 7.8% against 20.4% were taking antidiarrhoeal medication four or more times a day[8]; in the same people a year later, high-level diarrhoea frequency was 5.8% against 15.1%[9].

The order of magnitude in two other cancers is worth leaving on the table as well. A multicentre trial of dose-painted IMRT with chemotherapy in anal cancer (52 evaluable): acute grade 3 or worse gastrointestinal toxicity 21%, skin toxicity 23%[10]. Single-centre data in 308 men having 74.8 Gy for prostate cancer: cumulative late grade 2 or worse urinary toxicity at 5 years 7.3%, gastrointestinal 6.2%[11]. The treated volume has a number too: a consistent contouring protocol plus IMRT, daily image guidance and 5 mm margins took the mean volume receiving 43 Gy in cervical cancer patients from 2,500 cc down to about 1,500 cc[1]. When you hear "this technique is better" in clinic, this string of results is what can actually be cashed in — the record of the technique the National Health Insurance (NHI) already pays for, not an adjective such as "the basic one is still all right".

The rotational cell: two ways of delivering, and I do not rank inside it

TOMO and VMAT are both rotational modulation. I put them in the same cell and I do not rank them inside it, because the evidence has wins on both sides. One reading in favour of helical tomotherapy is Taiwanese: in 93 patients having concurrent chemoradiotherapy for cervical cancer (the last 30 of them used for the technique comparison), median rectal V30 was 56.4% with helical tomotherapy, 86.5% with VMAT and 97.5% with three-dimensional conformal, and grade 2 or worse acute diarrhoea was 10.0%, 54.5% and 66.7%[12] — single-centre, retrospective, small denominator; take the direction as a pointer, not as settled. There is a reading in favour of arc modulation as well: in Japanese retrospective data on 308 men with prostate cancer, late grade 2 or worse gastrointestinal toxicity was 0% with VMAT, 7.3% with five-field fixed IMRT and 11% with helical tomotherapy[11].

Two things have to be held together. First, no randomised clinical comparison of outcomes between helical tomotherapy and ordinary intensity-modulated radiotherapy was found; everything available for comparison is dose distribution and retrospective data. The only randomised pelvic trial containing helical tomotherapy compares it against the older technique: 123 men with high-risk prostate cancer, median follow-up 161 months, late grade 2 or worse rectal toxicity 8.3% with three-dimensional conformal against 11.1% with helical tomotherapy (p=.83 — no difference visible between the arms; the larger the p value, the more easily the difference is explained by sampling alone), with the authors themselves noting the small sample and the low event rate[13]. Second, and going the other way, helical tomotherapy cannot be called "a machine with no clinical evidence" either — the one randomised trial in the pelvis that halved late toxicity was done on a helical tomotherapy unit[7]. Which one is better in your situation depends on the site, on the shape of the target and on how that hospital plans.

Protons: a different particle, and a different status

Protons are not "a stronger IMRT"; the difference sits in the physics. A charged particle stops at the end of its depth and leaves almost no dose beyond it, which in the pelvis becomes the sentence above — about half as much tissue volume receiving low dose[3]. That is dose distribution; whether it buys a difference in side effects is a separate question.

The status at the Taiwan end takes one sentence. The scope of application of all three NHI proton items opens, in its own words, with "patients under nineteen years of age"[14], and in the full fee schedule the eight items relating to adult protons are all marked "HTA item" with 0 points[15] — for an adult with a pelvic cancer wanting protons, the shape in the official documents is entirely self-paid. The codes, the points and the source of the announcement are listed one by one in "Protons in the pelvis: where the evidence stands".

Nor may the difference be shrunk down to "they are all the same"

Describing the differences between techniques as "much of a muchness" is dishonest in the same way. 21.1% against 42.4% is not a small number[6]. On the high-dose arm of the prostate trial RTOG 0126 (79.2 Gy, 748 patients), the bladder and rectal volumes receiving 65, 70 and 75 Gy were all significantly lower with IMRT, and combined acute gastrointestinal and urinary toxicity fell significantly as well; but two labels come with it — technique was not randomly assigned (the trial began with three-dimensional conformal only, and an amendment a year later allowed IMRT), and in multivariable analysis late gastrointestinal toxicity was left with only a 26% relative reduction, which did not reach statistical significance[16].

The difference is real, but it is not available in every cancer. Rectal cancer is the counterexample. In a multicentre trial of preoperative chemoradiotherapy using IMRT (68 evaluable) the primary endpoint was not met, and the conclusion reads, in its own words, that using IMRT in preoperative chemoradiotherapy for rectal cancer "did not reduce the rate of GI toxicity"[17]; an Irish phase II trial that randomly assigned three-dimensional conformal against IMRT enrolled 94 people and was stopped early because its primary endpoint, acute gastrointestinal toxicity, showed futility at interim analysis[18]. Which endpoints improved and which did not is the whole skeleton of "What you buy is side effects, not survival".

Which cell you land in is decided by the indication

A few situations earn rotational modulation or protons an extra cell of discussion: a complex target that has to cover the pelvic nodes and the primary at the same time[19]; a need to spare bone marrow[20]; a pelvis that has been irradiated before and needs re-irradiation; and prostate cancer in a man with a hip replacement, which has been studied on its own because the standard bilateral proton beams would pass through the metal[21]. The other way round, a straightforward pelvic field is already handled well by modern photon technique — the string of numbers above is its report card. So "should I add another layer" is a question about the indication, not about the budget.

On the money, I write only the part I could check. Searching the full NHI fee schedule of 6,010 items column by column, there is no dedicated code for IMRT, for IGRT or for tomotherapy radiotherapy; the words "intensity-modulated radiotherapy" appear once in the whole table, in the notes to 36015B, "computerised treatment planning — complex", at 11,483 points[15]. That is a planning code, not a treatment code, and nothing can be inferred from it about whether the NHI pays or does not pay. Whether individual hospitals have set up self-paid items of their own, and at what amount, I could find no official announcement at all — please ask the medical affairs office to confirm the item name and the amount; prices circulating in the media I do not cite.

Put the name back into its own layer, then ask the next question

Next time you hear a technical name, work out first which layer it belongs to: the way modulation is delivered (fixed fields, arcs, helical tomotherapy), image guidance (the business of lining you up every day, which is in "Daily imaging: the underrated half"), or a change of particle (protons). Once you have sorted the layers, the sentence you actually need surfaces by itself: for my field and my cancer, which endpoint does this layer change? If a self-paid difference is mentioned, ask for the item name and the amount in writing. Whether to have radiotherapy at all, how many fractions, and whether chemotherapy runs alongside are decisions belonging to each of the five cancers separately; the map is in "Which cancers pelvic radiotherapy is used for".

From the physician

Sort the technical names into layers first, then ask about the indication, and put the price last. At your next appointment ask the team to spell out which regions your treated field includes and where the complexity lies; if someone hands you a percentage for how much better something is, remember to ask which cancer and which endpoint it came from. If there is a self-paid difference, ask the medical affairs office to put it in writing.
