DIAGNOSIS AND STAGING

Ta, T1 and CIS on the report

G3 on the report and "high grade" are not the same thing, and for T1 against Ta, agreement between pathologists runs at only 50 to 60%.

Bladder Cancer Guide · Diagnosis and staging · 11 references
#Staging#Molecular Testing#Evidence#Decisions

This is the foundation article of the whole topic, and the only one that treats uncertainty as content rather than as a footnote. A bladder cancer report carries two different languages for grade, one line that decides which treatment route you take, and several judgements on which experts do not agree with each other.

A bladder cancer report carries a set of characters that look like a numbering system: Ta, Tis, T1, T2. They are not a scale of severity. They are the two sides of a line, and the line is drawn at the muscle layer of the bladder wall. What that line settles is not "which stage you are"; it is which of two completely different processes you are about to go through.

This article is the foundation of the whole topic. The terms get defined here and are not re-explained later. One thing has to be said at the outset: you will not find "the five-year survival of bladder cancer" here, because a number like that does not say which side of the line it belongs to, and a survival figure without a side averages two groups in wholly different situations into a middle value that means nothing.

The first line is drawn at the muscle layer

The T definitions, word for word: Ta is non-invasive papillary carcinoma; Tis is carcinoma in situ, a "flat tumour"; T1 is tumour invading subepithelial connective tissue, that is, the lamina propria; T2 is tumour invading muscle, with T2a superficial muscle (inner half) and T2b deep muscle (outer half); T3 is tumour invading perivesical tissue, T3a microscopically and T3b macroscopically, as an extravesical mass; T4 is tumour invading prostate stroma, seminal vesicles, uterus, vagina, pelvic wall or abdominal wall, with T4a the first four and T4b the pelvic or abdominal wall[1].

The guideline's own sentence runs like this: urothelial tumours confined to the mucosa and those invading the lamina propria are classified as Ta and T1; high-grade tumours confined to the mucosa and intraepithelial are classified as CIS (Tis); all of these can be managed by transurethral resection, and they are therefore grouped for therapeutic purposes under the heading "non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer"[1]. And then the guideline adds a line that matters a great deal: the term non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer "is a group definition", and all tumours should be described individually according to stage, grade and other pathological characteristics[1]. It is a frame drawn in order to decide treatment, not a diagnosis.

While we are here, one old word can be disposed of. The guideline carries a recommendation at the stronger grade: do not use the term "superficial" bladder cancer, and the reason given in the text is that it is inaccurate[1]. T1 has already grown into the lamina propria. There is nothing superficial about it.

The edition question can be dealt with in passing as well. The UICC's TNM 9th edition was published in 2025[2], but in the official summary of changes, the bladder row is marked "no change" across clinical and pathological T, N and M and across stage groupings[3] — the definitions above are the same in the 8th edition and the 9th. Taiwan's cancer registry report for 112年 (diagnosis year 2023) states explicitly that its collapsed stage groupings follow AJCC 8th[4].

Which side of the line people in Taiwan are standing on

The Taiwan Cancer Registry long-form statistics for 112年 (diagnosis year 2023) recorded 3,809 bladder cancers, with the collapsed stage distribution (AJCC 8th) running: stage 0 38.08%, stage I 34.68%, stage II 13.06%, stage III 9.37%, stage IV 4.82%, with a further 72 cases unknown[4]. Adding the report's own categories together, on a denominator of 3,737: stage 0 plus stage I is 72.8%, and stages II to IV are 27.2%.

The stage 0 box needs opening up, because it holds two rather different things: in the stage field, Ta accounts for 1,321 cases and Tis for only 102[4].

And there is one more thing that gets people's arithmetic wrong. Taiwan has two official annual bladder cancer counts, and they can neither be interchanged nor subtracted from one another. The registry's long-term trend file gives 2,562 bladder cancers for 2023 (1,826 men, 736 women), and the file itself notes that these are invasive cancer incidence data[5]; the long-form statistics for the same year give 3,809, comprising 1,452 registered as in situ by behaviour code (38.1%) and 2,357 invasive cancers — that 1,452 is non-invasive papillary carcinoma in situ plus urothelial carcinoma in situ, which is the stage field's Ta plus Tis, and is not the same box as the 102 above[4]. When you see a number for how many people in Taiwan get bladder cancer, the first thing to ask is which of the two it is.

The registry's "stage" and the line drawn in clinic are two different languages

This is the box I most often see read the wrong way round. The US SEER programme's stage field is divided into in situ, localized, regional and distant, and the 2016 to 2022 distribution runs in situ 50%, localized 34%, regional 7%, distant 6%, unknown 3%, with five-year relative survival of 98.0%, 73.0%, 41.8% and 9.6% respectively[6].

The trouble is in the "localized" box: it holds both T1 (no muscle invasion) and T2 (muscle already invaded). Those two groups take entirely different treatment routes. So a sentence such as "bladder cancer has a five-year survival of 73%" is not merely imprecise; it mixes people from both sides of the line together, which amounts to having said nothing. I will not give a single survival figure in this article without saying which side of the line it stands on. As for Taiwan's own five-year survival for bladder cancer, the registry centre's published survival analysis covers only the ten commonest cancers and bladder cancer is not among them[7] — I could not find an official figure, and I do not quote unofficial retellings of one.

CIS is the flat kind, and at cystoscopy it often just looks inflamed

The guideline's definition, word for word: CIS is "a flat, high-grade, non-invasive urothelial carcinoma", and "it can be missed or misinterpreted as an inflammatory lesion during cystoscopy if not biopsied"; it is often multifocal and can occur in the bladder as well as in the upper urinary tract, the prostatic ducts and the urethra[1]. Clinically it comes in three types: primary (no previous or concurrent papillary tumour), secondary (detected during follow-up of a previous different tumour) and concurrent (alongside other urothelial tumours in the bladder)[1].

One more line is worth keeping: "the diagnosis of CIS cannot be made with imaging methods" — not with CT urography, not with intravenous urography, not with ultrasound, and not with multiparametric MRI either[1]. CIS can only be found by biopsy. Which is exactly why "the scans were all normal" is not a reassuring sentence in this disease.

Where CIS puts you: in the guideline's risk stratification, all patients with CIS fall into the high-risk group except for those in the very-high-risk category; primary CIS alone should be considered high risk; CIS of the prostatic urethra is very high risk[1]. How those four boxes decide whether you get instillations is in "Low, intermediate, high: what decides whether you get instillations", and is not opened up here.

Grade comes in two systems, and they do not convert into each other

Your report may say G1, G2, G3 (the 1973 system) or it may say low-grade and high-grade (the system used since 2004). Some hospitals write both. The guideline states plainly that between the two systems there is "significant movement of patients between categories", and the example it gives is that the 2004/2022 system produces more high-grade patients because it takes in a proportion of G2 patients whose prognosis is better than G3[1].

So I am not going to hand you a conversion table, because a conversion would get it wrong. The guideline panel's own systematic review puts it most bluntly: "the progression rate of G1 patients is similar to that of low-grade patients, whereas the progression rate of G3 patients is higher than that of high-grade patients"[8]. G3 is not the same thing as high grade; the two are not measuring quite the same quantity.

What can be used is the combined four-tier version. Data from 17 centres covering 1990 to 2019 and 5,145 patients with primary Ta/T1 disease (64% Ta, median follow-up 3.9 years) give five-year progression rates of 1.4% for low-grade/G1, 3.8% for low-grade/G2, 7.7% for high-grade/G2 and 18.8% for high-grade/G3 (log-rank P<0.001); the concordance index for predicting progression was 0.71 for the 1973 system, 0.67 for the 2004 system and 0.73 for the two combined[9]. Which is why the guideline recommends using the 2004/2022 system, together with the combined system where it is available[1]. Neither system has any predictive value for recurrence; where they have value is for progression[9].

The words on the report carry uncertainty of their own

This is a passage I rarely see in a patient leaflet, but patients have a right to it. The guideline records that the diagnosis of CIS has a concordance rate of only 70 to 78%, and that for T1 versus Ta staging, and for grading, overall interobserver agreement between pathologists is 50 to 60%[1]. That is not anybody being careless. These particular judgements sit in a grey zone by their nature.

Which is why the guideline writes that in T1 or higher stages and in difficult cases, "a second opinion by an experienced genitourinary pathologist may be considered"[1]. I reproduce that sentence as it stands because "a report can be looked at again" is something the guideline has written down, not an unreasonable request from a patient; whether your particular slides should be sent is a conversation for the clinic.

The small print is worth more than the score: variant histology and lymphovascular invasion

Words like micropapillary, plasmacytoid, nested and sarcomatoid turn up on reports from time to time; those are variant histologies of urothelial carcinoma. The 2022 World Health Organization classification treats all variants as high grade; up to 14.6% of patients with non-muscle-invasive disease carry a variant, and the percentage of variant in the specimen should be stated on the report because it carries prognostic value — under the 2022 classification, more than 5% high-grade component makes it a high-grade tumour[1]. What pushes a patient into the very-high-risk box includes pure micropapillary, sarcomatoid, nested, plasmacytoid and neuroendocrine types, along with concurrent CIS, lymphovascular invasion, multifocality, residual tumour and incomplete resection[1].

On the muscle-invasive side of the line, the grade line stops being read at all — the guideline is direct about it: "all muscle-invasive urothelial carcinomas of the bladder are high grade. Therefore, grade does not provide prognostic information"[10]. On either side of the line, even the rows you read are different.

Lay the report out and ask these four at the next appointment

Two facts from the Taiwan end while we are here. Of the 3,809 bladder cancers in 112年, urothelial carcinoma accounted for 95.59% of the 2,357 invasive cancers, with a further 30 neuroendocrine carcinomas, 36 adenocarcinomas and 10 squamous cell carcinomas — when the histology is different, the whole logic of treatment changes, and that needs explaining to you individually by your own team[4]. And on the catastrophic illness certificate, bladder cancer sits in the "other malignant neoplasms" box under the heading "cancers requiring active or long-term treatment", with the certificate valid for five years[11].

  • Which T am I? Which side of the muscle-layer line does it put me on?

  • On the grade line, which grading system does my report use — the 1973 G1, G2, G3, or the 2004/2022 low-grade and high-grade?

  • Does the report say anything about CIS? About variant histology? About lymphovascular invasion?

  • Is the stage I have been given a clinical stage or a pathological one? What is the difference between them?

That last question is the next article's subject: what imaging can answer and what it cannot is in "How staging is done, and what the scan can see". On the right-hand side of the line a route has to be chosen between removing the bladder and keeping it, which is in "Remove the bladder, or keep it". The same cell also grows in the renal pelvis and the ureter, and those numbers cannot be applied to the bladder or the other way round — see "It grows in the upper tract too".

From the physician

Copy five items off the report onto one sheet of paper and take it to the clinic — T, grade, CIS, variant histology, lymphovascular invasion. Every discussion afterwards comes back to those five boxes. If the report gives only one grading system, ask which system it is using; and if the stage is stuck at T1, the guideline has written down that difficult cases may be looked at again by a genitourinary pathologist.
