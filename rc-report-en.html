What T3, MRF and EMVI actually mean on your report

Line by line: what separates one T3 from another, what the one-millimetre margin decides, what tumour deposits signal, and which tests will not change your treatment.

Rectal Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 18 references
#Staging#Imaging#Biomarkers#Decisions#NHI Coverage

This is the dictionary for the whole series, and the other articles point back here whenever they need a staging term. You do not have to read it all at once — find the words that appear on your own report, and if you read one line, read the MRF.

A rectal cancer report may be the hardest of any cancer to read: one line can carry cT3b, mrN1, MRF−, EMVI+, TD and CRM, and clinic gives you minutes. Take this home instead.

The order is by what each item changes; what changes no decision is noise.

Check the edition: rectal cancer runs on the 8th, not the 9th

AJCC has been releasing Version 9 site by site: cervix uteri, appendix, anus, brain and spinal cord, several neuroendocrine sites, vulva, lung, thymus, nasopharynx, salivary gland, HPV-associated oropharynx. Colorectal adenocarcinoma is not among them (the list has neuroendocrine tumours of the colon and rectum, a different disease). The official page: all 8th Edition sites remain current until replaced by Version 9[1]. So "your stage under the 9th edition" has no basis.

A 9th-edition proposal exists. The AJCC colon cancer expert panel's version makes tumour deposits an independent staging parameter and rebalances T against N, fixing an 8th-edition oddity: stage IIIA survival approaching stage I and exceeding stage II. In 4,257 patients (median follow-up 8.5 years, 17.5% with deposits) it gave a clean hierarchy, though stage I still did not separate from IIA, nor IIA from IIB[2].

T is depth, and one T3 is not another

T measures not size but how far the tumour has burrowed out through the bowel wall: T3 is through the muscle layer into the surrounding fat — a box so wide it has to be opened up.

German Erlangen registry, 853 patients split at 5 mm beyond the muscularis propria: locoregional recurrence pT3a (≤5 mm) against pT3b (>5 mm) 10.4% against 26.3% (P<.0001), cancer-related five-year survival 85.4% against 54.1% (P<.0001); node-negative pT3a behaved almost like pT2 (91.2% against 93.6%)[3].

The four-tier version: T3a (<1 mm), T3b (1–5 mm), T3c (5–15 mm), T3d (>15 mm). In 291 patients five-year disease-free survival was 86.5%, 74.2%, 58.3% and 29.0%; grouped, T3a-b 77.6% against T3c-d 55.2% (P<.001). Extension beyond 5 mm independently predicted recurrence, hazard ratio 1.661 (1.013–2.725), with preoperative CEA ≥5 ng/mL (2.617) and node positivity (3.347)[4].

The same label, "T3", spans five-year disease-free survival from 86% to 29%; the "T3 survival rate" online averages those four patients and means little.

An honest limit: on MRI, reader agreement on the four substages is only moderate (κ 0.428), while the decisive split, T3a-b against T3c-d, is reliable (κ 0.697)[5]. Remember which side of the 5 mm line you are on, not the millimetre printed.

MRF: the one line most worth understanding

The rectum is wrapped in fat, and around the fat a thin membrane, the mesorectal fascia (MRF). The operation takes that packet out intact, so the tumour's distance from it asks whether the knife gets round. The pathological equivalent is the circumferential resection margin (CRM): within 1 mm counts as involved.

MERCURY followed 374 patients, median 62 months. Margin safe on MRI against involved: five-year overall survival 62.2% against 42.2% (hazard ratio 1.97, 95% confidence interval 1.27–3.04 — 1.97 means roughly twice the chance of the event over the same period, not "about twenty per cent more"), disease-free survival 67.2% against 47.3% (1.65), local recurrence 3.50 (1.53–8.00). On multivariate analysis it was the only preoperative parameter significant for all three, beating AJCC TNM[6]. A review of over 17,500 patients adds that pathological margin involvement predicts local recurrence far more strongly after preoperative treatment (6.3 against 2.0, P<.05), plus distant metastasis (2.8) and survival (1.7)[7].

What does it change? OCUM used this line to decide who went straight to surgery without chemoradiation first — how, and with what result, is in "The one scan that decides your treatment". Conversely, an involved MRF is not inoperable; it means treatment first.

EMVI: whether it has got into a vein

EMVI (extramural venous invasion) means tumour cells in the small veins outside the bowel wall. MRI against pathology: sensitivity 62%, specificity 88%; mrEMVI score 3 to 4 gave three-year relapse-free survival 35% against 74% for 0 to 2[8]. Across 17 studies and 3,821 patients, pretreatment mrEMVI carried disease-free survival hazard ratio 2.30 (1.54–3.44), overall survival 1.68, and risk ratio 4.11 (2.80–6.02) for synchronous distant metastases[9].

It changes systemic treatment, not the extent of the operation. EMVI positive does not mean the cancer has spread; it is a risk marker for whether it will, and 4.11 is a risk ratio. EMVI persisting after treatment weighs more — across 17 studies and 3,932 patients, persistent ymrEMVI gave disease-free survival 2.12 and overall survival 2.21, post-treatment deposits 2.85 and 2.12, while post-treatment nodal status was inconsistently associated with disease-free survival[10].

Tumour deposits and N1c: milder name, worse disease

A tumour deposit is an isolated cluster of cancer cells in the fat, with no lymph node structure. Under the 8th edition, deposits with negative nodes are staged N1c.

"N1c" reads like the mildest thing under N1. Across 18 studies and 236,867 stage III patients the opposite holds: deposits plus node metastases carried a five-year survival relative risk of 1.58 (1.50–1.65) against node-positive patients without deposits, and deposits with negative nodes did worse than positive nodes without deposits (RR 1.21, 1.06–1.38)[11]. Hence the 9th-edition proposal to give deposits their own weight[2].

Lateral pelvic nodes: East and West genuinely do not agree

The pelvic side wall nodes (internal iliac, obturator) are where Eastern and Western practice diverge most, and not because one side is wrong.

The 2024 JSCCR guidelines are explicit: lateral lymph node dissection is indicated when the tumour's lower border lies distal to the peritoneal reflection and it is cT3 or cT4. Japanese retrospective studies report lateral node metastasis in 15–20% of low rectal cancers, five-year survival 45–55% after R0 resection; in a JSCCR database propensity analysis, five-year overall survival for pT3/T4 low rectal cancer was 68.9% with dissection against 62.0% without[12].

The only randomised trial, JCOG0212, enrolled 701 patients with no enlarged lateral nodes on imaging. Non-inferiority of total mesorectal excision alone was not confirmed (five-year relapse-free survival 73.4% against 73.3%, hazard ratio 1.07, 90.9% CI 0.84–1.36, one-sided P=.0547); local recurrence 26 (7.4%) with dissection against 44 (12.6%) without, P=.024[13]. A Western pooled analysis offers a middle position: of 1,216 patients with cT3/T4 tumours within 8 cm of the anal verge, 58% had visible lateral nodes and 16% a short axis of 7 mm or more; five-year local recurrence 10.0%, 54% of it lateral. Short axis 7 mm or more carried a hazard ratio of 2.060 (P=.045) for lateral recurrence; there, adding lateral dissection to chemoradiation and total mesorectal excision gave five-year lateral recurrence of 5.7%, against 19.5% without (P=.042)[14].

The emerging synthesis: not everyone needs a lateral dissection, but a node of 7 mm or more does not disappear because chemoradiation was given. If "lateral pelvic lymph node" appears with a measurement, ask in clinic.

MSI/dMMR and RAS/BRAF answer different questions

MSI/dMMR asks whether a drug works particularly well for you. Expectations first: dMMR is far less common in rectal than colon cancer. In an international real-world study of 1,911 patients on total neoadjuvant therapy, 1,320 had a result and only 46 (3.5%) were dMMR/MSI-H; matched, pathological complete response was 12.5% against 14.2% for pMMR, no difference[15]. JSCCR's figure for colorectal cancer overall is around 15%[12].

So why is everyone tested? Because that 3.5% may take a different road — immunotherapy alone, no chemoradiation, no operation. How young that evidence is, and why it is not yet standard practice, is in "How the treatment order is set, and why it is not a menu". One thing is enough: pMMR is the norm, not bad news.

RAS/BRAF asks which targeted drug to use in metastatic disease. JSCCR puts the asymmetry plainly: for perioperative systemic therapy RAS and BRAF testing helps predict prognosis and is weakly recommended, while MSI/MMR testing guides postoperative treatment in stage II and III and is strongly recommended[12]. In non-metastatic rectal cancer, RAS and BRAF will not change your operation.

A Taiwanese detail rarely mentioned: National Health Insurance (健保) has covered next-generation sequencing (NGS) since May 2024[16], but colorectal cancer is not on the list. The NHI Q&A says it directly: for seven cancer groups including colorectal, "expert consensus recommends single-gene testing"[17]. The Taipei Medical University healthcare system (臺北醫學大學醫療體系) guideline, covering Wan Fang Hospital (萬芳醫院), likewise makes MMR/MSI mandatory and molecular testing such as KRAS/NRAS/BRAF/HER2 optional[18]. If someone offers you a large self-pay panel, those documents are your basis for asking what it does for the decision at hand.

Four lines to circle before clinic

First: is my MRF (circumferential resection margin) safe or involved? That decides whether treatment comes first. Second: which side of the 5 mm line is my T3? Third: are EMVI or tumour deposits present, and if so, does my systemic treatment change?

Fourth: is my MMR/MSI result back, and if dMMR, should a trial of immunotherapy be considered? All four are on the report in your hand; circle them and the few minutes in clinic become enough.

From the physician

Read these numbers alongside your age, fitness and other conditions; the same T3 leads to different advice in different people. Circle the codes you do not understand and ask about them directly, especially MRF and MMR/MSI.
