AFTER THE DIAGNOSIS

Two scans, answering different questions

MRI decides whether and where to biopsy; PSMA PET asks whether it has left the prostate — and no trial shows PSMA PET lengthens survival.

Prostate Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 16 references
#Prostate#Imaging#Staging#Decisions#NHI Coverage

The question I am asked is almost always about the expensive scan. The one that decides more, and comes first, is the cheaper one — and a clean MRI still does not close the case.

In prostate clinic the imaging question comes out pointed the wrong way. What I hear most is "doctor, should I pay for that very accurate PET scan?" But MRI and PSMA PET answer different questions, and the wrong order spends the money without letting you decide anything.

Two sentences up front. MRI deals mainly with "do I need a biopsy, and where do the needles go". PSMA PET deals with "the diagnosis is made, has it left the prostate". And PSMA PET is not currently among the covered indications for PET under National Health Insurance (健保).

How many men can skip the needle if MRI comes first

PROMIS enrolled 576 biopsy-naive men with PSA ≤15 ng/mL, each having multiparametric MRI and transrectal ultrasound biopsy, with template mapping biopsy as reference standard. By mapping, 230 (40%) had clinically significant cancer. For that cancer, MRI had a sensitivity of 93% (95% confidence interval 88–96%) against 48% (42–55%) for standard biopsy, p<0.0001 (the interval in brackets is the range the true value plausibly sits in); specificity ran the other way, 41% (36–46%) against 96% (94–98%). The authors estimated MRI triage could spare 27% of men a first biopsy, diagnose 5% fewer insignificant cancers and find 18% more significant ones[1].

PRECISION randomised 500 men. Of the 252 assigned to MRI, 71 (28%) had no biopsy at all because MRI showed no suspicious lesion; significant cancer detection was 38% against 26% in the standard biopsy group, adjusted difference 12 percentage points (95% confidence interval 4–20), p=0.005, while insignificant cancers fell 13 points (−19 to −7, p<0.001)[2].

One sentence: put MRI first and you find more of what needs treating, less of what does not, and some men skip the needle.

But a clean-looking MRI is not permission to skip the biopsy

This is the sentence I most often block in clinic.

Among the 251 analysable men in MRI-FIRST, of the 94 with Grade Group ≥2 disease, 14% were found only by systematic biopsy, 20% only by targeted biopsy, 66% by both; omitting systematic biopsy would have missed 5.2%, omitting targeted biopsy 7.6%. Overall detection was 29.9% against 32.3%, p=0.38, nothing to choose between them[3]. They do not catch the same men; drop either and you miss some.

The Dutch 4M study enrolled 626 biopsy-naive men with PSA ≥3 ng/mL: the MRI pathway found clinically significant cancer in 25%, the ultrasound biopsy pathway in 23%, relative sensitivity 1.09 (p=0.17); insignificant cancer was 14% against 25%. Nearly half had a negative MRI, and the price of sparing them a biopsy was missing 4% of clinically significant cancers[4].

One honest limitation. PROMIS used 1.5T and defined clinically significant as Gleason ≥4+3 or maximum cancer core length ≥6 mm, not the same ruler as the Grade Group ≥2 of the later studies, so these figures cannot be subtracted from one another. All of them ran in quality-controlled centres too. The same test, at a different hospital with a different reader, will not give the same result.

PI-RADS 3 is the hardest box, and PSA density splits it

The system in general use is PI-RADS version 2.1, updated in 2019[5]. Scores 1 and 2 mean low suspicion, 4 and 5 high; the 3 stuck in between is the awkward one.

Among 4,841 men who had MRI plus targeted and systematic biopsy, 790 were PI-RADS 3, 19% of them Grade Group ≥2. The only factor that pulled the box apart was PSA density: at a cut-off of 0.13 ng/mL/cc, detection of clinically significant cancer was 1% against 18%; splitting into three bands at 0.09 and 0.16 gave 0.5%, 2% and 17%[6]. This is retrospective multicentre data, not a randomised trial, but it hands you a question to ask: I am PI-RADS 3, so what is my PSA density?

PSMA PET answers an entirely different question

proPSMA randomised 302 high-risk men at 10 Australian hospitals to CT plus bone scan or ⁶⁸Ga-PSMA-11 PET-CT; 295 evaluable, 30% with pelvic nodal or distant metastases. Accuracy was 92% (88–95) against 65% (60–69) for conventional imaging, 27 percentage points higher (23–31), p<0.0001; sensitivity 85% against 38%, specificity 98% against 91%; equivocal reads 7% against 23%; radiation dose 8.4 against 19.2 mSv. Management changed in 28% against 15%, p=0.008[7].

In high-risk men heading for radiotherapy the shift is larger. Of the 142 enrolled in the first year of the THUNDER phase 2/3 trial who had both conventional imaging and PSMA PET, 43 had their stage changed: 42 (30%) upward and 1 downward; upstaging was to suspected nodal metastasis in 23%, distant metastasis in 13%[8].

When not to scan belongs here too. The NCCN patient guideline phrases bone scanning conditionally: you have one if you have bone pain, are at high risk of bone metastasis, or certain test results have changed[9]. It places prostate MRI as something that can help decide whether a biopsy is needed and, more commonly, as a way after diagnosis of seeing whether the cancer has grown outside the prostate or into nodes, to plan treatment or judge suitability for surveillance[9].

Bone symptoms have one exception that does not go through the "book a scan and we'll see" route: new back pain with leg weakness, numbness, or a change in bladder or bowel function means going in the same day, not waiting for an appointment. That is spinal cord compression, and the clock runs in hours. The full section is in "Back pain plus a weak leg: do not wait".

"Management changed" and "lived longer" are two different things

This has to be said flatly; it is the easiest thing here to misread.

A meta-analysis of 15 studies and 1,163 patients found that after ⁶⁸Ga-PSMA PET the pooled proportion whose management changed was 54% (95% confidence interval 47–60%). In biochemical recurrence, the proportions choosing radiotherapy, surgery and multimodal treatment all rose, while systemic treatment fell from 26% to 12%[10].

More than half had their plan rewritten. That is a fact. But every PSMA study I cite here has diagnostic accuracy or change in management as its endpoint — not one shows that a PSMA PET makes you live longer. Whether changing treatment on the strength of a PSMA result produces better outcomes has no randomised evidence behind it; THUNDER was designed to answer exactly that, and has not answered it yet[8].

Experts argue about this too. An editorial in European Urology holds that calling PSMA-driven upstaging "stage migration" is the wrong description: reducing false positives and thereby improving accuracy is not stage migration[11]. That is expert opinion, not trial data, but what it points at is real: a more accurate machine may only be showing what was already there, not worse disease.

A bright spot is not a metastasis

PSMA PET detection tracks PSA. In a prospective multicentre trial of 635 men with biochemical recurrence, disease was localised in 75% overall; by PSA, a PSA below 0.5 ng/mL gave only 38%, 0.5 to <1.0 gave 57%, 1.0 to <2.0 gave 84%, 2.0 to <5.0 gave 86% and ≥5.0 gave 97% (p<0.001), with a histopathologically validated positive predictive value 0.84 (0.75–0.90)[12].

Among the 217 with lesion-level validation, 8% had false positives, and 13 of the 20 false-positive regions (65%) fell in suspected prostate or prostate bed recurrence after radiotherapy; false negatives were also 8%, mostly small lesions or ones next to bladder urine uptake[13]. Australian data is more specific: of 62 men whose pre-treatment staging PSMA PET showed uptake in a solitary rib lesion only, 61 (98.4%) met criteria for benign disease, and in 69.4% that lesion had a higher SUVmax than the primary tumour[14].

Sensitivity is not as high as people assume. OSPREY used ¹⁸F-DCFPyL in a high-risk preoperative population (252 evaluable): median specificity 97.9% (94.5–99.4) met its prespecified endpoint, but median sensitivity was only 40.3% (28.1–52.5), which did not[15]. The tracers cannot be discussed as one thing: proPSMA used ⁶⁸Ga-PSMA-11, OSPREY ¹⁸F-DCFPyL, and their numbers do not carry across.

In practice: a single bright spot usually needs follow-up or corroboration before it counts. And do not go online comparing SUV values with your own — that number is not comparable between machines or tracers anyway.

In Taiwan, settle the money on these two scans first

The indications for PET under National Health Insurance (健保) are listed explicitly. The NHI Administration's Medical items commonly charged as self-pay by healthcare institutions — positron emission tomography sets whole-body PET at 36,500 points and regional PET at 26,500, and itemises the tumour indications: breast cancer, lymphoma, colorectal cancer, oesophageal cancer, head and neck cancer, primary lung cancer, melanoma, thyroid cancer and cervical cancer. Prostate cancer is not among them[16].

The same document carries a line that is frequently misused: PET does not have a high diagnostic yield in every cancer, and in common cancers such as liver, stomach and prostate cancer, high sensitivity together with false-positive and false-negative misreadings often occurs[16]. That sentence is about FDG PET, not PSMA PET. They are completely different tracers, and FDG performing poorly in prostate cancer is why PSMA was developed. Quoting that line to argue "PSMA PET is inaccurate in prostate cancer" collapses two things into one.

Which hospitals can perform PSMA PET, which tracer they use and what it costs to pay for yourself: I found no citable official document. Ask your case manager (個管師) or the hospital billing office (醫務課). The same goes for the coverage rules and prior-authorisation clauses for prostate MRI, where I have no citable current version either. Ask about both together.

Four questions before you sign the request form

First, is this MRI to decide whether I need a biopsy, or to stage me?

Second, what is my PI-RADS score? If it is a 3, what is my PSA density?

Third, do I need a PSMA PET, and will it change what happens next — if not, why am I having it?

Fourth, if I am paying myself, how much is it this time and which tracer?

One more once the report is back: how sure are you this bright spot is a metastasis, should we follow it up once first? How imaging stage lines up against the clinical stage on your report is in "The few lines on the report that change treatment".

From the physician

Which scan you need depends on what decision is waiting on it. Before you sign anything, ask what changes if the result is positive and what changes if it is negative; if the answer is nothing either way, the scan can wait. For PSMA PET availability, tracer and self-paid cost in Taiwan, ask your case manager or the hospital billing office.
