Where the staging chart puts you

BCLC is a map, not a sentencing document: its own text says the final decision belongs to the physician and the multidisciplinary team — and stage B above all is a spectrum, not a box.

Liver Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 9 references
#Staging#Decisions#Tumour Board

In data from ten high-volume centres across East and West, half of the liver resections fell outside what the guidelines of the day recommended; Taiwan's consensus states, at 100% agreement, that the intermediate stage is a heterogeneous group. What the staging chart does not say is often where your answer lies.

"Doctor, the internet says I'm stage B — does stage B mean I can't have surgery any more?" In clinic, the staging chart keeps being read as a sentencing document: people look up which box they fall into and assume the treatment written in that box is the only road. This article is about what that chart is actually for — and about the few lines its own authors wrote, which patients almost never get to see.

My position first: stereotactic body radiotherapy and proton therapy for liver cancer are treatments I deliver myself — and much of it is self-paid. The other side of every comparison in this topic is my colleagues' surgery and ablation. That is why every comparison comes with its primary source, so you can take it to the surgical and hepatology clinics for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

What the BCLC chart is actually doing

The most widely used liver cancer staging in the world is the Barcelona system (BCLC): it sorts patients into five stages — 0, A, B, C and D — and attaches a first-line treatment recommendation to each one, so whichever box you land in, the chart shows a default road out of it. The 2022 version made two important updates: the liver-function half now looks at decompensation and ALBI (the vocabulary covered in "You have two diseases: the cancer and the cirrhosis"), and alpha-fetoprotein was brought into consideration[1]. Versions first: the 2026 update has been published, but I could not obtain the original text, so this article's citations stop at the 2022 version[2]; I likewise could not obtain the original text of the latest liver cancer guideline of the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO), so I do not cite it.

The chart's own authors say the final decision is not on the chart

Few people know that the original text of BCLC 2022 contains these sentences. It writes: clinical guidelines and algorithms present the current state of knowledge, but the final decision has to be made by the responsible physician and the tumour team; treatment decisions should not be based solely on a simplified chart, but are a complex process requiring individualised judgement and expertise[1]. It even builds in a formal concept called treatment stage migration: when a patient's individual circumstances make the preferred treatment of their own box unsuitable, it is legitimate to use the treatment of a "later" box — tumour progression or treatment side effects can both move the recommendation to another box even when the stage itself has not changed[1]. So reading the chart as a sentencing document actually goes against the chart's own text. It is a map: it marks your position and a default route. How the road gets walked is decided by someone who can see all of your circumstances.

Stage B is a spectrum, not a box

The biggest arguments are about the intermediate stage (stage B). BCLC itself concedes that tumour burden within this stage can vary enormously, and that prognosis is further shaped by alpha-fetoprotein and liver function[1]; back in 2012 an international expert panel proposed cutting stage B into four subclasses, on the grounds that "this is a highly heterogeneous group of patients"[3]. Taiwan's official position is the same: the Taiwan Liver Cancer Association's 2025 consensus on intermediate-stage liver cancer opens with a statement carried at 100% agreement — intermediate-stage liver cancer is a heterogeneous group, and treatment should be tailored to tumour burden and liver function; within it, patients with limited tumour burden can be considered for curative-intent options, including resection, ablation, or transplant under extended criteria[4]. The language Taiwanese physicians actually use is tumour-burden boundaries like up-to-7 and up-to-11: within the same stage B, a burden inside the line and one beyond it walk different roads — and for patients beyond up-to-11, the consensus strongly advises against embolisation[4].

Practice already walks outside the chart — here is what the evidence looks like

The surgical world's field report is blunt. Ten high-volume centres across East and West, 2,046 patients undergoing liver resection: 36% were BCLC stage B and 14% stage C — half of the operations fell outside what the guidelines of the day recommended; five-year survival after resection in stage B was 57% (retrospective data, in highly selected patients), and the authors' conclusion was that current practice extends well beyond the guidelines and the guidelines should be updated accordingly[5]. There is a randomised trial too: a single centre in China randomised 173 patients with multiple liver cancers "beyond the Milan criteria but resectable" to resection or embolisation, with three-year overall survival of 51.5% against 18.1% (a predominantly hepatitis B population at a single centre — this cannot be extrapolated into "stage B should all have surgery")[6]. Hong Kong went as far as building its own staging system (HKLC, from more than 3,800 patients, retrospective and predominantly hepatitis B): it assigns a portion of BCLC stage B and C patients to aggressive treatment, and among patients BCLC called stage B but HKLC called stage II, five-year survival with curative-intent treatment was 52.1%, against 18.7% with embolisation — a system never validated in Western populations, as its own authors note[7]. What these pieces of evidence say together is not "the staging is wrong". It is: within the same box there is a selected group of patients who walked a road the chart does not draw, and the results held up.

Asia's other reading, and where Taiwanese patients actually stand

The Asian Pacific Association for the Study of the Liver's 2017 guideline is not entirely in step with the West: in Asian countries where local therapy is the mainstay, patients with grade A liver function are not prioritised for transplant; and confined to patients within the Milan criteria with Child-Pugh A, five-year survival after resection can exceed 70%[8]. That is a difference in ordering, not a statement that transplant does not matter — a young patient with poor liver function who meets the criteria should not have the transplant assessment skipped, and that question belongs to "Transplant: the one option that treats both diseases". Where Taiwanese patients actually stand has cross-national registry data: in the BRIDGE study of fourteen countries and more than 18,000 people, patients in Taiwan and Japan were most often diagnosed at the very early and early stages (BCLC 0/A) — about 70% — while the most common stage at diagnosis in North America, Europe, China and Korea was stage C; Taiwan's most common first-line treatment was resection (the Taiwan data come from a single centre; the authors state the site was chosen as representative, and I label that here all the same)[9]. As for how the different guidelines word their positions on stereotactic body radiotherapy — that is precisely my home ground, and it is laid out verbatim, guideline against guideline, in the two B-group articles; I will not steal that scene here.

Take your box into the consulting room

At your next appointment, use the chart the way it was meant to be used, with three questions. First: "Which box am I in? And what put me there — the number and size of the tumours, or my liver function?" Two people can enter the same box for different reasons, and the responses differ. Second: "Has my case been discussed by a multidisciplinary team? Have options beyond 'this box's default treatment' been considered — both more aggressive and more conservative ones?" BCLC's own text says the final decision belongs to the physician and the team[1], and that is a sentence a patient is entitled to quote. Third: "If I am stage B, which side of up-to-7 is my tumour burden on?" That question makes the conversation concrete at once. And staging is not decided once for life — it gets reassessed after treatment, and the rhythm of follow-up is in "Follow-up and AFP: how often to scan".

From the physician

If you have been told you are stage B, ask one question at your next visit: which side of up-to-7 is my tumour burden on? That question makes the conversation concrete at once.
