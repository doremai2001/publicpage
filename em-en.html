ENDOMETRIAL CANCER

Endometrial Cancer GuideFrom the pathology report to years after treatment — one question per article.

Most people with endometrial cancer will be well, and that has to be said plainly — but it must never turn into "so it hardly matters what you do". The two things that do the most harm in this cancer are overtreatment and delay: on one side a low-risk woman irradiated for no gain (PORTEC-1's complications, 25% against 6%), on the other a woman who bled for six months before coming in. These seventeen articles follow the order you will actually walk: diagnosis and staging, treatment and the evidence, the situations that need their own answer, and what comes after. My position first: vaginal brachytherapy and pelvic radiotherapy are the part I deliver myself, and the other side of every comparison here is my gynaecological-oncology colleagues' surgery and my gynaecological-oncology and medical-oncology colleagues' drugs — so the four articles that compare them open with the same declaration of interest, and every comparison carries its primary source so you can take it back to the gynaecological oncology clinic for a second opinion. Note the order in this cancer: surgery usually comes first, and the decision that matters is made afterwards, with the pathology report in your hand. Where the evidence is weak I say so plainly, and where a number could not be found I say that too.

1Diagnosis and staging

Why bleeding after the menopause cannot be waited out, how the four molecular groups are told apart, what FIGO 2023 changed and what it did not, and what the operation removes and does to the lymph nodes.

Bleeding after the menopause is not something to wait out
Of the women assessed in clinic for postmenopausal bleeding, about one in ten is eventually diagnosed with endometrial cancer; "most people do not have cancer" and "this symptom should not be left" are two sentences that can both be true at once.

Two kinds of endometrial cancer, now four molecular groups
The old two-type split looked at the patient's constitution; the new four groups look at the tumour's genetics — what the extra MMR, p53 and POLE lines on the report each mean, in one go.

The staging changed; your treatment does not change with it
The number of sub-stages went from 9 to 21, and between two and three in ten women move stage; but the randomised trials that decide radiotherapy and chemotherapy enrolled patients under the old staging.
17 references
#Staging#Evidence#Molecular Testing#Decisions
What the operation removes, and what happens to the lymph nodes
Total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy is the standard extent; the nodes are the hard part — taking them all out has not made anyone live longer, and the sentinel procedure misses roughly 3% to 4% of the patients who do have nodal metastases.

2Treatment and the evidence

Whether radiotherapy is still needed after the operation, what the vault-only kind actually involves, what adding chemotherapy buys and costs, how molecular classification changes those decisions, and who immunotherapy really helps.

After the operation, do you still need radiotherapy
Locoregional recurrence down from 14% to 4%, no difference in overall survival, complications 25% against 6% — PORTEC-1's three figures have to be read together.
9 references
#Evidence#Decisions#Side Effects#Staging
Vaginal brachytherapy: the smallest field there is
PORTEC-2's vaginal recurrence result is non-inferiority, not sameness; acute gastrointestinal toxicity 12.6% against 53.8%, and 10-year pelvic recurrence 6.3% against 0.9%.
9 references
#Brachytherapy#Treatment Pathway#Side Effects#Sexual & Fertility
Adding chemotherapy: what it buys and what it costs
PORTEC-3 gained seven percentage points of 10-year overall survival, with the benefit concentrated in stage III and serous disease; the price is grade 3 or worse adverse events, 12% against 60%.
9 references
#Chemotherapy#Evidence#Side Effects#Decisions
How molecular classification changes your treatment
The evidence for escalating p53-abnormal disease is a post-hoc analysis; in PORTEC-4a's molecular profile arm, 46% (168/367) were judged favourable and had no adjuvant treatment.
10 references
#Molecular Testing#Evidence#Decisions#Clinical Trials
Immunotherapy: who does it actually help
All four phase 3 trials report split by MMR status: dMMR and pMMR are too far apart to be discussed together, and the trial in the adjuvant setting did not meet its primary endpoint.

3Special situations

What it takes to keep the uterus and have a child, how much weight and diabetes actually account for, what a Lynch result means for you and your family, and the hormone question after treatment.

Keeping the uterus to have a child: what it takes
The entry conditions have to hold in full, and the guideline admits that not one of its recommendations is Level I evidence; complete response about 79.7%, recurrence 35.3%, live birth 20.5%.
18 references
#Fertility#Endocrine Therapy#Decisions#Evidence
Weight, diabetes and this cancer
Relative risk about 1.6 per 5 kg/m² of BMI, and a population attributable fraction of 34.0% in UK women; weight loss lowering incidence has observational evidence, weight loss lowering recurrence has no randomised trial.
20 references
#Nutrition#Exercise#Prevention#Evidence
Lynch syndrome: what the test means for you and your family
Family history alone would miss 43%, which is why the guidelines want universal testing; but only 15% of those with abnormal immunohistochemistry carry a germline mutation, and the two guidelines disagree on surveillance and on PMS2.
8 references
#Genetics & Family#Screening#Molecular Testing#Decisions
Hormones after treatment
There is one randomised trial, and it closed early with 41% adherence; local or systemic, early or later stage, uterus present or not — the three give different answers.

How follow-up is scheduled, and what to watch for
The guideline's follow-up section is four sentences, all four graded expert opinion, and it states outright that there is no evidence follow-up visits improve overall survival; what actually holds follow-up up is the other half it catches.

If it comes back, can it still be treated
Recurrence is not one disease; it is three situations. Where it is, and whether that area has been irradiated before, decide completely different numbers — and the three situations have denominators of very different sizes.

Lower-limb lymphoedema: prevention and treatment
Reported incidence runs from 1.3% to 49.4%, and the width of that range comes from the assessment method, not from the operation. This one covers how it is measured first, then how to prevent and treat it, and what Taiwan's clauses actually say.

Life after treatment
Non-cancer causes account for 41.5% of the deaths in this group. Exercise, weight loss and dilators rest on evidence of very different strengths, and each is labelled separately rather than blurred together.

This is general patient education and does not replace a consultation. Treatment for endometrial cancer depends at the same time on histology, grade, stage, molecular group and what the operation found, and the same stage leads to different recommendations in different people; decisions about your own treatment belong with your gynaecological oncologist and the multidisciplinary team.
