AFTER THE DIAGNOSIS

What the first month after diagnosis looks like

What is actually moving in the first month after diagnosis: imaging, assessment of the rest of the colon, the multidisciplinary team meeting, the catastrophic illness certificate — and why "when is my operation" has no standard answer.

Colon Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 11 references
#Getting Ready#Treatment Pathway#Tumour Board#Imaging#NHI Coverage

The catastrophic illness certificate takes effect on the date you file it, the NHI Administration has 14 days excluding holidays to decide, and for colon cancer the certificate runs for five years. This article puts that month's examinations, meetings and paperwork into order, and is straight about which of them cannot be hurried.

Most people walk out of the clinic on the day of diagnosis holding a scan booking slip and one instruction: go and get the imaging done. For the next two or three weeks it feels as though nothing at all is happening, while the cancer has spent another twenty days sitting inside you. I know that particular anxiety well.

So here is what is actually moving during that month. Once you know what each step is for, waiting becomes waiting with content in it, and you also know who to chase when one of the boxes gets stuck.

The part of the colon the scope never saw gets booked before your operation

If your colonoscopy could not be completed because the tumour blocked the way, the rest of the colon almost always has to be assessed before surgery. Among 13,683 patients with colorectal cancer in the Rotterdam cancer registry in the Netherlands, 534 (3.9%) had a second primary tumour at the same time — one person in every 25 — and in 34% of them the two tumours sat in different surgical resection segments[1]. Different segments means something very concrete: if nobody knows in advance, the first operation takes out too little, which amounts to operating twice. In that dataset, men and people over 70 were at higher risk (odds ratios 1.54 and 1.83; an odds ratio divides one group's "happened versus didn't happen" ratio by the other group's, and is not a multiple of probability)[1].

When the scope genuinely cannot get past, CT colonography is used instead to complete the assessment. A meta-analysis comparing studies that used CT colonography with those that did not found synchronous colorectal cancer in 5.7% with it against 3.9% without, a statistically significant difference[2]. Same kind of patients; look a bit more carefully and you find a few more. The ones missed where no extra assessment was done do not go away.

Why your doctor hasn't booked you a PET scan

"I hear a PET scan can see the whole body — should I pay for one?" I usually start by reading out the wording of the NHI fee schedule, because it sets the conditions out plainly: the tumour indications for whole-body PET (26072B) and regional PET (26073B) do include staging and suspected recurrence or restaging in colon and rectal cancer, but with a rider — staging must not have been achievable with CT, MRI, nuclear medicine scans and the like, or those examinations must be judged insufficient to provide the information needed for treatment, and the reason it is necessary has to be documented in the medical record[3]. It is the back-up, not the front line.

The evidence does not sit on the "one more scan for peace of mind" side either. A randomised trial across 9 hospitals and 21 surgeons in Ontario, Canada, enrolled patients with colorectal liver metastases judged resectable on imaging; of the 263 who had a PET scan before surgery, only 21 (8.0%) had their surgical plan changed by it, and survival did not differ between the groups. The authors' own conclusion was that this calls the value of PET in this setting into question[4]. That conclusion is confined to preoperative imaging of liver metastases already judged resectable, and cannot be extrapolated into PET being useless in general.

There is also a cost that gets mentioned less often. The NHI Administration's public information sheet states that using PET to screen for cancer must be paid for out of pocket, and that one scan delivers an ionising radiation dose of roughly 6 to 10 millisieverts, equivalent to 100 to 160 chest X-rays[5]. As for the reimbursement conditions for the CT and MRI used in staging, and whether prior review is required, I did not verify a provision I could cite this time, so that is one to confirm with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.

Your case will be discussed at a meeting you are not in

A multidisciplinary team meeting (MDT) is surgery, medical oncology, radiation oncology, radiology and pathology looking at the same case together. About the quality of the evidence for this I have to be straight with you: there are no randomised trials, only observational data. At one high-volume centre in England, among 4,617 colorectal cancer patients discussed by the team between 2003 and 2016, comparing three time periods, emergency resections fell from 15.5% to 9.0%, 90-day mortality from 14.8% to 10.7%, and two-year survival rose from 58.6% to 65%[6]. That is one hospital compared with itself across more than a decade, during which surgical technique, anaesthesia and drugs were all changing, so the improvement cannot be attributed to the team meeting as a single cause. A population-based study from northern Italy is a second piece of observational data pointing the same way[7].

What can be said is this: it is associated with better outcomes; it is not something that raises survival. How hospitals in Taiwan run these meetings, and whether your case will be scheduled into one, I could not find an official document I could cite — ask your case manager.

The catastrophic illness certificate: 14 working days, and it counts from the day you file

This is the thing most worth dealing with this month, and the one most often left until discharge. Under Article 2 of the Regulations for Exempting Insured Persons from Cost Sharing under the National Health Insurance (amended 16 September 2024), the application needs the application form, a medical certificate of diagnosis (with the ICD code entered in the diagnosis field), a medical record summary or examination reports as supporting evidence, and a photocopy of proof of identity. You may file it yourself, or have another person, a hospital or a clinic act as your agent. One detail here will otherwise cost you a wasted trip: the certificate of diagnosis is valid for 30 days from the date it is issued, and will not be accepted after that[8].

Article 3 requires the insurer to decide and notify within 14 days of receiving the application documents (excluding holidays), with time spent supplying missing documents deductible, and the approval recorded on your NHI card. Article 5 gives the date that really matters: the certificate takes effect on the date the application was submitted; where the validity period is two years or more, you may reapply up to three months before it expires[8]. File a day earlier and it starts a day earlier, which is one of the few parts of this you control. For malignant tumour of the colon the certificate runs for five years — under Appendix 1 it falls under other malignant tumours within the category of cancers requiring active or long-term treatment[9]. There are three ways to file: by post or in person, by you or an agent; online by the hospital through the NHI's information service network; or at an NHI Administration regional division[10].

It does not mean everything is free. Article 6 frames the exemption tightly: treatment for the illness named on the certificate, or treatment the treating physician judges related to it; other treatment given by the same physician at the same visit when attending for the catastrophic illness; other specialties' treatment given alongside during an admission for the catastrophic illness. Where the application is approved during an admission, cost sharing is waived from the first day of that admission[8]. Self-pay items and the difference on a private room are not inside that frame.

"When can I have the operation?" — the data cannot give you the answer you want

I know you want to hear a number of days. The honest answer is that there isn't one. The US National Cancer Data Base holds data on 514,103 patients with non-metastatic colon cancer. Taking 7 to 30 days from diagnosis to surgery as the reference, the longer the wait the higher the risk of death: the hazard ratio was 1.13 at 31 to 60 days, 1.49 at 61 to 90, 2.28 at 91 to 120 and 2.46 at 121 to 180. A hazard ratio is the ratio of the rates at which events occur in two groups: 1.13 means 13% faster, 2.46 means about one and a half times faster. But in the same dataset, the risk was also raised in people operated on within 7 days (1.56)[11]. These are averages worked out from hundreds of thousands of people, and statistically they are all very "significant"; significant is not the same as transferable to you, for the reason in the next paragraph.

When both ends look worse, that usually means the waiting itself is not what caused it. The ones operated on very fast are mostly obstruction or perforation emergencies; the ones who wait a long time are often people in poor condition, with several other illnesses, who need other problems dealt with first. This observational data cannot separate "the wait" from "why there was a wait", so it cannot be used to set a scheduling target, and I am not going to take it to the operating theatre to push for a slot. How long it takes from diagnosis to surgery in Taiwan, I could not find official statistics from the Ministry of Health and Welfare or the NHI Administration; it varies a great deal between hospitals, and asking your case manager will tell you more than any number here.

Whether you will need a temporary stoma is written up in "How long until your bowel settles down"; how much bowel is removed and by which approach belongs to "What the node count really decides". How to read the pathology or imaging report in your hand is in "Where T3N1 actually comes from".

The one sentence to get an answer to before this month is out

It is this: "When is my treatment expected to start, and if it can't be scheduled, what is the alternative plan?" Ask your case manager, and don't wait for the next clinic appointment. While you are at it, settle these too:

  • Was the whole colon seen at colonoscopy? If not, when and how will the remaining part be assessed?

  • Has the catastrophic illness certificate application gone in? What date was the certificate of diagnosis issued (remember the 30-day validity)?

  • Will my case go to a multidisciplinary team meeting, and roughly when?

From the physician

Hospitals differ a great deal in how fast they schedule, so what this article gives is the order of events, not a number of days. Before the end of today, ring your case manager and ask two things: has the catastrophic illness certificate application gone in, and what date was the certificate of diagnosis issued — that piece of paper is valid for 30 days from the date of issue, and after that it has to be issued again.
