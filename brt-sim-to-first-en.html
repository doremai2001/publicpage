From planning scan to first treatment

What the planning scan is doing, how the breath-hold is trained, what happens at the first treatment; as for how many days until it starts and how many minutes you lie there, I could find no citable source, so no number is given here.

The only time figure in this article I can put a source to is the deep inspiration breath-hold training session, 15 to 30 minutes. The three patients most want to know — how many days until it starts, how long one session takes, how many seconds one breath-hold lasts — have no general figure in the literature, so they do not appear here either.

On planning day, most people arrive expecting treatment to start. What actually happens over the afternoon is three things: you lie on a hard board in the position you will be asked to hold for the rest of the course, you have one CT scan that will never come back to you as a report, and you leave with a few ink dots smaller than a mole. Then the desk says: go home, we will call you. Between that day and the day you first lie on the treatment couch there is a quiet stretch. This article is about that stretch — what the planning scan is doing, how the breath-hold is trained, what happens at the first treatment; and as for the time figures everyone most wants to know, I will tell you honestly which ones I could find no citable source for, so I am not giving them.

My position first: every technique compared in this topic — ordinary intensity-modulated radiotherapy, TOMO, deep inspiration breath-hold — has a self-paid component among the more expensive options, and all of them are delivered in my own department; protons mean a referral elsewhere. So every "better" here comes with its primary source and the level of evidence behind it, and on cost I cite the official schedule where one exists and tell you to ask the medical affairs office where it does not. Concluding that the basic option is enough for you is a completely reasonable conclusion.

The planning CT is not looking for the cancer

The first step of radiotherapy is not the beam; it is acquiring a set of images made for treatment. The CT simulation is not there to diagnose anything. It fixes you in a position you will have to reproduce every day, and captures a three-dimensional map of your body in that position; the team then draws, slice by slice, the volume to be treated and the organs to be avoided. Anyone being set up for deep inspiration breath-hold has at least two scans that day: one in free breathing and one in deep inspiration breath-hold[1] — put the two side by side and you can see how far the heart has moved away from the field when the lungs are full.

Before the session ends, lines and marks go on the skin, and most hospitals leave a few small permanent ink dots. Those dots are the coordinates for the daily set-up: the typical arrangement described in the literature combines tattoo-point set-up with image guidance and optical surface tracking, several methods used together[1]. How many dots you get, and what happens when a drawn line fades, differs from hospital to hospital — go by what your own hospital tells you. The one universal rule is this: do not top the lines up yourself and do not scrub them off yourself. If something changes, tell the radiation therapists.

You hold the breath; the machine only keeps watch

Deep inspiration breath-hold is often imagined as the machine breathing for you. It is the other way round: the one who breathes in, holds, and stays still is you. The equipment monitors whether you have stopped where you are supposed to stop, and cuts the beam if you drift. The literature describes several arrangements: one uses a breath-hold device with a mouthpiece and nose clip, with a valve that closes once you reach the set depth; one is a voluntary breath-hold, run on the radiation therapist's instructions with marker points or body-surface position monitoring; and one places a small marker box on the chest or abdomen for real-time tracking, with some systems letting you watch your own breathing trace on a screen or through video goggles and bring the line into the target band yourself[2][1]. The safety design common to all of them is a very narrow gate: the tolerance around the inspiration position is typically only 2 to 5 millimetres, and the moment you fall outside it the machine stops the beam automatically — irradiation happens only while you are holding steady in deep inspiration[2]. How many seconds is each breath held for? The settings differ between platforms and between studies, and I could find no citable general figure, so I am not giving one; your radiation therapist will set it from how your practice goes.

How long the practice takes: the only time figure in this article I can source

The three times patients ask about most — how many days between the planning scan and the start, how many minutes one treatment takes, how many seconds one breath-hold lasts — are three I could find no citable general figure for in the literature. One time figure is available: the training session. A systematic review covering 38 studies reports that the common practice is to set aside a period before the planning scan for you to learn the breath-hold manoeuvre, and among studies that state a duration, a typical training session runs 15 to 30 minutes; some centres give written instructions first and then run the simulation[2]. Note that this is how long the practice takes, not how long the treatment takes; the two are not interchangeable. The figure comes from a review of literature published between 2000 and 2014, and local workflows differ, so go by what your hospital has arranged.

During treatment proper, you breathe in and hold again before each field, and one session repeats that several times over; the consistent observation across the literature is that patient compliance is high[2] — most people can learn it. The other half has to be said just as plainly: in one study of 72 patients who had already been pre-selected and trained, about 30% still could not complete the breath-hold planning scan[3]. Not managing it is not your fault and is not a failure to cooperate — the team plans it another way, going back to heart-sparing under free breathing. As for why the effort is worth making at all: a meta-analysis in left-sided breast cancer treated with volumetric modulated arc therapy found that deep inspiration breath-hold, compared with free breathing, lowers the dose received by the heart and the left anterior descending artery (LAD)[4] — that is dosimetric evidence, meaning a measurable fall in dose, not a reduction in cardiac events. Where that line sits, why the left side is a particular question, and what the cost status is: the full argument is in "Heart dose: the core question on the left side".

Why there is still a wait once the lines are drawn

There is a gap between the planning scan and the first treatment, and the clinical side is not idle in it. The doctor has to outline the target volume and the normal organs on the planning images, slice by slice; the physicist has to design and calculate the plan; and once the plan is finished it goes onto the machine for quality assurance, to confirm that what the machine actually delivers matches what the computer calculated. How many days? I could find no citable source, so I am not giving a number — it runs to days or weeks depending on each hospital's scheduling, and the date your case manager or the front desk gives you is more reliable than any article on the internet. What I usually say in clinic is this: in those waiting days you have not been forgotten. Your plan is being drawn, calculated and checked.

The first treatment: lying still takes longer than the beam

The shape of a treatment day is roughly: check in, identity check, into the treatment room, set-up, imaging to confirm position, irradiation. How many minutes one session takes is, again, something I could find no citable general figure for, and I am not inventing one for you. What can be said honestly is the shape: set-up and image verification take most of the time, and the beam itself is shorter than most people picture. It does not hurt and it is not hot. Anyone on deep inspiration breath-hold spends a little longer, because the hold is repeated. You see the radiation therapists at every session, and there are fixed review slots with a doctor during the course. How many fractions in total and how many weeks you will be coming is a separate decision, and that is in "Why am I not on the shorter course?". One more thing starts on day one: looking after your skin, and the principles for that are all in "Skin reactions: when they come, how to care for them".

Four questions worth getting answered before planning day

The day the planning appointment slip arrives, put these four in your phone and ask them when you get there. One: do I need to practise deep inspiration breath-hold, and if so, is it with a mouthpiece device or on voice instructions? Two: are the marks on my skin permanent ink dots or drawn lines — what do I do about showering, and what if they fade? Three: roughly how long between the planning scan and the first treatment? That answer differs at every hospital, which is exactly why you ask your team and not the internet. Four: the daily check-in routine during treatment, and roughly how much time to allow. All four have definite answers, and all four answers are in your hospital, not in the literature.

From the physician

What to ask on planning day is the process, not the numbers: do I need to practise the breath-hold, how do I look after the skin marks, who tells me the next step. In those waiting days your plan is being drawn, calculated and checked. Go by the timetable your own hospital gives you.
