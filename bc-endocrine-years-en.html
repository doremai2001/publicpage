HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

Five years of endocrine therapy, or ten?

The benefit of extending is small and arrives very late, and the cost lands on bone and endometrium; and for stopping the drug on your own, Taiwan has numbers of its own.

Breast Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 14 references
#Endocrine Therapy#Decisions#Recurrence#NHI Coverage

A Taiwanese National Health Insurance database study of 30,573 people defined an interruption as a gap of more than 180 days between two consecutive prescriptions — 14.9% had done it, and the hazard ratio between interruption and all-cause mortality is 1.32.

"Five years is up — I can stop now, can't I?" Some of the people who ask that have already stopped, a month or two before they ask. The order matters, because it is exactly that month or two that has to be dealt with. This article handles the length of treatment only: five years or ten, whether to add another drug, and where ovarian function suppression sits for premenopausal women. Joint pain, hot flushes, bone problems — the things that actually make people want to stop — are in "The side effects that actually make people stop". Both articles say the same sentence: you must not stop on your own.

Five years is not a container for the effect. It is part of the effect

Pooling individual data from 20 trials and 21,457 patients, about five years of tamoxifen in the ER-positive population (10,645 patients) gave recurrence rate ratios of 0.53 in years 0 to 4 and 0.68 in years 5 to 9 (both 2p<0.00001), and 0.97 in years 10 to 14; breast cancer mortality fell by about a third across the first 15 years (rate ratios of 0.71, 0.66 and 0.68 in those three periods)[1]. A rate ratio divides the two groups' event rates over the same stretch of time, and 0.53 means recurrences during those years came at only about half the speed. In the same analysis, the ER-negative population showed almost no effect from tamoxifen at all[1] — this drug only works in receptor-positive disease.

The box I read out in particular is that 0.68: after the drug stops, the protection carries on for roughly another ten years, and that is what taking the full five years bought.

Stopping after year five does not take the risk to zero

Data from 88 trials and 62,923 women who were ER-positive, completed five years of endocrine therapy and were disease-free at year 5 show recurrences continuing at a steady rate from year 5 through to year 20. Distant recurrence risk differs according to the original tumour size and node count — T1N0 13%, T1N1-3 20%, T1N4-9 34%; T2N0 19%, T2N1-3 26%, T2N4-9 41%[2]. The reason the question "should I take it for ten years" exists at all is that this curve does not stop.

Extending tamoxifen to ten years is currently held up by a single trial

ATLAS randomised 12,894 people who had completed five years of tamoxifen to continue to ten years or stop at five; the following numbers come from the 6,846 of them who were ER-positive: recurrence 617/3,428 against 711/3,418 (p=0.002), breast cancer death 331 against 397 (p=0.01), death from any cause 639 against 722 (p=0.01)[3]. The benefit arrives very late: the recurrence rate ratio was 0.90 (95% confidence interval 0.79 to 1.02) in years 5 to 9 and only fell to 0.75 (0.62 to 0.90) after year 10; cumulative breast cancer mortality in years 5 to 14 was 12.2% against 15.0%, an absolute reduction of 2.8 percentage points[3].

The cost is in the same paper (all 12,894 participants): relative risk of pulmonary embolism 1.87 (1.13 to 3.07); relative risk of endometrial cancer 1.74 (1.30 to 2.34), with a cumulative risk in years 5 to 14 of 3.1% (0.4% mortality) against 1.6% (0.2% mortality)[3]. The other British trial asking the same question exists only as a conference abstract, with no full paper, so ten-year tamoxifen today rests on ATLAS alone.

Extending an aromatase inhibitor to ten years: two trials out of step with each other

MA.17R enrolled 1,918 postmenopausal, hormone-receptor-positive patients who had completed five years of treatment including an aromatase inhibitor, and gave them a further five years of letrozole or placebo; at a median follow-up of 6.3 years, 5-year disease-free survival was 95% against 91%, a difference of 4 percentage points (hazard ratio for recurrence or contralateral breast cancer 0.66, P=0.01 — a hazard ratio looks at how fast things go wrong in the two groups, and 0.66 means the arm on the drug was much slower, not that "a third fewer people relapsed"), but 5-year overall survival was 93% against 94%, hazard ratio 0.97 (P=0.83), no difference; the letrozole group had more bone pain, more fractures and more new osteoporosis[4]. ABCSG-16 asked it a different way: 3,484 postmenopausal patients who had completed five years of treatment were given a further two or five years of anastrozole; the main analysis population was 3,208, with 335 events in each arm and a hazard ratio of 0.99 (0.85 to 1.15, P=0.90); the five-year group had a higher risk of clinical fracture (hazard ratio 1.35, 1.00 to 1.84)[5].

On the Taiwan side there is another layer, the shape of the provisions. Provision 9.1.3 of the National Health Insurance drug reimbursement regulations, covering letrozole as adjuvant therapy after surgery in postmenopausal, hormone-receptor-positive early breast cancer, must not exceed five years; used as extended therapy after five years of tamoxifen it is restricted to those with nodal metastasis, must not be used in those who are eleven years past surgery and free of recurrence, and the extension must not exceed four years[6]. There is a distance between the rule and the evidence, and any conversation about extended treatment runs straight into it.

Premenopausal: where ovarian function suppression sits

SOFT's 8-year update (premenopausal, ER/PR-positive early breast cancer): disease-free survival was 78.9% with tamoxifen alone, 83.2% with tamoxifen plus ovarian function suppression and 85.9% with exemestane plus ovarian function suppression; the cost is written on the same table, with grade 3 or higher adverse events of 24.6%, 31.0% and 32.3%[7]. In the combined long-term follow-up at a median of 13 years, exemestane plus ovarian function suppression against tamoxifen plus ovarian function suppression gave an absolute improvement in 12-year disease-free survival of 4.6 percentage points (hazard ratio 0.79, P<0.001), while 12-year overall survival was 90.1% against 89.1%, hazard ratio 0.93 (0.78 to 1.11), not a significant difference[8].

Here there is a gap peculiar to Taiwan: the NHI's GnRH provision (5.5.1) positions ovarian function suppression as a road that replaces chemotherapy, and permits it only in combination with tamoxifen[9] — which is to say that the strongest-performing combination in the paragraph above, exemestane plus ovarian function suppression, is not a combination the provisions permit in Taiwan. The full conditions of that provision, and why "you could have ovarian suppression instead of chemotherapy" is false reassurance in Taiwan, are in "What this test is actually trying to prove".

Adding a CDK4/6 inhibitor: the two trials are not the same group of people

monarchE enrolled 5,637 patients with hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative, node-positive early breast cancer at high risk of recurrence, adding two years of abemaciclib on top of endocrine therapy. The primary analysis at a median follow-up of 76.2 months: hazard ratio for death 0.842 (95% confidence interval 0.722 to 0.981, P=0.027), meeting the pre-set boundary; 7-year overall survival 86.8% against 85.0%, an absolute difference of 1.8 percentage points; 7-year invasive disease-free survival 77.4% against 70.9%[10]. In 2023, methodological researchers argued that the data were not sufficient to support routine use[11]; that critique predates this survival result.

NATALEE enrolled a different population: hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative patients at anatomical stage IIA (including some who were node-negative), IIB or III, given three years of ribociclib plus a non-steroidal aromatase inhibitor. At a median follow-up of 55.4 months the invasive disease-free survival hazard ratio was 0.716 (0.618 to 0.829), with the absolute difference widening from 2.7 percentage points at three years to 4.5 percentage points at five; overall survival is still maturing, hazard ratio 0.800 (0.637 to 1.003), the upper bound still sitting above 1[12]. The two trials define high risk differently, and I am not going to put their numbers into one sentence.

Taiwan reimburses abemaciclib only (9.107), on conditions narrower than monarchE's own entry criteria: the patient must be node-positive and meet one of "4 or more axillary nodes", "1 to 3 nodes with a tumour of 5 cm or larger" or "1 to 3 nodes with grade 3 differentiation"; standard adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy must already have been given, at most 12 weeks of endocrine therapy may have been taken beforehand, treatment must begin within 16 months of surgical resection, and use must not exceed two years. The provision additionally requires a hormone receptor, ER or PR, above 30%, and states that if the disease progresses during use the drug must be stopped and no other CDK4/6 inhibitor may be applied for — in other words, the other cost of those two years is this: if it does not hold the disease off, ribociclib and palbociclib will be out of reach under the NHI provisions when metastasis comes later, and that sentence has to be asked out loud at the very appointment where the decision to add the drug is made. Ribociclib is not reimbursed for early breast cancer — the conditions in provision 9.72 are all confined to after distant metastasis[6]. While on abemaciclib, diarrhoea severe enough that you cannot drink, fever, unusual bruising or bleeding, or yellowing of the skin and the whites of the eyes with darkening urine mean contacting the team the same day; which other drugs carry their own same-day thresholds is in "Which side effects mean phoning the same day".

Say the side effects out loud, but do not stop on your own

The adherence analysis from BIG 1-98 (6,144 postmenopausal, hormone-receptor-positive early breast cancer patients) shows that stopping letrozole early was associated with worse disease-free survival (multivariable hazard ratio 1.45, 1.09 to 1.93, P=0.01), and that 82.7% of the reasons for stopping early were adverse events[13]. How many people finish the course in the real world is what "The side effects that actually make people stop" counts up; that article's conclusion is fewer than half.

Taiwan's own numbers deserve more attention still. An NHI database cohort enrolled 30,573 patients who had surgery and used adjuvant endocrine therapy for at least twelve months, mean age at diagnosis 52.1: defining interruption as a gap of more than 180 days between two consecutive prescriptions, 4,565 (14.9%) had done it; non-adherent patients with a medication possession ratio below 80% numbered 6,942 (22.7%). After adjustment, interruption was associated with all-cause mortality (hazard ratio 1.32, 1.20 to 1.46) and so was non-adherence (1.45, 1.32 to 1.59), with the risk rising as the number of interruptions rose, and more markedly in younger patients[14].

In clinic I say two opposite things together. The first: the side effects must be said out loud. Switching drug (between tamoxifen and an aromatase inhibitor), adding medication to manage the symptoms, changing when the tablet is taken — these are all things a clinic can do, and that 82.7% shows that most people who stop are not unwilling to take it, they are suffering[13]. The second: you must not stop on your own, reduce the dose on your own, or give yourself "a few months off and see". The reason was written at the top of this article — the length of the course is itself where those effects come from, and shortening it yourself loses the effect while leaving you with the side effects you have already been through. When to adjust, and what to adjust to, is the team's decision.

To decide how many years, these have to be on the table

This is usually discussed between the fourth and fifth year. Take these with you:

  • The original pathology report: tumour size, number of nodes, grade. That year 5 to 20 curve is divided up by exactly those items.

  • Which drug you are taking, how many years you have taken it, and whether you have ever interrupted it. Both the provisions and the evidence are counted in years completed.

  • Your most recent bone density report and whether you have ever had a fracture. The cost of extended treatment falls mainly on bone; bone monitoring and medication are in "Bone density, bone-targeted drugs and exercise".

  • A list of the symptoms that genuinely trouble you this year, with how often and how much — far more use than the words "not too bad".

Then get this out of your mouth: "With my stage and my nodes, how much more recurrence would extending to ten years be estimated to prevent, and which route can my situation actually reach under the NHI provisions?" If the answer makes you want to stop, then stop in the clinic — not at home.

From the physician

The conversation has to start between the fourth and the fifth year. Take the original pathology report, which drug you have taken and for how many years, your most recent bone density, and a list of the symptoms that genuinely trouble you. The length of the course is itself part of the effect, so you must not stop on your own, reduce the dose on your own, or give yourself a few months off; whether to adjust is the treatment team's decision, and if you really are going to stop, stop in the clinic, not at home.
