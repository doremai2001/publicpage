What an undetectable ctDNA actually means

An undetectable result does not mean there is no residual disease, and a detected one does not mean recurrence is certain. De-escalation and escalation have both been through randomised trials, and each got stuck in a different place.

The term this article turns on is non-inferiority — it asks not whether the new approach is better, but whether it is worse than the old one by more than a margin agreed in advance. That one term decides how much you can take away from an undetectable report, and what you cannot.

Someone brings a report saying "ctDNA not detected" into clinic, and the first sentence is almost always: "Doctor, does this mean I don't have to have chemotherapy?" I understand why that piece of paper looks like a pardon. But the question this test can currently answer is far narrower than most people assume — and it is narrow in more than one direction.

Detected and undetectable really do pull two groups apart

Let me finish the strong side of it first. In a post hoc analysis of the French and Greek IDEA stage III colon cancer trials, 109 of the 554 stage III patients with a ctDNA result (19.7%) had ctDNA detected; the proportion free of recurrence at two years was 43.5% (95% confidence interval 34.1–52.6) where ctDNA was detected and 88.1% (84.7–90.8) where it was undetectable, and after adjustment for other factors ctDNA remained an independent prognostic factor for time to recurrence (adjusted hazard ratio 5.21, 95% confidence interval 3.59–7.58; a hazard ratio is the ratio between the rates at which events occur in two groups, and a confidence interval is the range that estimate can reasonably swing across)[1].

The same numbers have to be read forwards and backwards. 43.5% of the group with ctDNA detected had no recurrence at all within two years; and about 12% of the group in whom it was undetectable did recur within two years[1]. An earlier prospective stage II colon cancer cohort has the same shape: of 164 people with undetectable ctDNA who had no chemotherapy, 16 (9.8%) recurred[2]. The GALAXY observational arm of Japan's CIRCULATE-Japan enrolled 1,039 people and drew blood in the fourth week after surgery; detected ctDNA was more strongly associated with recurrence than any other clinicopathological factor (hazard ratio 10.82, P<0.001)[3] — 10.82 means that group recurred at a far higher rate than the undetectable group, but the paper gives no confidence interval in that cell, so I treat "10.82" as a direction, not a number you can use to work out your own odds. The prognostic groups separate widely; that is a fact. Whether that can be used to change treatment is a different question.

A non-inferiority trial answers "is it worse", not "is it better"

Whether this article gets read correctly turns on this one term. A non-inferiority design does not ask whether the new approach is better; it asks whether the new approach is worse than the old one by more than a margin agreed in advance. DYNAMIC, an Australian and New Zealand trial, randomised 455 people with stage II colon cancer 2:1 to ctDNA-guided management or standard clinicopathological management, with the tolerance agreed in advance set at 8.5 percentage points[4]. Chemotherapy use in the ctDNA-guided arm fell from 28% to 15%, two-year recurrence-free survival was 93.5% against 92.4%, an absolute difference of 1.1 percentage points (95% CI −4.1 to 6.2), inside the tolerance, so non-inferiority was met[4]. The mature five-year results point the same way: five-year recurrence-free survival 88% against 87%, five-year overall survival 93.8% against 93.3%[5].

"Non-inferiority was met" means this: within that tolerance of 8.5 percentage points, it could not be shown to be worse. It does not mean "just as good", and it certainly does not mean "better". And in that trial, the people whose ctDNA was undetectable and who therefore had no chemotherapy had three-year recurrence-free survival of 92.5% — which is to say that about 7.5% of that undetectable, untreated group recurred within three years[4].

The same team ran the same idea in stage III and got a different answer

DYNAMIC-III moved the logic to stage III colon cancer: of 968 evaluable patients, 702 (72.5%) had undetectable ctDNA, median follow-up 47 months[6]. On the de-escalation half, use of oxaliplatin-containing regimens fell from 88.6% to 34.8% and admissions to hospital from 13.2% to 8.5%, and three-year recurrence-free survival was 85.3% against 88.1% — which did not meet the non-inferiority margin[6].

So as of today, the randomised evidence for using undetectable ctDNA to save someone a course of chemotherapy rests on one stage II trial with a non-inferiority design, and the same team's stage III trial did not reach the same conclusion. A report of undetectable ctDNA is not a reason to cancel adjuvant chemotherapy. Whether you should have adjuvant chemotherapy still goes back to the pathology report and the stage. Three months or six, and when to start, is what "Three months or six" deals with. One reminder in passing: if oxaliplatin makes your throat tighten on cold water so that you cannot swallow, that is a same-day call to the treatment team; for what to watch with the other drugs, go to "The chemotherapy you take at home".

Escalating on a detected result is not untested — it was tested

The other half of DYNAMIC-III was escalation: people with ctDNA detected were given intensified treatment, and their two-year recurrence-free survival was 51% against 61% in the standard-treatment group — intensifying did not do better[6]. Japan's ALTAIR trial asked the question more cleanly still: 243 people whose ctDNA became detectable after standard treatment had finished, with no lesion visible on imaging, were randomised to six months of trifluridine/tipiracil or placebo. Median disease-free survival (from randomisation to recurrence or death) was 9.30 against 5.55 months, hazard ratio 0.79 (95% CI 0.60–1.05, P=0.107) — the primary endpoint was not met; and grade 3 or higher haematological toxicity was 73.0% on the drug against 3.3% on placebo[7].

An unproven benefit and a very clear cost: those two belong in the same paragraph. A detected result cannot be read the other way round either: it does not mean recurrence is certain, still less that drugs should be added now. Look back at the IDEA post hoc analysis: 43.5% of the people with ctDNA detected had no recurrence within two years[1].

"Undetectable" does not mean "not there"

The test itself has a ceiling on how sensitive it is. Take the methylation-based assay needing no tumour specimen, validated on 195 patients and 1,230 time points within the GALAXY study: in the molecular residual disease window after surgery its sensitivity was only 58.5% (38/65), meaning that of people who did have residual disease, roughly 40% were not picked up by that one blood draw; specificity — how often the assay stays undetectable in people who do not have disease — was 100% (63/63) in those who had not had adjuvant chemotherapy; sensitivity for picking up recurrence on longitudinal follow-up was 84.4% (54/64); and the average gap between the first detected result and recurrence actually showing itself on imaging or as symptoms was 4.6 months[8].

And something I always say in clinic: assays from different laboratories are not equivalent. One kind sends your own tumour off to be sequenced first, then tracks that set of mutations belonging to you at every blood draw; another needs no tumour specimen and looks for shared signals directly in the blood. How accurate they are and how easily they can be arranged are not the same between those two[9]. A review of ctDNA in gastrointestinal tumours lays the difficulties out: platforms do it differently, how much a tumour sheds into the blood varies a great deal from person to person, which day after surgery the blood is drawn also affects the result, and there are results that miss disease and results that flag disease which is not there; and whether treating before anything is visible on imaging actually buys anything remains uncertain today[10]. So I do not treat an "undetectable" from another hospital as the same sentence as an "undetectable" from this one.

Where the guidelines stand, and what I can look up in Taiwan

The patient version of NCCN Colon Cancer version 2.2026 is direct: people in whom ctDNA is found in the blood after surgery are more likely to have a recurrence later, researchers are still studying whether and how that result should be used to decide treatment, and at present ctDNA testing should not replace standard follow-up or other recommended treatment[11]. ASCO's 2026 ctDNA guideline asks that a result which is undetectable, indeterminate, or discordant with the clinical picture be confirmed on a tissue specimen, and that beyond detecting genetic alterations, the test is only appropriate to offer where a specific, evidence-supported action can be taken once the result is in hand[12].

On the Taiwan side, here is what I found: going through Taiwan's NHI fee schedule (medical service items, version effective 1 May 2025) item by item, there is no procedure listed under circulating tumour DNA, liquid biopsy or molecular residual disease at all[13]. The next-generation sequencing for solid tumours that the NHI has covered since 1 May 2024 uses single-gene testing in colorectal cancer, and its purpose is to find genetic alterations with a drug to match — not the same thing as the molecular residual disease testing discussed here[14]. The circulating tumour DNA prices you can find in hospitals' self-pay announcements are all broad genomic-profiling liquid biopsies, and they cannot be used as the price of the kind of assay DYNAMIC or GALAXY used. The price and the coverage conditions for this one have to be confirmed with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office; I am not going to give you a figure here.

Already tested, or not yet: the three questions in each case

  • Put it in the "alongside standard follow-up" column, not the "instead of" column. Clinic visits, CEA, CT and colonoscopy do not change their schedule because of a ctDNA report[11].

  • If you have already been tested, get three things said out loud at your next appointment: is this the tumour-informed assay or the one needing no tumour specimen, was the blood drawn in which week after surgery or after chemotherapy finished, and will this result change any part of my management. If the answer to the third is "no", there is no hurry to repeat it[12].

  • If you have not been tested, settle the price and the coverage with your case manager before deciding, and work out in advance what you would do if ctDNA is detected and what you would do if it is undetectable. If both answers are "carry on with the existing plan", you can skip it this time.

  • If you want to take part in a trial, ask directly in clinic whether a ctDNA-guided trial is recruiting. That is the most defensible way to use this test in a treatment decision at the moment.

From the physician

When a report comes back, settle three things: which kind of assay this was, at what point after surgery or chemotherapy the blood was drawn, and whether the result will change any part of your management. If the answer to the third is "no", there is no need to repeat it this time. Price and coverage conditions have to be confirmed with your case manager; I am not going to give you a figure in the article.
