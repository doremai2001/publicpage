THE FOUR TECHNOLOGIES

Protons: precise. Then what?

The oropharyngeal phase 3 trial gave protons one piece of randomised evidence that met non-inferiority; in most other common adult cancers the record is no-difference or not yet read out. The full record, with Taiwan's coverage and out-of-pocket reality, laid out in one place.

#Proton Therapy#Evidence#Costs & Self-Pay#NHI Coverage

The oropharyngeal phase 3 trial is the weightiest piece of randomised evidence protons currently have, and the easiest to over-read. This article tells it in three layers, then sets the trials that have and have not read out into one honest list, and ends in Taiwan: which number to get before you sign anything.

Warning 1 (what this displaces): Most of the treatments in this topic are paid out of pocket, and most sit outside standard treatment. Before you decide, you need to know that what they displace is usually not time but three things of real value: standard treatment you have not yet used up, money you will need for later lines, and your eligibility for clinical trials (many trials exclude people who have recently received other experimental treatments). And there is one more, harder to see — physical reserve. The strength burnt through in a course that does not work may no longer be there when a genuinely useful option appears. So the question is not whether this is worth trying — it is what you give up by doing it.

The proton version of this is concrete: adults pay entirely out of pocket, and I could not find a single hospital that publishes the total price of a whole course — so the first number this article wants you to get is the whole-course cost, written down in black and white.

Warning 2 (finding the right doctor): Do not make this kind of decision alone, and do not make it on the word of the person offering the treatment. The doctor you are looking for is one willing to tell you "this is not right for you" — if they never once say who this is NOT for, that is not a consultation, it is a sale.

My position first: proton therapy and hyperthermia are both technologies within my own field, and the hospital I work at has the equipment for them. So I draw every line in this topic tighter, not looser.

"Isn't proton therapy more precise?" — precise, yes, genuinely. What this article answers is the half of the sentence that usually goes unfinished in clinic: once it is precise, what does the patient actually get?

Hitting the target is a physical fact; a dose map is not a clinical outcome

A proton beam releases little dose on its way into the body, gives up most of its energy in one burst at the set depth, and then falls to almost nothing — the Bragg peak. Photons reach their dose maximum shortly after entering the body, keep depositing dose along the way, and pass out the other side[1]. So at the same tumour dose, protons can reduce the total dose that normal tissue takes — that much is not in dispute. What is in dispute is the next step: a prettier dose map does not mean patients live longer or have fewer side effects — why these are two different things is what "New does not mean better" spends its whole length on. Here is one example to hold onto: in the randomised lung-cancer trial, the proton arm had smaller low-dose regions in both heart and lung, yet the primary clinical endpoint was no better[2].

The oropharyngeal phase 3 trial: good news in my own field, so I tell it more slowly

The Frank trial, published in the Lancet in 2026, is the weightiest piece of randomised evidence protons currently have, and I will tell it in three layers. First layer: it is a non-inferiority design, and the question it answers is "did protons turn out worse", not "are protons better". 440 patients with stage III–IV oropharyngeal cancer were randomised to intensity-modulated proton therapy or intensity-modulated photon therapy; the primary endpoint, three-year progression-free survival (the time from the day of randomisation to disease progression or death), was 82.5% versus 83.0%, hazard ratio 0.88 — a hazard ratio divides the speed at which events happen in one group by the speed in the other, and 0.88 means the two groups moved at about the same pace; the 95% confidence interval (the range within which the true value may lie) ran from 0.57 to 1.35 and crosses 1, so no direction can be read from it. The trial had fixed its rule in advance: as long as the gap between the arms did not exceed nine percentage points, protons would count as "not worse" — the result fell inside that line, and non-inferiority was met[3]. Second layer: the secondary endpoints carry a signal towards the proton side — five-year overall survival 90.9% versus 81.0% (hazard ratio 0.58, p=0.045), and long-term dependence on a gastrostomy (a feeding tube into the stomach) 26.8% versus 40.2%, both lower with protons[3]. Third layer, and the limitations belong in this same paragraph: overall survival was a secondary endpoint and the trial was open-label; only 72% and 62% of the two arms actually received the treatment they were assigned to, most often because insurers refused to pay; and the funding included an equipment manufacturer[3]. Put the three layers together, and the sentence this paper can support is "in oropharyngeal cancer, disease control with protons is not inferior and severe toxicity is lower". It cannot support "protons are better".

The other randomised read-outs: no-difference outnumbers difference

Lung cancer: Liao 2018 randomised 149 people (92 photon, 57 proton); grade 3 or higher radiation pneumonitis was 6.5% versus 10.5%, local recurrence was also comparable, and the primary endpoint showed no difference; the authors put the higher pneumonitis rate in the earlier stretch down to a learning curve, and the technique was the older passive scattering[2]. Oesophageal cancer: Lin 2020, a randomised phase IIB trial (145 people), showed a posterior mean "total toxicity burden" of 17.4 with protons against 39.9 with photons, lower with protons; progression-free survival showed no difference — this is an advantage on a composite toxicity endpoint, not a survival advantage, and 80% of the proton treatments were still passive scattering[4]. Prostate cancer: PARTIQoL randomised 450 men and found no difference in quality of life in any domain at any timepoint, with five-year progression-free survival of 93.4% versus 93.7%; as of the fact-check date this result exists only as a conference abstract, and the full paper is unpublished[5]. Both arms did very well — "both are good" does not mean protons are useless; it means that in this cancer, the extra money buys no measurable difference. Leptomeningeal metastasis: Yang 2025, a randomised phase II (63 people), showed proton craniospinal irradiation stretched central-nervous-system progression-free survival from 2.3 to 8.2 months — but what it compared was two irradiation-volume strategies, not protons against photons over the same volume, and the numbers were small[6].

What has not read out cannot be talked about as a result

The phase 3 lung-cancer trial with overall survival as its endpoint, RTOG 1308, has finished enrolling, with primary completion estimated in 2028[7]; RadComp in breast cancer, with cardiac events as the primary endpoint, is estimated to run to 2036[8]; NRG-GI003 in hepatocellular carcinoma and NRG-BN005 in glioma have reached their primary completion dates with no public results as of my fact-check[9][10]. If someone says "protons have already been proven better in these cancers", you can ask them directly: which trial, and in which year did it read out?

The paediatric evidence is the strongest block of all four technologies — but adults cannot use it

Children's normal tissues are still developing, and the theoretical gain from cutting total dose is largest there. Chung 2013 matched 558 proton patients with 558 photon patients, median follow-up 6.7 and 6.0 years: second cancers 5.2% versus 7.5%, adjusted hazard ratio 0.52[11]. Kahalley 2020 compared 79 children with medulloblastoma treated at the same institution on the same protocol (37 proton, 42 photon); the long-term trajectories of IQ and working memory were clearly better in the proton group[12]. Both are cohort studies, not randomised trials — randomising children is widely held to be unethical — and the follow-up for second cancers is still far shorter than their risk window of one to two decades. The American Society for Radiation Oncology's current proton model coverage policy lists paediatric tumours, re-irradiation, skull-base chordoma and chondrosarcoma, and ocular tumours among the indications routinely supported for coverage; prostate, breast and oropharyngeal cancer sit in the second group, to be treated within a trial or a registry[13]. This passage is written for parents. Adults must not extrapolate the paediatric numbers to themselves: your tissues are not developing, and your cancer has its own trial record — it is the sections above.

In Taiwan: covered under nineteen; adulthood is another world

Why approved, covered and effective are three different things is taken apart across the whole of "Approved, covered, effective: three different things"; here I will set down only the Taiwan facts. From 1 January 2026 the NHI added three proton-therapy payment items, every one of them restricted to patients under nineteen, requiring prior review, and payable once in a lifetime for the same primary cancer[14]; the point values and the full table of provisions are in "Approved, covered, effective: three different things". Adults pay entirely out of pocket. Across Taiwan I found only one hospital whose proton centre publishes a per-session fee on its official page: intensity-modulated proton therapy at NT$21,750 to NT$34,500 per session depending on complexity, with respiratory gating, simulation and planning charged on top — that is one institution's own published figure, not a nationwide going rate, and the total still depends on the number of sessions[15]. For every other hospital there is no findable official total: ask the medical affairs office directly, and insist on the whole-course figure in writing. What this money displaces is exactly what the warning at the top of the page lists: out-of-pocket drugs for the later lines, the co-payments of a clinical trial, and your physical reserve.

When I would put protons on the table, and when I would not

The reasonable situations look like this: curative treatment in children and young people; a tumour pressed against a critical structure that cannot take one more unit of dose — re-irradiation, the skull base, the eye, the situations on the consensus list[13]; or joining a trial that is actually comparing protons with photons. The unreasonable situation is equally clear: a common adult cancer, randomised comparisons that are either no-difference or not yet read out, and the money you plan to commit is the money the later lines will need. For the great majority of patients with common cancers, what decides the outcome is the stage, the subtype, and whether the course of treatment gets properly completed — not which particle is used. Two sentences you can ask out loud at your next visit: "For my cancer, is there a randomised comparison that has read out?" "If this same money stays back for the later lines, what would it buy?" For those who want the dosimetric detail, the site's research-update page on proton therapy goes into it in depth; this article does not repeat its content.

From the physician

If you are considering paying for proton therapy yourself, take two questions to your next visit: for my cancer and stage, is there a randomised comparison that has read out? What is the total cost of the whole course, and can I have it in writing? It is not too late to decide once you hold both answers.
