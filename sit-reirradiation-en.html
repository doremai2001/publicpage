WHEN TREATMENT STALLS

Can somewhere already irradiated be treated again?

The European consensus says prospective evidence is scarce. Seven experts, one case: five would treat again, no two plans matched.

Situation Guides · When Treatment Stalls · 11 references
#Radiotherapy#Recurrence#Decisions#Getting Ready

I perform this treatment myself, which is exactly why the other side belongs here: one phase 2 trial of head and neck re-irradiation reported survival above its historical control alongside 9% treatment-related deaths and 22.4% grade 4 to 5 late toxicity among those surviving a year. Deciding who is worth that risk is what case-by-case assessment actually means.

"I've had radiotherapy there before — can it be done again?" I am asked this every month, and the person asking has usually searched already and arrives with half an answer. Let me put the position first: there is no general rule for this, and it has to be assessed case by case by the treating team. That is not an evasion — the 2022 consensus document from the European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology and the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer says of itself that prospective evidence on re-irradiation is scarce and that our understanding of the underlying radiobiology is insufficient[1]. What follows is where that sentence comes from.

"Normal tissue repairs itself" rests on animal experiments

Tissue does partially recover from radiation injury, and the cleanest measurement of it comes from 56 rhesus monkeys. The first course delivered one fixed dose, and a second course followed after an interval of one, two or three years; 4 of the 45 that completed the observation period developed spinal cord paresis. Modelled at the 5% incidence level, recovery of the initial dose was about 76% at one year, 85% at two years and 101% at three, and 61% even under the most conservative model[2].

The authors attach two warnings, and I am copying them across: follow-up in these experiments ran only to two or two and a half years after re-irradiation, whereas human myelopathy sometimes appears later than that, and long-term recovery varies a great deal between tissues[2]. So "it repairs" is true, and it is still a long way from "it can be irradiated again".

The human data is scattered case reports, gathered up and recalculated

The human spinal cord data comes from a pooled series of eight reports and 40 patients. The authors first converted the doses of both courses onto a single scale using a mathematical model before adding them — because the same total dose split into different numbers of fractions, at different doses per fraction, can differ greatly in biological effect, which is why the number written in your records cannot simply be added up. Of those 40, 11 developed myelopathy 4 to 25 months after the second course (median 11 months), and every one of the 11 fell into one of two situations: a high dose in a single course, or a very short interval between the two[3]. An update adding 38 further patients split the risk into three bands, with 1 case (3%) in the low-risk group, 25% in the intermediate and 90% in the high; the authors' recommendation runs to a single line — keep the dose as low as is technically achievable[4].

I am not going to print a dose figure or an interval in months anywhere in this article. The reason is practical: those are converted planning parameters that shift with the assumptions used, and the numbers in your own records are in a different unit. Printing them would only produce mistaken self-assessment. The same authors also flag that the steep dose gradients produced by stereotactic and intensity-modulated techniques need further evaluation against this model[4].

What image guidance and stereotactic technique changed, and what they did not

What they changed is whether the dose can be concentrated where it needs to go while the organ next door is spared. An analysis comparing patients who did and did not develop myelopathy found more than a threefold difference in the maximum point dose received by the thecal sac — the same phrase, "irradiate it again", carries a completely different risk under different plans[5]. That was a case-control analysis of 19 patients, a very small sample. A literature review of the brain points the same way: the smaller the treated volume, the higher the cumulative dose that can be given, with radionecrosis appearing above a certain cumulative level; and the same review found no correlation between the interval separating the two courses and the rate of necrosis[6]. So "wait a few months and it becomes safe" does not even hold up as a direction.

What they did not change is this: having irradiated the same place before is itself the strongest risk factor. In an analysis of 2,200 brain metastases, the one-year risk of symptomatic adverse radiation effect was 20% in lesions previously treated with stereotactic radiosurgery, against 3% in those never treated and 4% in those given whole-brain radiotherapy[7]. A series looking specifically at repeat treatment (229 lesions, 124 patients) reported one-year freedom from progression of 80%, symptomatic adverse effects at one year of 11%, and any imaging change within a year of 30%, with risk rising sharply as lesions got larger; the authors suggest considering other approaches for larger lesions[8]. Re-irradiation is not the only road, and a team will weigh surgery, drugs and supportive treatment alongside it. How those imaging changes are read is in "The scan looks worse. The disease may not be.".

Seven experts, one case, no consensus

To show why there is no general rule, I have not found better material than this paper. Seven international radiation oncologists were given the same case: a thoracic spine lesion progressing locally after two courses of palliative radiotherapy. Five would give a third course, and preferred stereotactic technique; but the fractionation they chose ranged from three fractions to twenty, the five differed from one another on the dose limit acceptable for the spinal cord, and they did not even agree on how the organs at risk should be contoured. The paper's concluding sentence is that no consensus was reached[9].

I would like this understood correctly. It is not that the doctor has yet to tell you the answer, and not that the doctor is being cautious. It is that the profession itself has no single answer to this question. What does have an answer is the method: convert both courses onto one scale and add them, define which organs lie in the beam's path, assess the size and site of the lesion, compare the alternatives. All of that needs your radiotherapy planning files, which live in the hospital and are not in a discharge summary.

The cost has to be written down, or this article becomes encouragement

I perform this treatment myself, which is exactly why I should set out the other side clearly. A phase 2 trial in recurrent squamous cell carcinoma of the head and neck used split-course twice-daily re-irradiation with chemotherapy in 100 analysable patients: five-year survival rose from a historical control of 3.8% to 14.9% (p=0.01), while nine patients (9%) died of causes related to treatment; and among those surviving a year, the five-year cumulative incidence of grade 4 to 5 late toxicity was 22.4% against 3.2% in the historical control (p=0.02)[10].

That trial accrued between 2000 and 2003, used techniques different from today's, and had no randomised control — the comparison is with historical data. But it puts both sides of this question on the table at once: in a design with no randomised control, compared only against historical data, what was seen was survival above the historical figure together with 9% treatment-related deaths and 22.4% severe late toxicity. What data like that supports is "some people may buy time this way"; it does not support "re-irradiation prolongs survival". Who is worth taking that risk for is what "assessed case by case" is actually doing.

I checked the fee schedule line by line, and there is no "re-irradiation" item

I checked all 5,159 records in the National Health Insurance (健保) fee schedule (支付標準) line by line: I found no matching item for re-irradiation as a separate procedure, and among the 45 radiotherapy codes I found no matching item whose name or rules mention re-irradiation; I also found no matching item for a separate intensity-modulated radiotherapy code in this version of the full table[11]. What is absent from the wording and how your own course is claimed are two different matters; ask the hospital billing office (醫務課).

One thing can be said. All three stereotactic items require prior review under the rules, and the two intracranial ones state that "all cases must be submitted to the insurer for prior case-by-case approval"[11]. These stereotactic items are, structurally, reviewed one case at a time — which is an odd echo of "there is no general rule". How conventionally fractionated re-irradiation is claimed, I found no matching provision for in the wording, so ask the hospital billing office (醫務課) about that too. The full table is the 114.03.13 version, verified 2026-08-30; these codes do not appear in the amendment schedule effective 115 年 9 月 1 日, but check the current version. I was unable to obtain any official Taiwanese document on indications for re-irradiation or cumulative dose limits.

Before that conversation, get the last course pulled out of the file

One: which site was treated last time, when did it finish, and what were the dose and fractionation? (Ask the hospital to retrieve the radiotherapy plan; do not rely on memory.) Two: after conversion, how much room is left in that organ? Three: how large is the volume this time, and how close are the spinal cord, brainstem or other organs to the lesion? Four, the one I most want asked: besides irradiating again, what are the options, and what does each cost? Wanting a second team's view is reasonable, and how to ask for it usefully is in "Asking for a second opinion, out loud". What happens to your current treatment meanwhile is, again, the medical team's decision. But one thing does not wait for a clinic date: new or clearly worsening back pain with leg weakness, numbness, or a change in bladder or bowel control is a warning of pressure on the nerves. Why that is a same-day matter, and a different question from whether you can be irradiated again, is in "Back pain plus a weak leg: do not wait".

From the physician

I have printed no dose figure and no interval in months, deliberately: those are converted planning parameters that shift with the assumptions used, and your records are in a different unit, so self-assessment will only mislead. This conversation starts with the hospital retrieving your previous radiotherapy plan. Separately, new or clearly worsening back pain with leg weakness, numbness or a change in bladder or bowel control is a same-day matter.
