WHEN YOU ARE THE FAMILY

Those ten minutes in clinic: what the family should ask

83% knew the recommended treatment; only 49% recalled whether the goal was cure or symptom control. That is the sentence that drops.

Situation Guides · When You Are the Family · 10 references
#Family#How to ask#Getting Ready#Decisions

Families underestimate one thing: in the Dutch study, what the patient and the companion recalled together was more than either alone. Going in with him is a function, not only support. Before you go in, move the question you most want answered to the front — the time gets squeezed off the back.

First, the number in the title. "Ten minutes" is what it feels like after two hours in the waiting area; it is not a fact I am citing. Taiwan has no official statistic for outpatient consultation length and no reliable research figure, so this piece will not print an average in seconds. What it can do is the other thing — which items fall out at that length, how to pick them back up, and when to move the conversation to a different setting.

Short is not a matter of anyone's attitude

A systematic review assembled data from 1946 to 2016, in six languages, covering 67 countries and 28,570,712 consultations: mean primary care consultation length ran from 48 seconds in Bangladesh to 22.5 minutes in Sweden, and in 18 countries, about half the world's population, it was under five minutes[1]. Taiwan is not among those 67 — I pulled the full text and searched it for "Taiwan"; zero hits. So what it can show is that the short consultation is a cross-national structural phenomenon, not the physician you happen to see being cold.

A structural problem does not go away because somebody tries harder, but it can be organised around. What gets asked in the room, and what goes somewhere else, can be settled in advance.

The item that falls out most often is what the treatment is for

A Swiss study interviewed 71 newly diagnosed lung cancer patients 1 to 3 days after they were told the diagnosis, and compared their answers with what the physicians recorded having said: 90% recalled the diagnosis correctly and 83% knew the recommended treatment, but only 49% correctly recalled whether the goal was cure or symptom control. Satisfaction ran in the same order: 76% highly satisfied with how the diagnosis was given, 73% with the treatment, 39% with the goal[2].

What that measures is the gap between recall and physician self-report; the sample is small and the setting single, and it supports no conclusion about whose responsibility any of it is. I write it out because it points at the one sentence most worth confirming again: the goal of this treatment.

Another study, of 260 cancer patients whose consultations were recorded and compared item by item against recall, found something counter-intuitive: the more the physician said about prognosis, the less the patient recalled overall, and patients with a worse prognosis recalled less; the effect of age only held once the total amount of information given was controlled for[3]. The heaviest passage is the one hardest to keep hold of.

What two people remember adds up

A Dutch study had 100 cancer patients aged 65 and over and 71 companions fill in recall questionnaires after the nursing consultation before chemotherapy, then compared them item by item against video: patient and companion together recalled more than either alone, and the two were complementary; patients who came alone recalled a similar proportion but less in absolute terms[4].

So having somebody with you is itself a concrete function, not moral support. The same study also recorded that where patients said they had not understood everything, the companion recalled more.

The question list does work, and works on one thing only

Writing down what you want to ask has been tested in randomised trials. A 2024 meta-analysis pooled 7 randomised trials and 1,059 patients with advanced cancer: patients using a question prompt list asked significantly more questions (standardised mean difference 0.73, 95% confidence interval 0.28 to 1.18) — a standardised mean difference is the gap left after different scales have been converted onto one ruler, and 0.73 is a clear gap, but what it measures is the number of questions and nothing else; variation between studies was large (I²=83%). The other half of the same analysis has to be written with it: on health-related outcomes — end-of-life care, anxiety and quality of life — there was no significant improvement[5].

A newer 2025 review of 14 randomised trials points the same way, and identifies whether anyone shows you how to use it as the difference: a question prompt list with instructions for its use raised question-asking and decision self-efficacy more than a bare list did (the abstract gives no pooled effect size, so all I can write is the direction)[6].

Stated precisely, the use of a question prompt list is getting what you want to ask out of your mouth, not "the more you ask the better the care". I have not seen a validated Chinese-language list, so the following are only common ways of putting it, not questions you have to ask — what is the goal of this treatment; what do the next three months roughly look like; which side effects have to be reported at once, and on which number; if it does not work as expected, what are the next options.

Taking that passage home with you

A Dutch double-blind randomised trial in 2000 recorded the whole of the first consultation and randomly gave or withheld the tape, in 201 people: the group given the tape were more satisfied and recalled more, while quality of life did not differ; 75% listened to it, and 73% of those listened with somebody else[7].

That was a research team recording under informed consent. Whether outpatient recording in Taiwan is regulated, and how individual hospitals handle it, I could not find a single document from a competent authority — so the only way I can write it is: if you want to record, ask the physician in person first. Most of the time "may I take notes while you talk?" or "could you say that part again so I can write it down?" gets you to the same place.

Some things should not be asked in front of him

The three statutes word the person to be informed differently, and that is worth knowing in itself. Patient Right to Autonomy Act (病人自主權利法) Article 5 I: "when a patient attends for care, the medical institution or physician shall, at a time and in a manner it judges appropriate, inform the patient himself of his condition and treatment plan… where the patient does not expressly object, the persons with a relevant connection may also be informed." Article 81 of the Medical Care Act (醫療法) says to inform "the patient or their statutory agent, spouse, relatives or person with a relevant connection"; Article 12-1 of the Physicians Act (醫師法) says to inform "the patient or their family members"; and Article 8 of the Hospice Palliative Care Act (安寧緩和醫療條例), on top of informing the terminally ill patient or the family, adds a proviso — where the patient clearly expresses a wish to know the condition and the various medical options, he shall be told[8] (current texts of each Act, verified 30 August 2026).

"Please don't tell him" is not something the law settles in one sentence — that question is in "Whether to tell him, and what the law says". What you can ask in clinic is a different sentence: whether there is a time or a setting suitable for talking separately.

When one consultation will not hold it, Taiwan has a name for the alternative

The NHI fee schedule (支付標準) contains an item called the palliative care family consultation fee (緩和醫療家庭諮詢費), item 02020B. Its rules are written very specifically: the indication is mainly current inpatients or emergency critically ill patients already in a terminal state; participants comprise the attending medical team and the patient or the patient's family; at least one hour per case; the content fully recorded, filed in the medical record, and signed by the participating team and the patient or family; not claimable by anyone already receiving hospice shared care (安寧共照), inpatient hospice care or home-based hospice care; and limited to two claims per person per hospital[9] (verified 30 August 2026). The conditions are narrow; whether they fit your situation is for the attending team or the case manager (個管師).

The family meeting itself has randomised evidence. An Australian cluster randomised trial in three hospitals enrolled 297 pairs of palliative care inpatients and their principal carers: the arm given a structured family meeting had significantly lower family psychological distress at day 10 and significantly higher preparedness for the caring role; quality of end-of-life care and health care resource use did not differ[10]. The effect landed on the family, which is not where most people expect it.

Taiwan has a cancer care quality certification scheme, but I could not obtain the text of its standards this time — so I will not say it requires a family meeting for every case, and I will not say it does not. What your hospital actually does, ask the case manager.

The fifteen minutes before you go in next time

Write down what you want to ask before you go in, then do two things. Move the question you most want answered to the front, because the time usually gets squeezed off the back. And circle the parts of the list that do not need the physician to answer them — scheduling, admission paperwork, grant documents, when the next blood test is — which are usually held by the case manager, the medical social work office (社工室) or the hospital billing office (醫務課).

If there is room for one sentence only, I would spend it here: "Is the goal of this treatment to clear the tumour, or to control it and make the symptoms easier?" That 49% above is about exactly this sentence going missing in transmission.

Who goes to clinic and who waits at home is a matter of dividing the work, in "Caring for someone: divide the work first"; whether somebody in another city or another country can join by video, and how far Taiwan's rules go on that, is in "Caring from another city, or another country".

From the physician

The only thing randomised trials confirm about a question list is that more questions get asked; anxiety, quality of life and end-of-life outcomes did not improve. If you want to record, ask the physician in person — I could find no Taiwanese regulation on it. Circle the items the physician does not have to answer and leave them for the case manager or the billing office.
