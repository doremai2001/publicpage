DURING TREATMENT AND AFTER

Pelvic skin breaks down in the folds

What goes first is what rubs; the bans on washing and on deodorant have both been reversed in the guidelines, and broken skin with weeping is the grade the clinical side has to see inside twenty-four hours.

The nearer the tumour sits to the anal verge, the higher the dose, and with chemotherapy alongside, the harder the skin is to look after — that axis has odds ratios you can look up. What I could find no citable source for is the week-by-week pelvic script of when it reddens and when it clears, so this article gives two time anchors and nothing in between.

Pelvic skin reactions are hardly ever picked up in the treatment room. Mostly it happens the other way round: one day after a shower it feels "a bit prickly down there", and then it gets left for a few more days before anyone mentions it at the next appointment. By the time it is being said out loud, it is usually past red. This article settles three things: what sort of place it starts in, which of the old rules the guidelines have now overturned, and which situation is past anything a cream can do and has to be seen by the clinical side inside a day.

My position first: pelvic radiotherapy is what I do every day, so every good word about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it. Implants such as the rectal spacer are not something my department provides; I write about them as an option you can go and ask about, and for cost and for who performs them, please ask that department and the medical affairs office.

Order of magnitude first, grade second

The 2025 practical recommendations on radiodermatitis from ESTRO, the European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology, open with the line that up to 95% of all cancer patients receiving radiotherapy develop acute radiation dermatitis during or after treatment[1]. Read the label on that sentence: it is a ceiling across all sites and all cancers, not the going rate in the pelvis.

The most comparable group inside the pelvis is rectal and anal cancer. A systematic review and meta-analysis — a study that pools separate studies into a single estimate — of 50 studies and 4,892 patients put the overall rate of radiation dermatitis at 58.7% (95% confidence interval 55.2 to 62.1, the band the true figure most plausibly sits in), grade 3 or worse at 12.3%, and moist desquamation, meaning skin broken and weeping, at 34.5%[2]. A Brazilian single-centre randomised trial in the same anal canal and rectal cancer population enrolled only 63 people and came out with an overall rate of 100%, severe in 36.5%, and 17.5% interrupting radiotherapy because of the dermatitis[3]. Those two are not contradicting each other; the denominator and how closely people were watched are different, the second having assessed everyone at every nursing visit. The box worth carrying is 12.3%: most people go red, a few get severe.

The fold is where the grading line runs

The international adverse-event grading standard defines grade 2 moist desquamation as patchy and "mostly confined to skin folds and creases", and grade 3 as moist desquamation "in areas other than skin folds and creases"[4]. The line between the grades is drawn on the fold itself: broken inside a fold is one thing, broken outside it is a more serious other thing. Which makes the daily question not "is it red" but "has it come out of the fold".

That ESTRO document names anal and vulvar cancer as the cancers at higher risk of skin reactions, on the grounds that the field is shallow to begin with, and notes that skin folds caused by obesity raise the skin dose in the fold[1]. On who is at more risk, the rectoanal meta-analysis gives one very usable axis: a tumour within 5 cm of the anal verge carried an odds ratio of 2.86 — an odds ratio compares one group's odds of the event with the other's, so read it as direction rather than as a multiple of the probability; no stoma 3.12; a total dose above 50 Gy 2.59; concurrent chemotherapy 2.73[2]. The direction is plain: the nearer the field sits to the anal verge, the higher the dose, and with chemotherapy running alongside, the harder the skin is to look after. The same meta-analysis also put the turning point for dose at 50 Gy, beyond which each further 5 Gy raised the risk of severe dermatitis by 29%[2].

A week-by-week pelvic script: two anchors, and a blank between them

The nursing guideline from BC Cancer in British Columbia puts it flatly: the reaction usually appears 1 to 4 weeks after treatment starts, and can persist for several weeks after treatment ends[4]. The second anchor is ESTRO's: a course running longer than 4 to 5 weeks delays the point at which skin reactions begin to recover[1]. Most pelvic courses land at five to six weeks, so the last day of treatment is not the finishing line. That is written into a guideline; it is not something I am saying to make you feel better.

Anything finer than that — which week it reddens, which week it breaks, how many weeks until it settles — I could find no pelvis-specific source for. Breast radiotherapy has a prospective study that produced a curve peaking about two weeks after the end, but the field, the skin tension and the friction conditions are all different, and I am not going to carry it across. The gap in the middle is a genuine blank.

Two old bans, and where they now stand

No washing and no deodorant have both been reversed in the guidelines: ESTRO's wording is to wash the skin daily with lukewarm water and gently pat the treated area dry, and on deodorant, that its use is permitted unless the skin is already broken — both graded L1 and strongly recommended[1]. In the pelvis I keep only the boundary: once the skin breaks, stop. How the randomised trials overturned those bans is set out in the breast topic's "Skin reactions: when they come, how to care for them" and the rectal cancer topic's "The “don’t wash it” rule has the evidence backwards", and I am not writing it twice. For moisturiser, ESTRO goes no higher than evidence level L4[1] — use it, but it is not armour.

Sitz baths, ointments, dressings: whose job is whose

One sentence in the BC Cancer guideline is written specifically for pelvic patients, and it appears twice, once in the grade 1 section and once in the grade 2 to 3 section: patients receiving perineal or rectal radiotherapy should take a daily sitz bath from the start of radiotherapy[4]. Its level needs stating clearly: that is a care recommendation written into a nursing guideline, not a result produced by a randomised trial — as I also wrote in the perineum article, I could find no trial evidence that sitz baths improve radiation dermatitis. So its place is comfort measure and care convention, and it is skin care, not a treatment for the bowel. Using a sitz bath to handle tenesmus, mucus or acute radiation proctitis has no guideline-level evidence behind it in anything I could find; that half sits in "Ten trips to the toilet and nothing feels finished". If a sitz bath feels good, have one — but do not let having had one push back the day you report a bowel symptom.

Steroid ointment is written in the guideline as something that may be used sparingly on inflammation, as directed by the physician[4]; the 2023 guideline from MASCC, the Multinational Association of Supportive Care in Cancer, puts mometasone and betamethasone on its list of recommendations for prevention[5]. That is a prescribing decision: take it to clinic and ask, rather than picking something off a pharmacy shelf. Dressings are the same. ESTRO writes for grade 2 "apply self-adhesive soft silicone dressings" and for grade 3 "apply soft silicone or other appropriate dressings to broken skin", both L1 strong recommendations[1] — but those recommendations are addressed to the clinical side; they are not an instruction to go and buy something and put it on. Photobiomodulation and Mepitel film, which are on the MASCC list, have consensus restricted to breast cancer, and the pelvis is not inside it[5].

One class of product does have randomised evidence in the pelvis. In the 63-patient single-blind trial above, people using a spray non-sting skin protectant film had a lower chance of developing confluent moist desquamation and a longer time free of moist desquamation; but the two arms did not differ on severity or on the number who interrupted radiotherapy, and the overall rate was still 100%[3]. It won on one endpoint. It is not a talisman.

Broken skin and weeping are not a cream problem

This section outranks every sentence of self-care above it. In the grade-to-action table of the BC Cancer guideline, grade 2 through grade 3 is marked "urgent: medical assessment required within 24 hours"[4]. Once there is broken skin, blistering or weeping, that is not the grade for stepping up the moisturiser; it is the grade for coming back so that we can look at it. The same guideline sets out four signs of infection, and I will run them starting from the one most easily missed: pain and swelling spreading beyond the treated area; purulent discharge from the wound; an odour coming from the wound; fever[4]. ESTRO's recommendation from grade 2 upwards is that infection be supervised by skin or wound care specialists, that a topical antimicrobial preparation be used as soon as there are signs of infection, and that adequate analgesia be prescribed where it is needed[1]. What an already-open wound should have on it, and how often that is changed, is a decision taken after the clinical side has seen it; please do not choose something at the pharmacy and stick it on yourself.

A pelvic course hands you one condition that other specialties do not have: you see the treatment team almost every day. Any change in the skin counts as reported the moment you say it while you are getting onto the treatment couch. You do not have to hold it until your clinic date.

Ask it before you leave the treatment room

Cost, in a sentence. The full NHI fee schedule contains 37026B, "skin care for radiotherapy", at 244 points, with a note that one course is counted per week and that the site treated, the dose and the number of fractions must be recorded on the claim[6]. The same table also carries 47047C, sitz bath, at 53 points[6] — an item existing on the table is not the same thing as a guideline recommending it, and those two have to be read apart. As for where specialist functional dressings stand for reimbursement in radiation dermatitis, I could find no corresponding indication provision anywhere in the full table; the types available and their cost vary by hospital and are settled by the clinical side after assessment; put that question to the medical affairs office or to your radiation oncology nurse.

There is one action to take away. Check those few creases every day, make "has it come out of the fold" the only test you apply, and say what you saw as you get onto the treatment couch next time — and if you want to put anything new on, ask that in the same breath. As for pain and swelling spreading beyond the treated area, pus, an odour from the wound, fever, and broken skin with weeping, those five do not wait for your next appointment; we need to see them the same day.

From the physician

Put the sitz bath in its right place: it is skin care, not a treatment for the bowel. The folds redden first and break first, so what you check daily is whether those creases have come out of bounds. Pain and swelling spreading beyond the treated area, pus, an odour from the wound, fever, and broken skin with weeping — those five mean coming back the same day, not reaching for a different pot of cream.
