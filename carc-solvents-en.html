AT WORK

Washing your hands in solvent

Benzene is a Group 1 carcinogen, yet those few minutes at the petrol pump do not reach even a one-in-a-million excess risk; the dividing line is cumulative exposure at work.

Carcinogens at work and at home · At Work · 17 references
#Occupational#Solvents#Special Exams#At Home

What benzene is established to cause is acute myeloid leukaemia; chronic lymphocytic leukaemia has been argued over throughout, and not even Taiwan's Schedule of Occupational Diseases settles it. This piece also explains the “Grade 3 management” on your health examination report that nobody ever talks you through.

Those three minutes at the petrol pump are not where the worry belongs. What actually delivers benzene into a body is the can of solvent used to wash oil off your hands, the garage that opens into the living room, and the cigarette lit indoors — figures for each, one at a time, below. If you have spent twenty years in an organic solvent environment, the last section has something you can do today.

Which leukaemia is benzene actually proven to cause

Monograph 120 of the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), 2018, places benzene in Group 1[1]. Group 1 describes how certain the evidence is, not how dangerous the agent is — "Group 1 is not a danger ranking" and this topic's entry page, "Carcinogens at work and at home", cover that in full.

Inside that grade, benzene has sufficient evidence for one thing only: acute non-lymphocytic leukaemia in adults, which in clinic we call acute myeloid leukaemia (AML). Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL), non-Hodgkin lymphoma, multiple myeloma, chronic myeloid leukaemia, lung cancer and AML in children all sit in the "limited evidence" box instead[1].

A line does the rounds: "benzene-induced leukaemia, CLL excepted". I went and looked at the Schedule of Occupational Diseases under the Labor Occupational Accident Insurance. Item 7.19, benzene, corresponds to "blood cancer (Leukemia, non-Hodgkin lymphoma and multiple myeloma)", and the provision does not exclude CLL[2]. CLL sits in the box where the evidence is not yet enough and the field still argues, not in the box of things officially struck out.

Those three minutes at the pump: what do they come to?

The World Health Organization's indoor air quality guidelines give a coefficient you can work with: each 1 μg/m³ of benzene in air corresponds to a lifetime excess leukaemia risk of 6×10⁻⁶; run backwards, the concentrations at which lifetime excess risk falls at one in ten thousand, one in a hundred thousand and one in a million are 17, 1.7 and 0.17 μg/m³[3].

Then the measurements. A Baltimore study had members of the public sample for themselves while refuelling: the geometric mean of 32 events was 3.2 ppb, and by probabilistic risk assessment the authors calculated that consumers refuelling do not even reach the one-in-a-million excess risk threshold, and that the scenario for petrol station staff did not exceed one in ten thousand either. The same paper works out something counter-intuitive: for an ordinary consumer, accumulated benzene exposure from everyday ambient air carries more risk than those few minutes at the pump[4].

US NHANES data from 2017 to March 2020 does measure it: having recently pumped petrol yourself, and having recently smoked, are both associated with higher biomarkers of benzene exposure[5]. Being measurable and being enough to cause cancer are two different things.

The biggest benzene source in your house is probably the garage and the cigarette

The same WHO guideline ranks the sources in daily life: each cigarette releases 430 to 590 μg of benzene, and indoor benzene rises by thirty to seventy per cent where there is second-hand smoke; a garage attached to the house contributes forty to sixty per cent of indoor benzene, and can reach 8 μg/m³; the interior of a new building can reach 30 μg/m³; paint, adhesives and solvents are sources too[3].

Set those beside the 1.7 μg/m³ of the previous section and the judgement at the top of this article has a denominator. On ventilation, "How much should the formaldehyde in a new home worry you?" does the job better than I can here.

In the same document, WHO closes the question off: benzene is a genotoxic carcinogen, and no safe level of exposure can be recommended[3]. Every concentration standard below is to be read as a management trigger, not a safety threshold.

So what quantity does actually matter

The workplace is another order of magnitude. A meta-analysis of 15 studies puts the relative risk of leukaemia in occupationally benzene-exposed workers at 1.72 (95% confidence interval 1.37 to 2.17) — this group develops leukaemia at about 1.7 times the rate of the unexposed, not "a 1.7 times chance of getting it"; the bracket is the range in which the estimate plausibly sits, and the whole of it lies above 1, which rules out "no difference between the groups". By cumulative exposure in three tiers it runs 1.64 (below 40 ppm-years), 1.90 (40 to 99.9) and 2.62 (100 and above), with a trend test P value of 0.015[6]. A ppm-year is concentration times years: ten years in a 1 ppm environment is 10 ppm-years.

At the low-dose end, a flexible meta-regression pooling 9 human studies and 30 risk estimates shows that at a cumulative exposure of only 10 ppm-years the relative risk of leukaemia is already 1.14 (1.04 to 1.26)[7].

It is at the high-exposure end that the gap opens. The standardised mortality ratio for leukaemia in the US Pliofilm rubber plant cohort was 337 (95% confidence interval 154 to 641), and by cumulative exposure stratum 109 (below 40 ppm-years), 322 (40 to 199), 1,186 (200 to 399) and 6,637 (400 and above)[8]. A standardised mortality ratio of 100 means the same as the general population; that 6,637 stratum is a group that spent an entire working life steeped in high-concentration benzene, not a plant measured and managed under today's law. The multiples have to go back onto an absolute scale: the WHO coefficient above says that for lifetime excess risk to reach one in ten thousand, benzene in air has to stay at 17 μg/m³ over the long term[3] — the multiples multiply a very small base, so even in the highest exposure stratum leukaemia is not what happens to most people. How many occupational leukaemia cases have been recognised in Taiwan, I could find no citable official statistic for, and I am not going to invent one.

One more study gets misquoted constantly. In Science in 2004, 250 exposed workers against 140 controls: even where the workplace concentration was below 1 ppm, the workers' white cell and platelet counts were significantly lower than the controls'[9]. It measured blood counts, which is haematotoxicity, not cancer incidence — it proves the body reacts below 1 ppm, and cannot be read as "below 1 ppm you get leukaemia".

What "Grade 3 management" on the health examination report means

That grade on the report is the column people bring to my clinic more than any other in these articles, so I will finish it here, and the other pieces will link back when health examinations come up.

Employers must arrange periodic special health examinations for workers in specially hazardous operations. Benzene work is number 16 in the Recommended Guideline on Health Management Grading for Workers' Special Health Examinations, issued by the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), Ministry of Labor, and the items are: work history and a symptom enquiry; past history of blood, kidney and liver disease and long-term medication; physical examination of the blood system, skin and mucous membranes and conjunctiva; and a full blood count with white cell differential[10].

Results are sorted into four management grades: Grade 1 is no abnormality, Grade 2 is an abnormality judged unrelated to the work, and it is Grades 3 and 4 that require action. Grades 3 and 4 share one supporting criterion: an eight-hour time-weighted average concentration of 0.5 ppm or above; the deciding point is blood results showing marrow suppression or a haematopoietic malignancy, meaning two or more abnormal among red cells, white cells and platelets[10]. Taiwan's PEL-TWA for benzene is 1 ppm (3.2 mg/m³)[10][11].

Being put in Grade 3 or 4 is not a notice that leukaemia is on the way. It is the signal that starts a process: occupational medicine assessment, repeat testing, follow-up, and a look back at the working environment — ventilation and local exhaust, protective equipment, and where necessary a change of duties. That 0.5 ppm is a management trigger, not a safety threshold; WHO has already said benzene has no lower safe bound[3].

I have met people who want to resign on the strength of that report. That is usually the worst option available: the moment you leave, the environmental improvement and follow-up your employer owes you leave with you. Go to occupational medicine first. Those who have already left still have a route, but the name has to be right: people in the job get a "preventive occupational disease health examination", and among the categories announced by the Bureau of Labor Insurance benzene is category 16, trichloroethylene and tetrachloroethylene category 10[12]; what you can apply for after changing operation, leaving the job or withdrawing from the insurance is a different thing, a "preventive occupational disease health follow-up examination". One word apart in the name, but a different desk and different eligibility — how to go about it and what to bring is written out in "Leave that asbestos roof alone", and for the number of examinations and which categories of operation qualify, ask the Bureau of Labor Insurance directly. That is the one part of this topic where being late means missing it.

If a blood cancer really is diagnosed, occupational disease recognition is still a route. OSHA's Reference Guideline for the Recognition of Occupational Benzene Poisoning requires exposure history, clinical findings, blood or marrow abnormality, biological monitoring and environmental measurement all together, and mentions an induction period for leukaemia of about 5 years, with 3 years or more of exposure required[11]. How to prepare is, again, in "Leave that asbestos roof alone".

Degreaser, dry-cleaning solvent, sterilising gas: what each one damages

Benzene is not the only one. From the IARC classification list: trichloroethylene (TCE) Group 1, tetrachloroethylene (PCE) Group 2A, 1,3-butadiene Group 1, ethylene oxide Group 1[13].

Trichloroethylene is a common solvent for metal degreasing and cleaning. OSHA's Reference Guideline for the Recognition of Occupational Trichloroethylene Poisoning states that IARC lists it as Group 1 and that the target is renal cell carcinoma; the evidence for non-Hodgkin lymphoma and hepatocellular carcinoma is weaker, but they are still held to be related. Taiwan's PEL-TWA for TCE is 50 ppm[14]. Item 7.17 of the Schedule of Occupational Diseases likewise corresponds to kidney cancer[2].

Tetrachloroethylene is the solvent the dry-cleaning trade has used from the 1950s to today. IARC Monograph 106 notes its association with bladder cancer, oesophageal cancer and non-Hodgkin lymphoma, but the final classification stops at Group 2A, not Group 1[13][15] — I have heard dry-cleaning solvent called Group 1 too many times.

Exposure to 1,3-butadiene is concentrated in rubber manufacturing and petrochemicals; IARC Monograph 100F lists it as Group 1, with sufficient evidence for cancers of the haematolymphatic organs[16]. The same volume also lists ethylene oxide — used in hospitals for gas sterilisation of heat-sensitive instruments — as Group 1, but this particular Group 1 deserves spelling out: the human evidence reaches only the "limited" grade (lymphohaematopoietic malignancies and breast cancer), and the classification rests on animal evidence and genotoxic mechanism data[17]. Both correspond to blood cancer, at items 7.21 and 7.20 of the Schedule of Occupational Diseases[2].

That bottle you wash your hands with can be swapped today

  • Stop washing your hands in degreaser, thinner or any solvent. This is the easiest exposure to change; switch to an industrial hand cleaner and the oil still comes off.

  • If spraying, degreasing or parts cleaning happens in an enclosed space, deal with ventilation and local exhaust before you talk about masks. Engineering control comes before protective equipment, and "The price of cutting one stone benchtop" says why.

  • Check whether your work has been scheduled into the special health examination. On the report, read the "health management grade" column first, not just the figures printed in red.

  • Do not pay for a urinary benzene metabolite test (tt-MA, S-PMA) of your own accord. It only means something alongside workplace environmental measurement; on its own it manufactures anxiety.

  • At home, deal first with the garage that opens indoors and with second-hand smoke inside the house; the magnitude is far above the petrol station[3].

I cannot tell you that you will certainly not get it; benzene genuinely has no lower safe bound. What I can give you is the magnitude: in an environment measured and managed under the law, the overwhelming majority of exposed people will not get leukaemia from benzene. What can change today is the bottle by the sink, the door to the garage, and whether anyone helps you read the next health examination report.

From the physician

I do not want you finishing this and paying for a urinary benzene metabolite test. That figure only means something alongside workplace environmental measurement, and one isolated test produces nothing but anxiety. What is worth doing is confirming whether your work has been scheduled into the special health examination, and whether anyone reads the management grade column with you when the report comes back.
