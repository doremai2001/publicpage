AFTER THE DIAGNOSIS

Will I need a stoma, and when will anyone know

The answer depends on tumour height, sphincter involvement, response to treatment and the bowel control you already have — and why shrinkage is not a promise to save the sphincter.

Rectal Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 14 references
#Stoma#Surgery#Decisions#Bowel Function

Separate two things before surgery rather than after it: keeping the sphincter and controlling the bowel are different outcomes. Most people ask about the first, and it is the second that shapes the next ten years.

This is close to the first question asked in rectal cancer clinic, and the family often asks first: "Doctor, will I (will he) need a stoma?" I know the word you want is no. But an answer at the first visit would likely be wrong, and wrong in the direction that makes you feel better.

So this piece lays out what decides the question: when the answer appears, what produces it, and what to ask before then.

Two conditions must hold, and nobody tells you the second

The 2024 guidelines of the Japanese Society for Cancer of the Colon and Rectum (JSCCR) give two conditions for sphincter-preserving surgery, both of which must hold: resection with no oncological remnant, meaning both the distal and the circumferential resection margins are negative; and anal function that can be maintained after the operation[1].

The second is overlooked longest. Even where the join is technically possible, if your continence was already poor — age, childbirth history, sphincter damage, long-standing diarrhoea — you may afterwards be at the toilet twenty times a day, leaking at night, afraid to leave the house. That is not "saved"; it is a different problem, and it belongs in the conversation before surgery.

The first variable is measured in centimetres

How far the lower edge of the tumour sits from the anal verge is the strongest single factor. In a Korean series of 409 patients with tumours within 3 cm of the anal verge, 335 (81.9%) had an abdominoperineal resection and only 18.1% sphincter-saving surgery[2]. Within 5 cm of the anorectal junction, in a US multicentre database of 431 stage I–III patients, the rate was 35.7%[3].

Within 3 cm the tumour is already against the sphincter complex; a clear margin means taking the sphincter with it, and then nothing is left to preserve. Not surgical skill — anatomy.

Three operations, and why the names matter

  • Low anterior resection (LAR): the rectal segment with the tumour is removed and the bowel above joined to what remains, anus preserved. The lower the tumour, the lower the join, and the higher the functional price.

  • Intersphincteric resection (ISR): for lower tumours part of the internal sphincter is taken as well. JSCCR's conditions are specific: a clear circumferential margin must be achievable, meaning the external sphincter and levator ani are not invaded; the distal margin generally 2 cm or more for T2/T3 and 1 cm or more for T1. For poorly differentiated cancers, or where the sphincter is already involved, ISR is not recommended[1].

  • Abdominoperineal resection (APR): the anus and sphincter are removed with the tumour and a permanent colostomy is formed.

The colorectal cancer guideline of the Taipei Medical University healthcare system (臺北醫學大學醫療體系), which includes Wan Fang Hospital (萬芳醫院), writes these as 根除性手術切除 (radical resection), 保留肛門手術 (sphincter-preserving surgery) and 腸造口手術 (stoma surgery), without acronyms[4] — mostly what you will see on the consent form.

"If chemoradiation shrinks the tumour, doesn't that save the sphincter?"

This is the passage in the series where I least want to be vague. The answer: randomised trials have not shown that.

A systematic review of 10 randomised trials and 4,596 patients tested exactly this. In the all-randomised analyses the tumours in the experimental arms shrank, but not one trial produced a statistically significant increase in the anterior resection rate. Three trials marked out in advance the patients judged to need an abdominoperineal resection; only the German CAO/ARO/AIO-94 trial showed a higher rate there, as a secondary endpoint with features that may have inflated the estimate. The French Lyon R90-01 and Polish trials, both designed for this question, did not confirm it. The randomised evidence does not support preoperative radiotherapy raising the anterior resection rate[5].

So what does it give you? In the 823 patients of the German CAO/ARO/AIO-94 trial: five-year local recurrence 6% against 13%, and less toxicity than the same treatment after surgery; but survival, at five years and again at eleven, was the same in both arms[6][7]. The full figures and the reasoning are in "How the treatment order is set, and why it is not a menu".

Turning an abdominoperineal resection into a low anterior resection cannot be relied on. I would rather disappoint you now than have you wake from surgery and find you were promised something nobody can guarantee.

An APR is not the losing side

Where the sphincter was never involved, the two operations give the same cancer outcomes. In those 431 US Rectal Cancer Consortium patients with tumours within 5 cm, abdominoperineal against low anterior resection gave a disease-free survival hazard ratio of 0.90 (95% CI 0.53–1.52, P=.70) and overall survival 1.29 (0.71–2.32, P=.39), with no difference in complications or stay[3]. The same for intersphincteric against abdominoperineal resection: 20 non-randomised studies, 1,217 against 1,135 patients, five-year disease-free survival hazard ratio 0.84 (0.55–1.29), overall survival 0.93 (0.60–1.46), neither significant[8]. The caution: these were not randomised, and choosing who gets an intersphincteric resection filters out patients in poorer shape — they show it is safe in the right patients, not that it is better.

How many "temporary" stomas are never reversed

With a low join, the surgeon often brings out a temporary ileostomy to divert stool and protect the anastomosis below.

Across 19 studies and 9,932 patients having restorative rectal cancer surgery with a defunctioning ileostomy, 18.7% (95% CI 15.3–22.5) still had the stoma beyond 12 months. Three risk factors each carried roughly fivefold odds: local or systemic recurrence, anastomotic leak, stage IV at diagnosis[9]. A Spanish study of 549 patients used a different definition: 10.4% still had a permanent stoma two years after sphincter-preserving surgery. A prediction model from the Swedish registry achieved an AUC of only 0.63 on external validation[10].

Neither figure applies directly to you; the definitions differ. An AUC of 0.63 means, in plain language, that for predicting whether this person's stoma becomes permanent the best tool we have is a little better than a coin toss. Closing a stoma is no small matter: across 48 studies and 6,107 ileostomy closures, morbidity was 17.3%, mortality 0.4%, small bowel obstruction 7.2%, wound sepsis 5.0%, and 3.7% needed conversion to a laparotomy during the closure itself[11].

Keeping the sphincter is not controlling it

Across 21 studies and 3,730 patients, major low anterior resection syndrome (LARS) reached three to four in ten, individual studies ranging from 12% to 70% — decimal places are meaningless here, only the order of magnitude. Two independent risk factors: a tumour less than 5 cm from the anal margin (P<.001), and having had preoperative chemoradiation (P=.006)[12].

Look at who those factors describe: the people most desperate to avoid a stoma, and most likely to be offered preoperative chemoradiation. What days with major LARS look like, whether it improves, and why some patients look back and find life better with a stoma, is in "Keeping the anus is not the same as keeping control". Remember one sentence: "the sphincter was preserved" and "bowel control is manageable" are two different outcomes, and they have to be asked about separately.

Some will have heard of watch and wait, where a very good response means no operation. It exists, but it is not a way of avoiding a stoma: in the International Watch & Wait Database, 880 patients from 47 institutes with a clinical complete response, two-year cumulative local regrowth was 25.2% (22.2–28.5)[13]. JSCCR is conservative too: non-operative management "should not be performed casually" in cStage I to III rectal cancer, and belongs in prospective trials with written protocols and objective assessment criteria[1]. That subject has its own article.

When the answer arrives, and what to do today

The timeline: once the pelvic MRI is back we know how far the tumour sits from the sphincter and whether the external sphincter and levator ani are involved, which narrows things considerably. Then the multidisciplinary team meeting — colorectal core members include colorectal surgery, radiation oncology, medical oncology, imaging and pathology, and Taiwan's cancer care accreditation requires a set proportion of new patients discussed there before treatment begins[14]. If preoperative treatment is planned, add the reassessment after it; in a few cases the final answer comes on the operating table.

Before then, the most direct thing is to ask the team which of three answers applies on current imaging: near-certain permanent stoma, near-certain none, or genuinely either. Those three call for completely different preparation.

In either of the last two, meet the stoma nurse (造口護理師) before surgery for site marking. Marked stomas have fewer complications and less difficulty with self-care — the numbers are in "Just diagnosed, and nobody has touched the tumour yet" — and the mark must be made while you stand, sit and bend forward, so the only chance is before surgery. One last thing only you can do: describe your bowel habit honestly — how many times a day, whether you leak, whether there is night-time incontinence. That is the raw material for the second condition, and nobody can answer it for you.

From the physician

The proportions here are population figures, not your probability — the best individual prediction model reaches an AUC of only 0.63. Ask before surgery which of the three answers you fall under, and ask for the stoma nurse (造口護理師) to mark the site while you can still stand, sit and bend.
