THE LOW-RISK BOX: HOW AGGRESSIVE TO BE

Half the gland, or all of it

In 2025 the line moved to 2 cm and the verb went from "may be sufficient" to "should be a lobectomy"; whether you end up on a tablet after a half depends on where the TSH goal is set.

Thyroid Cancer Guide · The low-risk box: how aggressive to be · 15 references
#Surgery#Decisions#Evidence#Endocrine Therapy

You assume all of it is cleaner and half of it is the easy option. There is no randomised trial under this line at all; and "more than half of lobectomy patients ended up on thyroid hormone" is a figure from the old TSH target. Set the goal where the 2025 edition sets it, and seven to eight in ten do not need the tablet.

"Doctor, do I have half taken out or all of it?" Almost everyone asks this, and most add a second line: "Isn't taking it all out cleaner?"

Clean is a hard word to argue with. But there is a line for this written into the guideline, and in 2025 both the position of that line and the verb attached to it changed. This article covers where the line sits, what each side of it costs, and the box most often misread — whether you end up on a tablet after half. Whether to operate at all is in 〈Watching instead of operating, on conditions〉, whether the nodes are dealt with at the same time is in 〈Should the nodes come out too〉, and the real chance of each complication is in 〈What the operation takes with it〉.

In 2025 the line moved to 2 cm, and the verb changed with it

Recommendation 35B of the 2015 edition said this: for a tumour larger than 1 cm and smaller than 4 cm, with no extrathyroidal extension and no clinical nodal metastasis, the initial operation can be bilateral or unilateral; for low-risk papillary and follicular cancers, lobectomy alone — which is what the guideline calls the operation people call a half — "may be sufficient". Strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence[1]. The operative words are "can" and "may".

The 2025 edition splits that whole box in two. Recommendation 15A: for cancer 2 cm or under, without gross extrathyroidal extension and without nodal or distant metastasis, the initial surgical procedure should be a thyroid lobectomy, unless there are cancers on both sides or another reason to remove the opposite lobe. Strong recommendation, moderate certainty evidence. Recommendation 15B handles the box above 2 cm and up to 4 cm: lobectomy may be the preferred initial treatment, because the risk and side effects are significantly lower; but the patient and the treatment team may still adopt total thyroidectomy in order to give radioiodine or to make follow-up easier to read. That box is a conditional recommendation on low-to-moderate certainty[2].

So the verb went from "may be sufficient" to "should be a lobectomy" at 2 cm and under. Above 4 cm, or any size with gross extrathyroidal extension, or clinically apparent nodal metastasis, or distant metastasis, Recommendation 15C says the initial procedure should include a total thyroidectomy. Strong recommendation, moderate certainty[2].

Under that line there is no randomised trial

The shape of the evidence decides how you should read every number below. The 2025 edition says so itself: because outcomes in low-risk differentiated thyroid cancer are generally good and randomised controlled trials are lacking, the panel had to rely on large database studies[2].

The one registered randomised attempt is a feasibility pilot at a single American centre. Its primary endpoint is the proportion of eligible patients who agree to enrol — that is, it was testing whether a proper trial could be run at all; the enrolment recorded on the registry is 2, and the study closed in January 2026; that field is a count of people enrolled, and the registry gives no separate figure for how many were randomised[3][4]. So on "which is better, half or all", there is still no randomised evidence to stand on.

What the registries said, and what they did not

The 2007 analysis of 52,173 papillary cancers in the American national cancer database is the classic statement of the old idea that anything over 1 cm needs everything out: under 1 cm, extent of surgery affected neither recurrence nor survival; at 1 cm and above, lobectomy carried higher recurrence and higher mortality, and that held in the 1 to 2 cm box as well[5].

In 2014 the same database was worked again: 61,775 papillary cancers of 1.0 to 4.0 cm diagnosed 1998 to 2006, median follow-up 82 months. After adjustment, overall survival showed no difference between the two operations: across all 1.0 to 4.0 cm, hazard ratio 0.96 (95% confidence interval 0.84 to 1.09); 1.0 to 2.0 cm, 1.05 (0.88 to 1.26); 2.1 to 4.0 cm, 0.89 (0.73 to 1.07). All three sit on 1[6]. A 2015 paper looking at people under 45 with stage I disease of 1.1 to 4.0 cm found the same[7].

Two things have to be said alongside those three papers. First, their endpoint is overall survival, not disease-specific survival — most people with differentiated thyroid cancer die of something else, so overall survival gets heavily diluted. Second, the two groups were never the same people to begin with: in the 2014 paper, the total thyroidectomy group had nodal metastasis in 27% against 7% in the lobectomy group, extrathyroidal extension 16% against 5%, multifocality 44% against 29%[6]. Surgeons were already sending the worse-looking disease for total thyroidectomy. That is confounding by indication, and no database is large enough to make it go away. One more caution: "stage I" in the 2015 paper is the AJCC seventh edition with its cut-off at 45, not the eighth edition's 55, so it cannot be held up against a report written today[7].

Whether you need the tablet after half depends on where the target sits

First, the thing that changes every number below it. The 2025 edition writes that for people whose recurrence risk falls in the low or intermediate band — that is the older three-band wording, used here as the guideline uses it — the goal for thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH, the signal the pituitary sends to make the thyroid work) is within the normal reference range, whether the operation was a total thyroidectomy or a lobectomy; and that prior studies suggest with the goal set inside the normal range, about seven to eight in ten people who have a lobectomy can avoid thyroid hormone supplementation, while with the goal set in that older, lower band, only two to three in ten can[2]. Every cohort below used the old target as its threshold. So what they answer is "under the old target, how many people failed to meet it" — not "after a half, how many people have no choice but to take the tablet".

The figure most often quoted is a 2012 meta-analysis of 32 studies: overall risk of hypothyroidism after lobectomy 22% (95% confidence interval 19 to 27); only 4 of those studies separated clinical from subclinical, and they put subclinical at 12% and clinical at 4%[8]. That figure mixes benign and malignant disease, and its endpoint is being diagnosed with hypothyroidism, not being put on a tablet. The 2025 edition, restating this paper, labels the 22% as biochemical hypothyroidism, which is not what the paper says, so what I have quoted is the paper.

The threshold for the next two is the old target line, TSH above 2 mIU/L. In a series of 555 lobectomies, of the 478 people not already on thyroid hormone before surgery, 350 (73%) had TSH rise above 2 mIU/L within a year[9]; another cohort restricted to low-risk differentiated thyroid cancer ran higher still, and I have put its numbers and its premises in 〈The tablet you take for life, and how much〉[10]. The proportion actually prescribed something is a different number: among 33,756 lobectomies in a national claims dataset, 59.3% of those operated on for malignancy took thyroid hormone against 39.4% of those operated on for benign disease, adjusted odds ratio 2.34 (95% confidence interval 2.20 to 2.48)[11] — and that dataset does not tell us where anyone's target had been set.

Who is more likely to be found hypothyroid afterwards? People whose TSH was already on the high side before surgery. In a meta-analysis, a preoperative TSH above 2.5 gave a relative risk of 3.16 (2.03 to 4.90), positive thyroid antibodies 3.52, thyroiditis on the pathology 3.30; the endpoint for that set is being diagnosed with hypothyroidism, not starting a tablet[12]. A relative risk is one group's proportion divided by the other group's proportion — not the same arithmetic as the hazard ratio above, which compares the rate at which events arrive. So what a half spares you is not "a tablet for life", it is the certain lifelong replacement that follows taking the whole gland out. How much to take, and why the 2025 edition does not suggest long-term TSH suppression for low or intermediate risk without evidence of recurrence, is in that same article.

If it comes back after a half, where it usually comes back

The 2025 edition is explicit: most recurrences after lobectomy alone appear in the opposite lobe, and most are successfully salvaged by completion thyroidectomy[2]. In a 30-year Japanese cohort, of 1,327 people with low-risk papillary microcarcinoma who had a lobectomy, 18 (1.4%) recurred in the remaining thyroid[13].

Follow-up after a half is not lighter. It is aimed at different things — with half a thyroid still in place, thyroglobulin is read against a different baseline than after a total.

Completion surgery: how many get there, and what it costs

If you choose a half, know in advance that a second operation is on the table. The ranges the 2025 edition gives: 5% to 43% in retrospective studies, 11% to 34% in meta-analyses; about 21% converted to total thyroidectomy during the operation because higher-risk features turned up, and about 27% to 30% completed later on the basis of final pathology. The guideline writes one sentence meant to be said to patients in advance — they must be aware of at least a one-in-five possibility of conversion during surgery or completion afterwards[2].

Is that second operation dangerous? In a single-institution series of 786, the 149 who had a lobectomy then a completion had less temporary hypoparathyroidism than the 637 who had a total thyroidectomy in one sitting[14]. In a national surgical quality registry of 70,638, 30-day mortality was 0.1% for both, and overall complications were comparable[15]; but that registry does not record hypoparathyroidism or recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy, so the safety it describes cannot be used to talk about voice and calcium.

What each side of the line costs

Set against lobectomy, total thyroidectomy carries these pooled relative risks: temporary low calcium close to tenfold, permanent low calcium about threefold, recurrent laryngeal nerve injury and postoperative bleeding both between about 1.7 and 2.6[12]. I am deliberately not giving absolute percentages, because those depend on how that hospital examines the larynx and on how many months it counts as permanent — every denominator and every definition is worked through in 〈What the operation takes with it〉.

How much to take out: who to ask, and what to ask

You can read these out. "How many centimetres does mine measure? Does it fall in the 2 cm or under box?" "Do you think I need radioiodine? If I do, does that mean everything has to come out?" "If the frozen section or the final pathology turns up higher-risk features, will you advise a completion? Roughly what are the odds?" "If I have a half, when will you check my TSH, and above what value will you start me on a tablet?" And one I would like you to say out loud: "If I choose the half, what am I giving up?"

From the physician

You can read these out at your next appointment: "How many centimetres does mine measure? Does it fall in the 2 cm or under box?" "If the final pathology turns up higher-risk features, will you advise a completion thyroidectomy, and roughly what are the odds?" "If I have a half, where will you set my TSH goal, and above what value will you start me on a tablet?"
