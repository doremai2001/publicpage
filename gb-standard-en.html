WHAT THE EVIDENCE SAYS

Six weeks together, then six cycles

In the concurrent stretch the tablets are taken every day, weekends included; in maintenance, on five days out of twenty-eight. 60 Gy has a randomised trial holding it up, and every attempt so far at pushing the dose higher has come back negative.

Glioblastoma Guide · What the Evidence Says · 15 references
#IMRT#Chemotherapy#Treatment Pathway#Evidence

There is no six months anywhere in the Taiwanese reimbursement clause — what it sets out is prior approval every three months, with imaging attached for each renewal. This article breaks the course into its three stretches, and answers head-on whether you can keep taking the drug for longer.

"Doctor, how long am I doing this for?" I get asked that every week. Eight people in ten have remembered "six weeks" and "six months" and have not remembered the thing underneath: the two stretches of drug are taken in completely different ways. In the concurrent stretch the tablets are taken every day, weekends included; in maintenance they are taken on five days out of twenty-eight. I have seen both ways of getting that wrong — someone stopping the drug over the weekend on their own, and someone taking the maintenance drug to the rhythm of the concurrent stretch.

My position first: the radiotherapy part of glioblastoma treatment is the part I do myself; the other side of every comparison in this topic is my neurosurgical colleagues' operation and my neuro-oncology colleagues' drugs. Tumour-treating fields I do not use, and they are not a service of my department. So every comparison comes with its primary source, so that you can take it to the surgical and neuro-oncology clinics for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

Six weeks, a break, six cycles: that is three stretches, not one

The regimen the whole world runs today comes from the EORTC 26981-22981 / NCIC CE.3 trial published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2005: 85 centres, 573 patients with newly diagnosed glioblastoma, median age 56, 84% of whom had had debulking surgery[1]. It looks like this:

  • Six weeks of radiotherapy: 2 Gy a day, five days a week, 60 Gy in 30 fractions in total[1].

  • Concurrent temozolomide: 75 mg/m², from the first day of radiotherapy to the last, with no break in between. The EANO 2021 guideline spells it out further — including weekends[1][2].

  • Maintenance: six cycles, each cycle 28 days, drug taken on the first 5 days only, at 150 to 200 mg/m²[1][2].

Those three lines describe the structure of the course in that trial, so that you can see the two stretches of drug run to different rhythms; the mg/m² above are the doses in the trial protocol, not your prescription. How many capsules you actually take, and which days you hold, are decided by your doctor from your body surface area, your blood counts and your liver and kidney function, and they are written on your medicine bag — if your medicine bag differs from this article, the bag wins. Do not add, do not cut, and do not make up a dose you missed.

Between the end of radiotherapy and the first maintenance cycle there is a break. Trial protocols usually write four weeks[3], while GBM AGILE, the international platform trial still recruiting, writes 2 to 6 weeks from the last day of radiotherapy for its control arm[4]. So "four weeks off" is a common value, not a rule; your dates are set by the team from your counts and your recovery. As for which machine treats you and what the technique is called, I am not writing about which machine: the public evidence answers questions about dose and fractionation, and it does not answer questions about equipment.

Adding temozolomide moved median overall survival from 12.1 months to 14.6, and the proportion still alive at two years from 10.4% to 26.5%[1]; the five-year cell is 1.9% against 9.8%[5]. These numbers are the spread of a group of people, not anybody's deadline — how to read them is in "That number, and how I am going to tell you about it".

That 60 Gy is pinned down by a randomised trial

The MRC BR2 trial in Britain, published in 1991, assigned 474 patients with grade 3 and grade 4 astrocytoma 2:1 at random to 60 Gy in 30 fractions over six weeks or 45 Gy in 20 fractions over four weeks, with neither arm given adjuvant chemotherapy. Median survival went from 9 months to 12, hazard ratio 0.75, P=0.007[6]. A hazard ratio is not "the probability becomes 0.75 times as big"; it says that within the same stretch of time, events happened more slowly. EANO's own sentence points the same way: 60 Gy given in 1.8 to 2 Gy fractions roughly doubles median survival[2].

But that trial left a line of its own: "late complications were not assessed"[6]. So "the higher dose did not add short-term side effects" cannot be stretched into "there are no long-term ones"; that part is in "Memory, concentration, and changes in who he is".

Push the dose higher and four different attempts have come back empty

Four routes have been tried for pushing past 60 Gy, and the direction they all point is almost uncomfortable. The first was adding a stereotactic radiosurgery boost to standard radiotherapy: RTOG 93-05 enrolled 203 people, median survival 13.5 against 13.6 months, P=0.5711, with no difference in survival rates or in patterns of failure[7]. The second was changing the way the dose is split up: the 2020 Cochrane review of 11 randomised trials and 2,062 people found the hyperfractionation and accelerated radiotherapy data could not even be pooled, and were not enough to draw a conclusion from[8]. The third was topping up the dose on the operating table: INTRAGO-II in Lancet Oncology in 2026, 298 people, median progression-free survival 11.0 against 11.4 months, hazard ratio 1.1, P=0.47; radiation necrosis in 11 cases against 3. The authors' own conclusion was that this "questions the value of further local dose intensification in resectable glioblastoma"[9].

The fourth is the one many people have heard of, NRG-BN001. It has read out; the results are posted on the trials registry and there is no formal paper yet[10]. 431 patients were analysable, and the design randomised separately within photon centres and within proton centres. In the photon half: standard 16.3 months against dose-escalated 18.8, hazard ratio 0.95 (95% confidence interval 0.81 to 1.10 — the bracket is the plausible range for the estimate, not your personal probability, and it crosses 1, which means even the direction is uncertain), log-rank P=0.25 — no difference[10].

The proton half's numbers look better (22.0 against 22.8 months, hazard ratio 0.81, log-rank P=0.11 — by this trial's pre-specified test, not significant), and this is exactly where I want you to be careful. The protocol states that the two experimental arms would only be compared with each other if each had first beaten its own control arm; that condition was not met, so by protocol, protons were never compared with photons[10]. And both of the proton centres' arms sat higher than both of the photon centres' arms — the proton centres' control arm was also better (22.0 against 16.3 months), and that is a centre effect, not a beam effect. The registry also states that the proton centres accrued more slowly than expected, so the sample size was reduced and follow-up extended[10]. Where protons stand in this disease is in "Fields, immunotherapy, vaccines, protons, BNCT: where each one stands".

Starting late and breaking off midway are two different questions

"It has been three weeks from surgery to radiotherapy — is that too long?" A 2009 JCO analysis used the RTOG database, 16 trials and 2,855 patients with supratentorial glioblastoma, split into four groups by the interval from surgery to radiotherapy, and saw no worsening of survival with a later start; the group who waited more than four weeks in fact had higher median survival than the group treated within two weeks (12.5 against 9.2 months, P<0.0001)[11]. That sentence must not be read as "starting later is better". It is a retrospective analysis, enrolment was restricted to within six weeks in the first place, and people in poorer condition get booked into radiotherapy sooner — that is selection bias. The authors themselves went no further than "a short delay in starting radiotherapy, within this relatively narrow six-week window, did not appear to compromise survival"[11]. A Brazilian retrospective series of 115 patients points the same way, 13.5 against 14.2 months for within six weeks and after, P=0.470, with the things associated with survival in the multivariable model being performance status, extent of resection and whether adjuvant treatment was given[12].

As for "does stopping for three days in the middle matter" — I could not find data on that in glioblastoma, not one paper. Other cancers have studies of overall treatment time; those numbers were not calculated for this disease and I am not going to borrow them. What I can give you is one line of clinical common sense: don't break if you don't have to. If you genuinely have to (counts too low, an infection, not coping), the team decides how it is made up, not you.

"Can I keep taking it for a few more months" — that has two layers

A 2017 Neuro-Oncology paper pooled individual patient data from four randomised trials and picked out 624 people still free of progression after the sixth cycle: 291 carried on, 333 stopped at six. After adjusting for age, performance status, extent of resection and MGMT status, progression-free survival was slightly better (hazard ratio 0.80, P=0.03; in the MGMT-methylated group, 0.65), but overall survival was unaffected (hazard ratio 0.92, P=0.52)[13]. Note that whether someone carried on was the local doctor's decision, not a random assignment[13].

The genuinely randomised one is the Spanish GEINO 14-01: 159 people free of progression after six cycles, assigned at random to stop or to continue to 12 cycles. The primary endpoint, six-month progression-free survival, was 55.7% against 61.3%, no difference; overall survival showed no difference either, while the continuation arm had significantly more lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia and nausea and vomiting[14]. What the authors wrote was: more toxicity, no added benefit[14].

"When is the next MRI booked" is also a question about getting the drug

This part of the Taiwanese system does not look much like the international protocol, and almost no patient knows it. Clause 9.25 of Section 9 of the NHI drug reimbursement regulations covers temozolomide. Item two reads — this is my rendering of the clause — "newly diagnosed glioblastoma multiforme, given concurrently with radiotherapy and then as adjuvant treatment", and item three reads "may be used after prior approval, maximum daily dose 200mg/m2. Each prior-approval application is limited to a course of three months; a further application must present objective evidence (for example, imaging) confirming the absence of progression before use may continue"[15].

There is no six months in the clause, and no six cycles either. The rhythm you will actually meet is a gate every three months, with imaging exchanged for the next stretch of drug. So when your follow-up MRI is booked is not only about your disease; it also decides whether your drug supply joins up — the other side of that is in "How the follow-up scans are scheduled, and why bigger is not always worse". Incidentally, the clause still uses the old name 多形神經膠母細胞瘤, glioblastoma multiforme[15] — that is the pace at which administration catches up, and it does not mean the scope of cover is wider or narrower.

One more thing not to miss. In those six concurrent weeks, grade 3 or 4 haematological toxicity ran at 7% in the original trial[1] — not a high proportion, but if it lands on you it is 100%. What to do about fever, unusual bruising or bleeding is in "When to come back the same day: three tiers", and please read that before anything happens. One sentence you can get out at your next clinic visit: how far does my prior approval run, and which scan do I need to bring for the next renewal?

From the physician

What actually goes wrong in this regimen is not the dose, it is the weekend cell. Keep two pieces of paper: the radiotherapy schedule and the chemotherapy instructions. Every day in the concurrent stretch, five days out of twenty-eight in maintenance — compare them once and you have it. When your medicine bag differs from any article, the bag wins. And if fever or unusual bruising turns up during those six concurrent weeks, do not wait for the next appointment.
