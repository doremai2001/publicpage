BEYOND STANDARD TREATMENT

CAR-T is not the same thing as cell therapy

Special review, leukapheresis, cells sent away to be engineered, then weeks of monitoring: where it sits after standard treatment, and its two toxicities.

Situation Guides · Beyond Standard Treatment · 8 references
#Cell therapy#Newer therapies#Immunotherapy#Regulation#Reading evidence

One idea has to be blocked first: do this and skip the earlier lines. The provision runs the other way — reimbursement applies only after the specified standard treatment has failed or relapsed, so skipping makes you ineligible. And weeks pass between approval and infusion; in ZUMA-1, 9% never got there.

Search "cell therapy" and you will turn up two completely different things at once, and the standard of proof behind them is a whole level apart. There is one sentence that separates them: have the cells being put back in had their genes altered?

The difference is in the gene, not in the cell

CAR-T works like this: the patient's own T cells are collected, a viral vector is used to put a chimeric antigen receptor gene into them so the receptor recognises one surface antigen (the product currently reimbursed in Taiwan recognises CD19 on B cells), and after expansion they are infused back. The engineered T cell can grab its target directly, without needing human leukocyte antigen presentation.

Because the genes have been altered, it travels the drug licence road. Article 3 of the Regenerative Medicine Products Act (再生醫療製劑條例) states plainly that regenerative medicine products are drugs within Article 6 of the Pharmaceutical Affairs Act, and Article 6 requires registration and a drug licence[1] (effective 1 January 2026, verified 2026-08-30). What the Regulations Governing Specific Medical Techniques (特管辦法) govern are cells produced by non-genetic-engineering manipulation[2] — CIK, DC and NK sit on that side, under a technical-practice permit, not a drug licence, which is "The regulator approved it — approved what, exactly?". A drug licence has to be bought with clinical trials. A technical permit does not. That is the most important sentence in this article.

Between deciding and infusing, there are several weeks

First, where it stands: both reimbursed indications require that the specified standard treatment has failed or relapsed, or that specialists have confirmed it cannot be given — and before infusion there is still a round of lymphodepleting chemotherapy. It is the step after standard treatment, not a way of getting the earlier lines out of the way. Skipping them makes you ineligible for reimbursement. In Taiwan the process starts with prior special case review, which requires documents including the minutes of a multidisciplinary team meeting. Only after that is approved are T cells collected by leukapheresis, and the NHI provision even specifies the blood counts on collection day (thresholds written with the same symbols as the text): neutrophils ≥1000/µL, lymphocytes ≥100/µL, haemoglobin ≥8.0 g/dL, platelets ≥50000/µL[3] (115.08.21 version, verified 2026-08-30).

The cells then go to a centralised manufacturing site, and that takes weeks. The wait is itself a real risk: in the ZUMA-1 trial, 110 of 111 enrolled patients (99%) had a product successfully manufactured, but only 101 (91%) were actually infused — the gap is progression and death[4]. The Taiwanese time limit is that infusion must be completed within six months of the special review being approved[3].

Before infusion comes low-dose cyclophosphamide and fludarabine to "deplete lymphocytes" and make room. The infusion itself happens once — the NHI wording is one course in a lifetime[3]. The laborious part is the two to four weeks of inpatient monitoring afterwards.

What the ward is watching for is these two things

Cytokine release syndrome is a supraphysiological inflammatory reaction that starts with fever and, when severe, drops the blood pressure and the oxygen saturation. ICANS (immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome) is confusion, loss of speech, tremor, seizures, and in severe cases cerebral oedema.

Read the numbers carefully. ELIANA, in children and young adults with relapsed or refractory B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (75 infused): cytokine release syndrome in 77%, of whom 48% received tocilizumab; neurological events 40%, no cerebral oedema; grade 3 to 4 suspected treatment-related adverse events 73%[5]. ZUMA-1, in refractory large B-cell lymphoma (101 infused): grade 3 or worse cytokine release syndrome 13%, grade 3 or worse neurological events 28%, grade 3 or worse neutropenia 78%, anaemia 43%, thrombocytopenia 38%, and three patients died during treatment[4].

The percentages from the two trials cannot be compared with each other, because the grading scales in use at the time differed; the unified ASTCT consensus grading only appeared in 2019[6]. Taiwan writes readiness into the provision instead: a hospital must hold at least 10 vials of intravenous tocilizumab[3]. That is a concrete threshold a patient can ask about and can look up.

Effect: two single-arm phase 2 trials, and what they can and cannot say

ELIANA was a single-cohort phase 2 trial across 25 centres, 75 patients: overall remission within three months 81%, with minimal residual disease negative in every responder; six-month event-free survival 73% and overall survival 90%, which at twelve months became 50% and 76%[5]. ZUMA-1 was also single-arm phase 2: objective response 82%, complete response 54%, with 40% still in complete response at a median follow-up of 15.4 months, and 18-month overall survival 52%[4].

What this level of evidence supports is: "in this group of people who had run out of standard treatment, this proportion went into remission, and some of them stayed there a long time." 81% is a remission rate, not a cure rate; twelve-month event-free survival in the same trial is 50%. These trials had no second group to compare against, so they cannot answer "better than what". How new a technique is and whether it has beaten current practice are two separate lines — "New does not mean better".

Why solid tumours keep stalling

This is not pessimism; there are four obstacles you can name[7][8]. There is no marker that sits only on the cancer cell — solid tumour antigens are on normal tissue too, so the attack lands on both; the price of CD19 is losing your B cells, and that is the exception, not the rule. The marker is not uniform within one tumour, so aiming at a single target selects for the cells that escape. The cells cannot get in — leukaemia cells float in the blood, where the CAR-T lands; a solid tumour is a block of tissue, and the cells have to leave the bloodstream, cross abnormal vessels and push into dense stroma. And once in, they are suppressed: low oxygen, nutrients used up, TGF-β and assorted suppressive cells exhaust the T cells quickly.

In Section 9 of the NHI drug reimbursement rules there is no reimbursed CAR-T item for any solid tumour indication[3] (verified 2026-08-30); on the licensing side I verified nothing, so I assert nothing in either direction. Haematology numbers do not move across to solid tumours.

Taiwan reimburses one product, and the conditions are the real subject

In Section 9 of the NHI drug reimbursement rules there is only item 9.103, tisagenlecleucel (effective 2023-11-01, revised 2024-02-01 and 2025-08-01); I searched the whole section and found no other CAR-T product[3] (verified 2026-08-30). Which CAR-T products hold a Taiwanese drug licence, for what indications and from what date, I could not find an official record for, so I am writing neither that they are licensed nor that they are not. Ask a haematology-oncology team or the competent authority.

The conditions are written in great detail, and all of them can be checked and asked about in advance (thresholds again as the text writes them): B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia is limited to patients under 25, after two lines of standard treatment or with two transplant specialists confirming transplant is not possible, life expectancy ≥3 months, performance status ECOG under 2 (with three or more medical records), left ventricular ejection fraction >50%, oxygen saturation on room air >92%; diffuse large B-cell lymphoma is limited to relapse or progression after autologous transplant, or meeting one of five age and comorbidity-index criteria. Shared conditions include CD19 still expressed on the tumour, brain imaging beforehand to exclude central nervous system involvement, and no concurrent chemotherapy (other than the lymphodepleting course), immunosuppressants or targeted drugs; excluded are HIV infection, active hepatitis B or C, and primary central nervous system lymphoma[3]. On the hospital side: an accredited haematopoietic stem cell transplant training hospital, an average of ≥20 transplants a year over three years, a standing multidisciplinary team with ≥6 hours of training every two years, and from 民國118年1月1日 (1 January 2029) international accreditation of the cell collection and processing laboratory. Which products hold a licence, which are reimbursed, and which have been shown to work are three separate enquiries — "Approved, covered, effective: three different things".

What it costs to pay for privately I found no official source for, so I am not putting a figure here. How to get a written itemisation is in "How to ask about a self-pay item".

Before the referral, find out whether the exclusions rule you out

  1. Do I clear the exclusion criteria? Hepatitis viral load, central nervous system involvement, a history of autoimmune disease — these can be settled before any time is spent.

  2. Counting from today, how many weeks until infusion, and what holds my disease during that time? In ZUMA-1, 9% of patients never got there.

  3. Does this team and its drug stock meet what the provision requires? The tocilizumab holding, the transplant volume and the team training are thresholds written into the text: askable, and checkable.

From the physician

Check the exclusion criteria before anything else: age, brain imaging, hepatitis viral load, cardiac and oxygen thresholds are all written into the reimbursement text and can be looked up in advance. Read the effect figures precisely too — 81% is a remission rate, not a cure rate, twelve-month event-free survival in the same trial is 50%, and a single-arm trial has no second group to compare with.
