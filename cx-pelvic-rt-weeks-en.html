DURING TREATMENT

Getting through five or six weeks of pelvic radiotherapy

The whole series' emergency master table lives here: the fever threshold in degrees, what diarrhoea with dehydration looks like, which situations mean same-day contact. And why a course that stretches a week longer loses control rate.

Cervical Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 15 references
#Side Effects#Daily Life#Treatment Pathway

Weeks four and five, when it is at its worst, are exactly when most people think about taking leave. This article lays out the three pieces of evidence behind "do not interrupt on your own", and spells out how the "low-residue diet" convention has not caught up with the evidence — when it is bad, say so and let the team handle it, instead of handling it through absence.

My position first: definitive chemoradiation and brachytherapy are the treatments I deliver every day. So every sentence about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it — not more warmly.

Pelvic radiotherapy is a full-time assignment: reporting to the hospital five days a week, for five weeks running, with chemotherapy woven through once a week and brachytherapy to close the course. Two things need answering: what these five or six weeks will bring, and which situations mean contacting the team the same day — that list is the master table for this whole topic, and every other article points back here. And one sentence I want at the very front: this course can be paused by the team, but you cannot grant yourself leave from it. Why — the numbers are set out below.

First, the shape of the five or six weeks

The standard arrangement is external-beam radiotherapy of 45 Gy in 25 fractions, five a week for about five weeks, using techniques such as IMRT or VMAT with daily image guidance[1][2]; chemotherapy takes one day each week, and that day's own timetable is in "The weekly chemotherapy day". Brachytherapy is usually 3 to 4 sessions, scheduled towards the end of the course or completed within one to two weeks after it; why it cannot be skipped or replaced with an external-beam boost — the full evidence — is in "Brachytherapy: the scariest name, the decisive days". The guideline sets the ceiling on total treatment time, brachytherapy included, at seven weeks[1] — and that number is the protagonist of this article.

"I'm exhausted — I'll rest a week and carry on": look at the price tag first

Here sits one of the hardest pieces of data in radiobiology: when treatment stretches out, the tumour speeds up its regrowth, and the guideline's own wording is that delays and interruptions of treatment must be avoided, with total treatment time not exceeding seven weeks[1]. The numbers come from three sources, each with its own era label. From the radiotherapy-alone era: an analysis of 386 patients found that beyond 52 days, local control and overall survival each fell by roughly 1% per day of delay[3]; in another series of 209 patients, those finishing within 55 days had five-year survival of 65% against 54% for those who ran past it, and pelvic control of 87% against 72%[4]. The chemoradiation era did not make the numbers any kinder: in a retrospective analysis of 113 patients, those whose brachytherapy was not completed within 56 days had 3.8 times the pelvic recurrence risk of those who finished in time (95% confidence interval 1.2 to 16 — an interval that wide means the number can only be read for direction, not size), with three-year pelvic recurrence of 26% against 9%[5]. The "1% per day" is from an older era and the samples are each small — the exact value is not worth memorising; but the direction is consistent enough that the guideline wrote it straight into a ceiling. And that ceiling is not something that simply happens: in EMBRACE-I, 21% of patients overran the 50 days that EMBRACE-II set[2] — scheduling, public holidays, side-effect management: every link in the chain is racing the clock.

A pause the team calls and leave you grant yourself are two different things

When the blood counts are too low or the diarrhoea too fierce, the doctor will call a stop anyway — that is a monitored pause: we know how many days were lost and why, when they will be made up, and the later schedule is adjusted to chase them if necessary. The days you simply do not turn up are different: nobody knows, nothing is compensated, and nobody is treating the symptom that made you not want to come. So this article asks exactly one thing of you, and it is the opposite of "tough it out": when it is bad, say so — the earlier you say it, the more room the team has to deal with it through drugs and scheduling. Press pause yourself, and what you lose is not comfort; it is the control rate. Weeks four and five are usually the worst, and they are exactly when most people think about taking leave — in those weeks, tell the treatment-room nurse "today is awful" directly, rather than saying it through absence.

How much the added chemotherapy adds: the part with denominators

Radiotherapy plus weekly chemotherapy does bring more acute toxicity than radiotherapy alone. A systematic review of 19 randomised trials: an odds ratio of 2.15 for grade 3 to 4 white-cell toxicity and 1.92 for gastrointestinal toxicity[6] — an odds ratio is not a multiple of the probability; it compares the ratio of happened-to-did-not-happen, so it is more honest to look at absolute numbers: in the randomised trial in bulky stage IB disease, among 183 patients on radiotherapy plus weekly cisplatin, transient grade 3 to 4 haematological toxicity was 21% (2% with radiotherapy alone) and gastrointestinal toxicity 14% (against 5%)[7]. Read those with the population label attached: that is a bulky IB trial, not a universal figure for every stage.

The part without denominators I will state just as plainly. Fatigue, radiation cystitis (urinary frequency, discomfort passing urine), skin turning red and peeling — the American Cancer Society's patient page lists all of these as common[8]; but for the definitive chemoradiation population I could not find a reliable incidence I could cite, so all I can write is "common, but I cannot give you a reliable number". Common does not mean it has to be endured — needing the toilet so often you cannot sleep, or being too tired to get out of bed, are things to raise at review.

"A low-residue diet for radiotherapy"? The evidence points the other way

Most hospitals' education sheets still say low-residue, and I want to say this carefully: this is convention that has not yet caught up with the evidence — not anybody's fault. A 2017 randomised trial of 166 pelvic radiotherapy patients split them into low-fibre, habitual-diet and high-fibre groups, and the high-fibre group had better bowel symptoms than the habitual group both at the end of treatment and one year later; the authors say outright that these restrictive, non-evidence-based recommendations should be abandoned[9]. What the Cochrane review (4 trials, 413 people) supports is a different adjustment — modification of fat or lactose can reduce diarrhoea at the end of radiotherapy (relative risk 0.66, that is, the proportion with diarrhoea fell by about a third; 95% confidence interval 0.51 to 0.87, moderate quality, mostly from older-technique eras)[10]. So my advice is: do not start banning a long list of foods on your own; take the actual diarrhoea to a dietitian and adjust individually. Drug management of diarrhoea has an ESMO guideline to follow[11]; how to use antidiarrhoeals is for the team to direct — do not buy your own to sit on the symptom. Suppress the number, and you have covered up the warning light.

Which situations mean same-day contact: the whole list is here

This list applies to the entire chemoradiation course. Apart from the infusion-reaction line, whose action is "handle it on the spot", the action for every line is the same: contact the treatment team or return to the hospital the same day. Do not wait for the next visit.

  • Fever: a single oral temperature of 38.3°C, or 38.0°C sustained for an hour — the definition in the guideline of the Infectious Diseases Society of America[12]. Chemotherapy pushes the white cells down, and the cisplatin label records deaths from infection in neutropenic patients[13]. Treat the reading itself as an emergency; do not take a fever tablet and watch overnight.

  • Diarrhoea with dehydration: watery stools that will not stop, plus not being able to drink, urine output visibly falling, dizziness on standing; or diarrhoea together with fever or bloody stools. I am not giving a "times per day" threshold, because I could not find original wording I could cite — the combination of symptoms is enough on its own.

  • Trouble passing urine: being completely unable to pass urine, or urine that is visibly blood throughout.

  • Heavy vaginal bleeding: soaking through a pad in a short time, bleeding that will not stop, or bleeding together with dizziness.

  • An allergic-type reaction during or within minutes of the infusion: facial swelling, chest tightness or wheeze, a racing heart, feeling about to faint — call out on the spot; if you are still in the hospital it is handled there, and if you have already left, return at once[13]. In the days after chemotherapy, urine output clearly reduced or swelling of the legs: contact the team the same day[13] — the background to both, and the practical side of that day, are in "The weekly chemotherapy day". Tinnitus or a change in hearing is not an emergency, but it absolutely must be mentioned at the next visit — also in that article.

  • After sex: fever or shaking chills, severe perineal or anal pain, bleeding that will not stop — this cannot wait until the next day. The full context of this one is on the site in "Sex during treatment: who raises it?".

A five-week logbook you can start keeping today

Two Taiwan items. IMRT and VMAT have no dedicated items in the NHI fee schedule's item file — external-beam radiotherapy is billed by field[14]; how each hospital claims it and whether there is a self-paid difference, ask the medical affairs office and your case manager directly — I am not going to vouch for either side. When you feel there is nobody to ask, 104 hospitals across Taiwan have cancer resource centres, with the toll-free line 0809-010580[15]. Then, today, set up a small notebook or a phone memo, three lines a day: temperature, taken once; number of bowel movements, and whether you were up in the night; the single most uncomfortable thing today. Look back on it after five or six weeks and it becomes the basis on which you and the team adjust treatment — and the way this course gets finished inside seven weeks without a single step of it being you gritting through alone.

From the physician

Screenshot the emergency master table section and keep it on your phone — give your family a copy too. From today, note three lines a day: temperature, bowel movements, the most uncomfortable thing. Whether the course finishes inside seven weeks is something this logbook can help with.
