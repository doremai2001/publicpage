Lower-limb lymphoedema: prevention and treatment

Reported incidence runs from 1.3% to 49.4%, and the width of that range comes from the assessment method, not from the operation. This one covers how it is measured first, then how to prevent and treat it, and what Taiwan's clauses actually say.

The same 235 women, assessed for oedema by four different methods, produce entirely different risk factors — the odds ratio for lymphadenectomy runs from "cannot be made out at all" up to 14.42. So when you see any single figure, ask first: who measured it, how, and in which group of people.

"Doctor, if I have this operation, will my legs swell up? What are the odds?" I cannot give you a number, and the reason I cannot is worth more than the number would be: reported incidence runs from 1.3% to 49.4%, and that range is wide mainly because the assessment methods differ, not because the operations do. The general principles, the ones worked out on arms, are on the site in "A swollen arm: the real odds of lymphoedema"; legs have their own way of being measured, their own timetable, and their own clauses.

The same 235 people, four ways of measuring, four different answers

A prospective study across 14 Swedish hospitals enrolled 235 women with endometrial cancer (116 who had a lymphadenectomy, 119 who did not) and, a year after surgery, assessed oedema in the same women by four methods at once: raw volume from circumferences, volume standardised to body mass index, clinical grading, and the patient's own perception. For lymphadenectomy as a risk factor, the adjusted odds ratio was 14.42 (95% confidence interval 3.49 to 59.62) under the standardised volume, only 2.11 (1.04 to 4.29) under clinical grading, 2.51 (1.33 to 4.73) by patient perception, and under raw volume it could not be made out[1]. Adjuvant radiotherapy was more extreme: it was a risk factor only under the standardised volume method (odds ratio 15.02)[1]. The authors' sentence, as it stands: "Apparent risk factors for lymphedema differed considerably depending on the method used to determine lymphedema" — and their conclusion is that the field needs an agreed standard for determining it. While we are here: an odds ratio is not a multiple of probability, and when events are common it looks a good deal larger than the true multiple of risk.

Read that 1.3% to 49.4% range with the assessment method attached to each figure

Every figure has to be read with its method. A Swedish robotic-surgery cohort of 188 used clinical assessment: 1.3% in those who had sentinel lymph nodes removed only, 18.1% in those who had pelvic plus infrarenal para-aortic lymphadenectomy[2]. New York used a 13-item self-completed questionnaire in 599 analysable patients: 27% (49/180) in the sentinel node group, 41% (144/352) in the lymphadenectomy group, odds ratio 1.8 (1.22 to 2.69) adjusted for external beam radiotherapy and weight[3]. Mayo used the same questionnaire in 378 patients: 26.0% against 49.4%, adjusted odds ratio 2.75; restricted to contemporaneous patients treated after sentinel mapping was introduced, still 26.0% against 39.0%[4]. An Italian laparoscopic cohort of 239 gave 21.4% against 44.6%, odds ratio 3.11[5].

Denmark is different again: a national prospective cohort of 486 women with low-grade disease, presumed FIGO 2009 stage I, all having sentinel node mapping, compared the EORTC QLQ-EN24 leg oedema score (0 to 100) before surgery and at 12 months: a mean rise of only 5.0 points, below the 8-point threshold for clinical significance[6]. Radiotherapy is a separate independent factor: in the New York questionnaire, 51% of those who had external beam radiotherapy reported swelling against 35% of those who had not, odds ratio 1.95[3]. Choosing between sentinel node mapping and lymphadenectomy is a surgical decision, written up in "What the operation removes, and what happens to the lymph nodes"; and note that sentinel node mapping does not mean you will not swell — in the questionnaires above, 21% to 27% of the sentinel groups still reported swelling.

The first year is the window, and the body signals before the leg swells

A Japanese prospective study of 190 women followed five years (gynaecological cancer surgery including pelvic lymphadenectomy, oedema defined as International Society of Lymphology stage I or above) found a 5-year cumulative incidence of 39.6%, highest in the first year; 51.6% among those given taxane chemotherapy, 49.1% among those who had 60 or more nodes removed[7]. The Danish data add a practical observation: the score at three months already predicts the 12-month score[6]. So "say something early" is not a courtesy, it has a basis. The other thing worth knowing in advance is that swelling does not necessarily stay. A prospective sentinel node cohort of 150 cervical cancer patients (a different population — keep the label), followed 24 months, had a cumulative 17.3% mild and 9.2% moderate oedema, at a median of 9 months; a further 22% had transient swelling that settled by itself within 6 months[8]. So the first time you notice your leg has got bigger, that is not necessarily the next several decades — but nor is it something to wait out: measure it, write it down, say it, then see which way it goes.

Earlier signals can be measured too: indocyanine green lymphography followed 80 asymptomatic lower limbs, 26 of them subclinically abnormal; after a median 24 months, 50% of the subclinical group had developed objective oedema against 5.6% of the rest, though the authors also write that not all subclinical cases progress[9].

Prevention: almost nothing has been shown to work

Start with the intervention most often recommended. Within the same Japanese cohort, 87 women who had no adjuvant treatment were grouped by their own preference: 24 did simple lymphatic drainage daily for a full year, 63 did not, both followed five years. Neither the rate of change in leg circumference nor the extracellular water ratio differed, and the 5-year cumulative incidence was 37.5% against 23.5% — pointing, if anything, the other way. The authors' conclusion, word for word, is "SLD does not contribute to the prevention of LLE"[10]. So I am not going to tell you that massaging yourself every day will stop your legs swelling. Education has been tried too: a Turkish pilot randomised trial of 27 patients ran a nurse-led lower-limb lymphoedema prevention programme, and the intervention group had significant improvements in quality of life, general health, self-efficacy and oedema symptom scores, but no measurable change in leg circumference in the short term[11]. Those two go in separate boxes: learning what to look for, how to measure and when to speak up has its own value; that is a different thing from "your legs will not swell".

The only "prevention" that points the same way across studies is removing fewer lymph nodes, and that is a surgical strategy, not a treatment. Weight is the same shape: it is an independent associated factor in several studies, but no data show that losing weight prevents or treats lower-limb swelling once it has happened — that is an association, not intervention evidence; weight itself is in "Weight, diabetes and this cancer".

Treatment: compression does the work, and everything else carries its own level of evidence

The most complete block of evidence comes from a Cochrane review of arm swelling after breast cancer (keep the population label; extrapolating to legs is our own extrapolation): the review as a whole covers 6 trials, but the head-to-head of manual lymphatic drainage plus compression bandaging against compression bandaging alone rests on 2 of them, 83 women. Within those 2 trials, compression bandaging alone reduced excess volume by 30% to 38.6%, and adding manual lymphatic drainage took off a further 7.11% (95% confidence interval 1.75 to 12.47); whether the reduction holds depends on continuing to wear a fitted compression garment[12]. Compression does the work, manual drainage is the bonus, and holding on to the result depends on continuing to wear it.

The evidence for legs is much thinner: a systematic review found only 6 randomised trials, 289 patients, pointing towards physical therapy significantly reducing lower-limb volume or circumference and improving pain and gait[13]. For pneumatic compression I could not find citable randomised data in a gynaecological cancer lower-limb population, so I will write neither that it works nor that it does not. Surgery is one road: a pooled analysis of 23 papers, 648 limbs, follow-up beyond two years, calculated that annual episodes of cellulitis in the leg fell by 1.32 after lymphovenous anastomosis and by 1.38 after lymph node transfer, with a 21.98% reduction in circumference after transfer[14]. All of those numbers come from before-and-after comparisons with no control group, and the heterogeneity is high.

Sudden redness, heat and pain — this is the one section that cannot wait until tomorrow

Only this section of the article is an emergency: redness, heat, tightness and pain appearing suddenly on the leg with an indistinct border, possibly with fever and chills, spreading within hours — same-day medical attention, not something that waits for the next appointment. How to judge a fever and which number to ring is in "The fever that cannot wait until morning".

Two sets of figures give the scale. In a cohort of 2,920 women with breast cancer-related lymphoedema, 231 (7.9%) had an episode of cellulitis, of whom 39.0% had a recurrence, independent risk factors including having had radiotherapy (hazard ratio 2.15)[15]. For legs there are only figures from an advanced population: 124 patients had 335 episodes of erysipelas over 1,680 person-years before liposuction, 0.20 episodes per person per year, period prevalence 52%[16] — that is advanced, fat-deposited oedema, and does not stand for the ordinary post-operative patient.

The other thing that has to be ruled out is deep vein thrombosis. Lymphoedema usually comes on slowly; a leg that swells overnight and hurts is treated as thrombosis first — endometrial cancer survivors already carry a raised risk of venous and lymphatic system disease (hazard ratio 2.71 in a matched cohort of 44,386)[17]. How to tell them apart is in "A swollen calf: can it wait until tomorrow?"; a swollen leg can also be a sign of recurrence, and that thread is in "How follow-up is scheduled, and what to watch for". These possibilities are not for you to sort out alone; say it and let the team sort it out.

What makes legs different from arms is that they block walking

Arm swelling affects lifting, gripping and getting dressed; leg swelling affects standing, walking, stairs and getting up after sitting a long time, so it jams work and going out directly. In a survey of 677 long-term gynaecological cancer survivors (more than five years from diagnosis, 32.9% of them endometrial cancer), 36.9% still had symptoms, and the commonest was lymphoedema at 36.2%[18].

There is also a barrier rarely discussed and yet measurable: not being able to get the garment on. A study of 71 women over 65 with lower-limb oedema after gynaecological cancer surgery found grip strength significantly higher in those using flat-knit compression stockings than circular-knit ones (21.1 kg against 19.1 kg)[19] — failing to get it on is a grip strength problem, not a compliance problem; say so if you cannot, because the knit differs and there are aids. Going up a shoe size is common. Whether to wear compression on a long journey sitting still has no data in this population, so all I can give is the general rule: get up and walk about, put the leg up.

Is there any subsidy for compression garments? I searched both official documents

The NHI table has one lymphoedema procedure: 47091B, "lymphoedema care — manual lymphatic drainage (must reach forty minutes)", 450 points, its note reading, in translation, "1. Indication: patients with lymphoedema in terminal cancer. 2. Personnel: must have received relevant training in lymphatic care. A record of the indication, the procedure and the time taken is required after completion. 3. Paediatric supplement item."[20]. A woman with stage I disease whose operation is done and whose leg is swelling does not, on the literal wording of the indication, fall inside that scope. That is as far as the clause goes; whether it can actually be claimed is a question for the medical affairs office or your case manager.

I searched the same full table (version effective 1140501, 6,013 items) word by word: zero hits for "compression garment", "elastic garment" and "elastic stocking"; "pneumatic" returns only maximum inspiratory pressure and pneumatic tonometry, two unrelated items; "lymphoedema" returns nothing beyond 47091B; and the rehabilitation physical therapy clauses contain "massage" but not the words "lymphatic drainage"[20]. I also downloaded the whole appendix to the Regulations on Subsidies for Assistive Device Costs for Persons with Disabilities and searched it word by word: "compression garment", "elastic stocking", "lymphatic" and "oedema" likewise returned zero hits; though Article 7 of those regulations states that a municipal or county competent authority "may, taking its financial situation into consideration, add subsidised items or raise the maximum subsidy amount"[21]. So the correct way to put it is that I could not find a listed item in either of those two official documents — not that there is no coverage; whether your own municipality adds to it is a question for the social affairs bureau and the hospital social worker.

For the first year after surgery, two fixed habits are enough: every morning look at whether the two legs are symmetrical and whether your shoes have got tighter, and if something changes ring before the next appointment rather than waiting until the shoes will not go on; and agree the measuring method with your team — the method decides the conclusion, and what you measure at home with a tape may be a different thing from what the hospital measures.

From the physician

One-sided, sudden, red, hot and painful — same-day medical attention, do not wait for the next appointment. A leg that swells overnight and hurts: rule out a thrombosis first, do not assume lymphoedema.
