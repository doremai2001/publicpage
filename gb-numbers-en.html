WHAT THE EVIDENCE SAYS

That number, and how I am going to tell you about it

Median survival means half the people are before this line and half after it — how to read the curve, the separate denominator behind each of the three numbers on the tail, and why I do not hand out a deadline.

Glioblastoma Guide · What the Evidence Says · 16 references
#Evidence#Decisions#Quality of Life

The best prognostic grading tool we have explains 20% of the differences in survival between people; the remaining 80%, we do not know what it is. Every section here rests on that sentence, and the opening hands you a way to stop reading.

You have already seen that number online. I know, because almost everyone who sits down opposite me has. So I am not going to pretend it does not exist — what I am going to talk about is who it describes, and why it was not calculated for you. (Below I sometimes use the abbreviation GBM for glioblastoma; it is the same disease.)

First, a way out. A systematic review of 14 studies states that there is genuinely a group of malignant glioma patients who do not want a precise prognosis, and that we cannot identify them in advance[1]. So if you do not want to read this now, you can stop here; that is a choice that has been studied, not an evasion.

My position first: the radiotherapy part of glioblastoma treatment is the part I do myself; the other side of every comparison in this topic is my neurosurgical colleagues' operation and my neuro-oncology colleagues' drugs. Tumour-treating fields I do not use, and they are not a service of my department. So every comparison comes with its primary source, so that you can take it to the surgical and neuro-oncology clinics for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

What that line means is: half the people are before it, half are after it

The number you found most likely comes from the 2005 randomised trial that established standard treatment: median overall survival 14.6 months in the arm given radiotherapy plus temozolomide, 12.1 months with radiotherapy alone[2]. "Median" means lining that group of people up by how long they survived and taking the one in the middle. Which tells you, at the same time, that half the people are on the right-hand side of this line — that is not consolation, that is the definition.

The five-year follow-up of the same trial is more useful than the median. In the combined-treatment arm, 27.2% at two years, 16.0% at three, 9.8% at five; in the radiotherapy-alone arm, 10.9%, 4.4% and 1.9%[3]. Two things have to be seen at once. First, what standard treatment contributes has been demonstrated by a randomised trial: in the original report of that same trial, two-year survival went from 10.4% to 26.5%[2] (the 27.2% above is the same cell recalculated at the five-year analysis; the two numbers refer to the same thing), and at five years from 1.9% to 9.8%[3] — "it makes no difference anyway" does not stand up against the data. Second, this curve has a tail, and the people on the tail are real: the authors' sentence is that in the more favourable prognostic categories, a small number of patients survived beyond five years[3].

Those few numbers on the tail each have a denominator of their own

"10% are still alive at five years" and "only 4% at five years" are both true, because they are not counting the same people. Three numbers side by side, each with its denominator:

  • Five-year survival of 9.8% in a trial cohort, from the combined-treatment arm of the trial above — the 287 people enrolled between 2000 and 2002[3]. People who can get into a trial have been selected for in the first place.

  • Five-year survival of 4% in the real world, from a 2020 paper in Scientific Reports pooling 23 population cohorts out of 63 studies: five-year survival for patients enrolled after 2005 was 4% (95% confidence interval 2 to 5%, 1,291 of 28,748 — the bracket is the plausible range for the estimate, not your personal probability)[4].

  • The ten-year cell, about 0.71%, from a 2018 systematic review covering 36 studies published between 1950 and 2014 and 162 cases; that cohort had a mean age at diagnosis of 31.1 and mostly had never had IDH testing[5] — under the 2021 classification (see "Glioblastoma: what it is, and what it is not"), some of them would not be called glioblastoma today. So "some people have lived ten years" has to be said as "under the old classification, some people lived ten years".

The same paper carries one more conclusion that matters in both directions: after 2005, two-year survival went from 9% to 18% and three-year from 4% to 11%, but the five-year cell went from 3% to 4% — no improvement[4]. The earlier stretch really has got better; the last stretch has not yet. Taiwan's own survival statistics I could not find official data for — every number above belongs to someone else's population.

What has actually been found in the people who lived a long time

A German study in Brain in 2007 collected 55 patients who had survived more than three years: they were younger at diagnosis, in good performance status, and MGMT promoter methylation was present in 28 of the 36 testable cases (the paper's word is hypermethylation, which is the "methylated" on your report). The line most worth reading out is a negative one — not one of the socioeconomic, environmental or occupational factors assessed was associated with long-term survival[6]. What people ate, where they lived, what work they did: all of it was looked at.

The 2013 paper in Clinical Cancer Research is more telling still: among 69 patients who had survived more than 36 months with centrally confirmed pathology, the IDH1/2 mutation rate was 34% against 4.3% in controls — so among the long-term survivors in the older literature, one in three carried a molecular feature that would be renamed today. And among the IDH-wildtype long-term survivors, apart from MGMT methylation being more common, nothing could be found that distinguished them from anyone else[7].

In a 2024 study built on molecular confirmation, 3 of 23 people who had survived three years or more were still re-diagnosed after their genetic testing was complete; and the long-term survivors included people carrying textbook adverse prognostic markers[8]. The reverse holds too: there are people with an IDH mutation who did not live long[7].

Those grading tools were built to design trials, not to predict you

The 1993 analysis that cut 1,578 patients with malignant glioma into 12 subgroups produced median survivals from 4.7 to 58.6 months; but the use the authors wrote down for it was improving the design and stratification of clinical trials[9]. It is a tool for trials.

The simplified version rebuilt in 2011 on 1,672 glioblastoma patients keeps only four variables (age, performance status, extent of resection, neurological function), and comes with a figure that is rarely quoted — these models explain only 15% to 20% of the variation in survival[10]. Which is to say that the best grading tool we have explains 20% of the differences in survival between people, and the remaining 80% we do not know what it is. The 2024 study says the same thing in different words: generalised prognostic prediction is not accurate for the individual patient[8].

Which factors push people to the right? Younger age, better performance status, MGMT methylation, better neurological and mental function — the direction is consistent across several sources[9][10][6]. Extent of resection is on the list too, but it has to be written half a grade down: every link between it and survival comes from observational data, and the reasons are in "What taking it all out actually means".

Once you have come part of the way, the odds in front of you change

This is the thing least often said in clinic and among the most useful. A 2012 paper in Cancer used 10,022 people in the SEER database who had had radiotherapy to work out conditional survival: the probability of surviving another two years goes from 19.8% at the point of diagnosis to 65.9% at five years. Same people, same disease; only the starting point has moved along[11].

A 2026 prospective registry of 315 molecularly confirmed IDH-wildtype patients (a single cohort from 2008 to 2017, not your probability) contains a finding that everyone just out of theatre should hear: postoperative residual tumour volume mainly affects survival in the first few months after the operation; further out, what speaks is MGMT methylation and age[12]. "There is a bit left after my operation, does that mean there is nothing to be done" — that is a direct answer to it.

Whether knowing makes it harder has itself been studied

A 2015 JCO study followed 590 patients with metastatic solid tumours that had progressed after chemotherapy through to death (not brain tumours — hold on to that label): 71% wanted to be told their life expectancy, and only 17.6% recalled a doctor having told them; those who recalled it estimated their own life expectancy at a median of 12 months, those who did not at 48 months[13]. The key sentence is this one: prognostic disclosure was associated with more realistic life-expectancy estimates, and did not damage patients' emotional wellbeing or their relationship with their physician[13].

But it is not painless. In the same data, for every additional 12 months of self-estimated life expectancy, the odds ratio for having a do-not-resuscitate order was 0.439, and for preferring life-extending treatment over comfort-focused care 1.493[13]. An odds ratio is not "a multiple of the probability", but the direction is plain: saying it plainly changes your decisions. That is exactly why I say it, and I am not pretending it does not hurt.

There is one more thing that matters particularly in a Taiwanese clinic. A 2005 Australian study asked 126 people whose cancer cannot be put right which behaviours by a doctor were unhelpful for holding on to hope: the doctor appearing nervous (91%), telling the family the prognosis first (87%), and using euphemisms (82%)[14]. Families knowing before the patient is the norm in Taiwan, and I understand it comes from a wish to protect. I only want to put that 87% on the table: if the patient himself is willing to hear it, please have him in the room.

I am not giving you a number to treat as a deadline

There is a more honest way of doing this, which is to give not one number but three scenarios: best case, worst case, typical. It has been validated in metastatic breast cancer — researchers took percentiles off the curves of 36 trials and 13,083 women, and found the three scenarios could be estimated from simple multiples of the median[15]. And when patients were asked (the respondents were people with experience of cancer, not brain tumour patients): 88% wanted all three scenarios and only 5% wanted a single median[16].

But those multiples have never been validated in glioma, and I could not find a single paper. So I am not going to apply them to this disease's median and hand you three numbers of months, because I would have made them up myself.

Which is why I am not giving you a number to treat as a deadline: because that number was not calculated for you alone. It is the median of a group of people enrolled in the early 2000s whose circumstances are not yours. What you want to do with it, I will talk through with you, but I am not going to let it turn into a date in your diary. If you do want to ask this question, swap it for this one: given my age, my performance status and my report as they are now, which thing about the next three months worries you? That answer is more use than a number of months.

And one thing that has nothing to do with numbers and is useful today: which situations cannot wait for the next appointment and mean coming back to hospital the same day is in "When to come back the same day: three tiers".

From the physician

I am not giving you a number to treat as a deadline, because that number was not calculated for you alone. If reading this leaves you more anxious, put it down and come back in a few days — not wanting to know is a choice that has been studied. If you do want to ask, swap it for this: given my age, my performance status and my report, which thing about the next three months worries you? And one line for families: if the patient himself is willing to hear it, please have him in the room.
