When will insurance pay, and who decides?

Where claims disputes actually arise, and why the answer is in the wording of your own policy.

Patient Education · Money, People And Time · 14 references
#Cost & Insurance#Decisions

I am a radiation oncologist, not an agent. What I can do is write the medical facts down so they stand up — and tell you where these arguments usually start, and who to go to when one cannot be settled.

My position first. I am a radiation oncologist, not an insurance agent or a financial adviser. This piece is not financial advice. I will not, and cannot, tell you what to buy, whether to surrender a policy, or that you will certainly be paid this time. Two things I can do: set the medical facts down clearly on the diagnosis certificate and in the notes, and say what policies generally look like, where the disputes are, and who to go to when it cannot be settled.

The biggest source of disputes is not "surgery" — it is medical necessity

This runs against intuition. In the Financial Ombudsman Institution's (金融消費評議中心) full-year 2025 statistics, life insurance claims disputes totalled 8,238 cases. First was medical necessity, 2,192 cases, 26.61%, then claim-amount determination 987 (11.98%) and delayed payment 924 (11.22%); determination of what counts as surgery came fourth, 719 cases, 8.73%[1]. The first quarter of 2026 is the same shape: of 1,281 claims complaints, medical necessity 278 (21.70%) is still first, surgery 101 cases, 7.88%[2].

The "medical necessity" argument is usually whether this admission was necessary, and whether this many days were. Cancer patients hit that block most easily, and it is where I can help: before discharge, ask your attending physician to set out on the diagnosis certificate the medical reasons for the admission and what was done — dates, procedure names, why they needed an inpatient setting. What goes on the paper is fact, not argument.

Reimbursement-type and fixed-daily-benefit are not looking at the same thing

Taiwan has official model policy wording (示範條款) for hospitalisation medical expense insurance, reimbursement-type and fixed-daily-benefit, both last amended 28 June 2024[3]. It is not your policy but the regulator's reference text — for seeing what these contracts look like, then comparing your own. The reimbursement-type version splits the benefit three ways — daily room charge, inpatient medical expenses, surgical expenses — each on the same premise: costs incurred during the admission that under NHI rules the insured must bear or that fall outside NHI cover[3]. The fixed-daily-benefit version ignores spending: it pays the agreed daily amount times the days actually in hospital[4].

The hardest wall is "hospitalisation". Article 2 of the model wording defines it as someone a physician has diagnosed as needing admission for their illness or injury, who has formally completed admission procedures and is genuinely receiving treatment in hospital[3]. Because that definition turns on whether admission procedures were formally completed, radiotherapy and outpatient chemotherapy where you report to the clinic each day usually will not fall inside it. But I cannot judge whether your policy pays — the model wording is not your policy, and it offers two versions, one counting day-stay and day care in, one not. Whether yours has an outpatient benefit, and how it defines hospitalisation, is in Article 2 and the benefit clauses; or ask the insurer to answer by quoting it.

In a policy, "surgery" is a table, not a medical concept

Article 8 caps the surgical expense benefit at the contract's per-occurrence limit times the percentage in the "schedule of surgical procedures and fees". If your operation is not on that schedule, insurer and insured "shall agree a benefit percentage by reference to a procedure on the schedule of comparable extent"[3]. On an older policy that schedule may be over a decade old, with newer techniques missing — "agree" and "comparable extent" are the doorway to the dispute.

The regulator has issued principles for that judgement: on 6 May 2014 the Financial Supervisory Commission (金融監督管理委員會) approved principles for determining "comparable extent", by letter Jin-Guan-Bao-Shou No. 10302026480 (金管保壽字第 10302026480 號函)[5]. One limitation: its annex is a scanned image with no extractable text, so I cannot tell you the criteria point by point. You can require the insurer to state which item it compares yours to, and on what basis. On outpatient surgery the Commission also spoke, by letter of 25 August 2017, Jin-Guan-Bao-Shou No. 10602542670 (金管保壽第 10602542670 號函): where the disease falls within cover and the procedure is highly substitutable, it "suggests handling the matter by agreement with the policyholder or by liberal interpretation, according to the facts of the individual case"[6]. Read "suggests" and "the facts of the individual case" carefully: administrative guidance to take into a negotiation, not a conclusion.

Waiting periods and pre-existing conditions are two entirely different laws

Point 67 of the Notes for the Review of Personal Insurance Products (人身保險商品審查應注意事項, amended 30 July 2025) says the waiting period at inception for critical illness and cancer insurance may be at most ninety days, that none may be agreed on reinstatement, and that it be marked in bold in a conspicuous place in the sales documents and policy; point 78 sets a maximum of thirty days for health insurance other than cancer and critical illness cover[7]. That is a ceiling, not your number of days.

Pre-existing conditions and the duty of disclosure get run together; they are different things. Article 64 of the Insurance Act (保險法) governs truthful disclosure: where the proposer conceals, omits or misstates something sufficient to change or reduce the insurer's estimate of the risk, the contract may be rescinded; but that right lapses if not exercised within one month of the insurer learning of the ground, and cannot be exercised at all once two years have passed since the contract was concluded[8]. Article 127 governs something else: where the insured was already ill or pregnant at conclusion, the insurer bears no liability for that illness or delivery[9]. The first is rescission, the second never covered at all. Reading a refusal letter, work out which is cited.

Cancer insurance has no nationwide template to compare against

I read the Life Insurance Association's list of model policy wordings: life, investment-linked, personal accident, travel accident and group cover, two hospitalisation medical expense versions, and a long-term care wording — but there is no "model policy wording for cancer insurance" on the list[10]. So cancer insurance has no common template and products may differ widely — the strongest argument I have: nobody can judge from a distance whether your cancer policy pays, because not even a common template exists.

Two things on the product side. Point 62 of the same Notes says cancer insurance covers cancer as the insured event and "should not exclude carcinoma in situ"; point 68 acknowledges policies may limit the items or number of benefits, giving as examples "cancer excluding carcinoma in situ" and "bone marrow transplantation limited to once in a lifetime"[7]. Side by side: the regulator says it should not be excluded, and older policies excluding carcinoma in situ are a reality. Which version yours is means reading the clauses or ringing the service line.

When it cannot be settled, the deadlines matter more than the argument

Article 13, paragraph 2 of the Financial Consumer Protection Act (金融消費者保護法): a dispute must first be raised as a complaint with the financial services enterprise, which must handle it and reply within thirty days; if you do not accept the outcome, or it fails to deal with it in time, you may apply for ombudsman review within sixty days of receiving the outcome or of the deadline expiring[11]. That body is the Financial Ombudsman Institution, www.foi.org.tw, which takes online applications and publishes its decisions[12]. The order cannot be reversed, and a missed deadline is gone.

The binding force has to be described honestly, with three limits. Article 29 provides that an enterprise which signed the advance consent must accept an ombudsman decision only where each claim's amount or property value is below a set threshold[13]; the thresholds announced by the Financial Supervisory Commission on 17 September 2021 by announcement Jin-Guan-Fa No. 11001948991 (金管法字第 11001948991 號) are NT$1.2 million for property insurance benefits, personal insurance benefits (excluding multiple-payment medical insurance benefits) and investment-linked insurance, and NT$120,000 for the rest[14] — those bracketed words matter, because reimbursement-type and fixed-daily-benefit medical cover, which pays repeatedly, sits in the NT$120,000 bracket. The Institution also explains that where a decision favours the applicant only because a right of rescission was improperly exercised, it is not a claim for an amount or property value, and the enterprise is not bound by it[12].

What can be done now is getting the documents in order

Article 19 lists the claim documents: the claim form, the policy or a transcript, a medical diagnosis certificate or proof of admission, the original medical fee receipts, and proof of the beneficiary's identity[3]. Originals go wrong when there is more than one policy; ask the hospital how originals and certified copies are stamped, and confirm with each insurer. Checking a self-pay consent form against a receipt is in "How to ask about a self-pay item".

And two "two years" not to confuse. Article 64's two years limits the insurer's right to rescind[8]; Article 20 of the model wording's two years is your right to claim, extinguished if not exercised within two years from the day it could first be made[3]. When the course ends, put the claims in rather than the paperwork in a drawer.

All of that is what these contracts generally look like. The outcome turns on which clause of your policy says what. The real answer is in your own policy wording, and nobody can judge it for you from a distance — myself included. Before your next visit, dig it out and photograph the pages you cannot follow. The medical facts I can help you set down; the clauses go to the insurer, a broker or agent, or the Institution.

From the physician

No two policies read alike, and none of this predicts what yours will do. Before your next appointment, photograph the clauses you cannot follow. I can set the medical facts down clearly; the wording goes to the insurer, a broker or agent, or the Financial Ombudsman Institution.
