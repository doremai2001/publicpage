How a meningioma gets called grade 1, 2 or 3

That number on the report has only three boxes, and there is no grade 4. What decides which box it lands in is the mitotic count, brain invasion and two molecular markers — not how many centimetres across it is.

Benign Brain Tumour Guide · Meningioma · 7 references
#Staging#Molecular Testing#Evidence#Recurrence

Written for anyone holding a pathology report that nobody has gone through line by line. The grade is not a ranking of how bad things are; it is a set of criteria that can be taken apart — and the two newest of them do not look at what the tumour looks like at all.

"Doctor, what stage am I?" Meningioma has no stages, only grades. The line on your report says grade 1, grade 2 or grade 3, and what it settles is not how bad a shape you are in. It settles which box the next questions start from — whether radiotherapy gets added, how tightly the follow-up scans are spaced. The 2021 revision moved two things: it wrote two molecular markers into the criteria, and it promoted brain invasion, on its own, into grade 2.

One sentence you can put down straight away: there is no grade 4 meningioma

In the 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) classification of tumours of the central nervous system (5th edition), in the table that lists the grades available to each tumour type, the meningioma row reads "1, 2, 3"[1]. There is no grade 4 meningioma. Grade 4 tumours do exist in the head — those are the glioblastoma family, and the site has a separate topic for them; meningioma does not have that box at all. So if your report says grade 3, that is the top of the meningioma ruler, not the third rung of four.

I put that first because the commonest fear I meet in clinic is someone reading the "3" as "one step from the worst". This ruler has three rungs, and all three measure the same thing: how likely this is to come back, and how closely the next few years have to be watched. It is not a scale of how long you have. What "benign" and "malignant" each mean inside the skull is in "Three tumours, three different logics"; this article only handles how the grade gets arrived at.

The first change: what it looks like under the microscope no longer sets the grade by itself

Before 2021, a few histological subtypes were assigned a higher grade on appearance alone. Grade and subtype have now been uncoupled, and what counts is the features. The International Consortium on Meningiomas (ICOM) consensus review of 2024 states the new rule plainly: in the absence of the two molecular changes in the next section, grade is assigned on histopathological features — you count mitoses, or you count how many of the five "soft" criteria for atypia are met, and meeting three of them is enough[2]. Those five are sheeting architecture, hypercellularity, small-cell change, prominent nucleoli and spontaneous necrosis[2].

The mitotic thresholds are written hard: 4 to 19 mitoses per 10 high-power fields lands in grade 2, 20 or more lands in grade 3[2]. So that line on the pathology report giving the mitotic count per 10 high-power fields is not decoration — it is the criterion itself, and it is the first number to ask about, not how many centimetres across the tumour is. Subtype names (meningothelial, fibrous, transitional and the rest) still get printed, but they no longer decide the grade on their own. If you have read online that some particular subtype is more aggressive, what you were probably reading was written against the pre-2021 rules.

Brain invasion: enough on its own for grade 2, and nobody is sure yet what it means

What ICOM says, in substance, is this: brain invasion by itself is now sufficient to make a tumour grade 2 — and the same sentence goes on to say that, in the absence of other high-grade features, the relationship between brain invasion and prognosis is still unclear[2]. To see brain invasion at all, the specimen has to actually contain brain tissue. Meningiomas grow outside the brain, sampling practice differs between countries, and a great many specimens simply have nothing there to look at[2].

So when brain invasion appears on your report, the correct reading is "this item pushes the grade to 2, and the recommendations downstream will be different because of it" — not "it has eaten into my brain, so mine is especially vicious". The criterion changed; the confidence behind the criterion did not change with it, and I would rather say that out loud. One question worth getting out of your mouth: besides brain invasion, does my report carry any other grade 2 feature? Having only that one, and having it alongside a raised mitotic count, are two different situations.

Two molecular markers that reach grade 3 without looking at the tissue at all

A hotspot mutation in the TERT promoter, or homozygous deletion of CDKN2A/B — either one of those present makes the tumour grade 3 outright[2]. The European Association of Neuro-Oncology (EANO) 2021 guideline says the same thing: irrespective of whether the histology shows anaplastic features, either of these changes by itself sets the grade at 3[3]. ICOM adds a limitation in the same breath: these molecular changes are uncommon in meningioma to begin with, and rarer still in cases where the histology looks unremarkable[2].

For CDKN2A/B there is an individual-patient-data meta-analysis of four studies and 2,521 people: homozygous deletion had a prevalence of 4.9%, and the reconstructed median progression-free survival (the time from treatment to the tumour growing again on imaging, or to death) was 180.0 months for wild type, 26.1 months for heterozygous deletion and 11.0 months for homozygous deletion[4]. Here is the part that is easy to misread: only the homozygous box is used for grading. The hazard ratio of 5.5 for heterozygous deletion (95% confidence interval 4.0 to 7.6 — a hazard ratio divides the rate at which events occur in one group by the rate in the other, and a confidence interval is the range the estimate plausibly sits within) is not small, but it is not a grading criterion[4][2]. Heterozygous on the report does not mean grade 3.

How the three grades split, and the trap sitting in the word "malignant"

In United States registry data tabulated under the 2021 grading, meningiomas divide as 80.1% grade 1, 18.3% grade 2 and 1.5% grade 3[2]. I am quoting that set of proportions as ICOM relays it; the consensus itself does not say which report year that registry figure comes from or how many people were counted, so I can only cite it as written. The direction — that the overwhelming majority of meningiomas you will meet are grade 1 — is solid. The digits after the decimal point are not your personal probability.

Meningioma is the commonest primary brain tumour in the United States registry. For the share and the incidence this whole topic uses one report, the United States brain tumour registry (CBTRUS): the 2018 to 2022 edition gives meningioma as 42.6% of all primary brain and other central nervous system tumours and 57.4% of all non-malignant tumours; the 2017 to 2021 edition gives an incidence of 10.15 per 100,000. The denominators and the source are set out in "Three tumours, three different logics". Percentages from different report years must never be added to or subtracted from one another. Ten-year relative survival (survival compared with a matched general population) for non-malignant meningioma is 83.4% — again a registry figure relayed by ICOM without a report year attached[2]. Why Taiwan has no equivalent official figure is in the same article.

One more thing that frightens people unnecessarily: "malignant meningioma" in registry data is a coding field, and it is not the same as grade 3 — in the United States SEER series only 63.6% of that group were grade 3, with 20.4% grade 1 and 15% grade 2 mixed in, because the field runs off ICD coding rather than central pathology review[2]. So when you see that word, the first step is to establish whether it came from the pathology report or from a registry code. Both can sit in the same set of notes, and they can disagree.

Methylation classes predict better in studies, and still cannot decide a treatment

A 2017 retrospective analysis of 497 meningiomas from 10 European centres sorted them into six clinically relevant methylation classes, which predicted recurrence better than WHO grading (comparison of Brier scores, p=0.0096), and validated this in an independent cohort of 140[5]. That is a real signal.

But the 2024 ICOM consensus also says, in the same document, that a unified molecular classification has not been established to this day[2]. The obstacles it lists are concrete — sequencing or methylation arrays have to be run, and not every centre has that technology, on top of differences in reimbursement and in experience with interpretation, and no settled answer on which assay to pick[2]. Integrated grading schemes that fold WHO grade, copy-number changes and methylation family into a single score do outperform WHO grade alone in retrospective cohorts, but the consensus position is that centres swap between them according to their own resources and no agreed replacement exists yet[2]. The analysis run on specimens from a prospective trial (38 grade 2 and 3 meningiomas from EORTC 22042-26042) pointed the right way but did not reach statistical significance[6].

So if you ask me whether methylation testing can decide your radiotherapy question, today the answer is no. Its place right now is as a research tool, not a decision tool; the evidence on that question is in "After the operation, do you add radiotherapy".

The grade reads this specimen, it is not an identity card

The same meningioma can be given different grades at different times and at different operations; ICOM discusses exactly this in the context of secondary atypical meningioma — grade 1 the first time, grade 2 after recurrence — which carries a higher recurrence rate[2]. That is not somebody getting it wrong. Tumours change, and sampling has boundaries by its nature.

This has a practical consequence. If you are holding two pathology reports from different dates with different grades on them, those are not two contradictory answers. They are two time points. The grade is a starting position: it settles how the next few years get watched, not how much time you have left.

Take the report and circle four lines before anything else

Before any of the later questions can be discussed, four lines on the pathology report are worth circling: the WHO grade, the mitotic count per 10 high-power fields, whether brain invasion is mentioned, and whether TERT or CDKN2A/B were tested. If no molecular testing was done, that in itself is a question you can ask out loud: given what my report says, should anything be added?

At the Taiwan end there are two things I can establish. The National Health Insurance (NHI) fee schedule does contain next-generation sequencing items for solid tumours (30301B, 30302B, 30303B, 10,000 to 30,000 points, effective 1 September 2026), with the clause reading "indications: as set out in appendix 2.2.1" and the rule that "each person may claim only one of 30301B, 30302B or 30303B per cancer type, reimbursed once in a lifetime"[7]; the list of cancer types in that appendix is not itemised anywhere I could find in the schedule — not finding a listed item is not the same as there being no cover, and how an application would be made is a question for the medical affairs office. The second thing is this: a full-text search of the current schedule returns zero hits for CDKN2A, TERT, methylation, Ki-67 and MGMT[7]. Zero hits means those five have no item of their own in the current version. It does not mean the tests cannot be done, and it does not mean anyone has to pay for them out of pocket. How to read the extent of resection comes next, in "How much came out, and whether that still counts".

From the physician

I have met too many people who read the "2" on their report as "nearly cancer now". It is not. Grade 2 changes how closely the next few years get watched and whether a conversation with radiation oncology is needed; it does not change whether you should be able to sleep tonight.
