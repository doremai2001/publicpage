Out of pocket, but not a free-for-all

Japan turned the slice of particle beam therapy its insurance does not cover into a statutory drawer called Advanced Medical Care A: the money is entirely out of pocket, but the treatment policy, the consent form, all-case registration and society site visits are legal conditions.

Insights · Radiation Therapy · 19 references

In Japan, particle beam therapy sits in three drawers at once: insurance, Advanced Medical Care A, and Advanced Medical Care B. What decides the drawer is the cancer type, not the machine. This piece is only about the middle one. The most interesting thing about it is not how large the out-of-pocket sum is; it is how many rules are tied to you after you have paid it.

Same accelerator, and what a Japanese patient pays out of their own pocket differs a great deal — the only variable is which drawer the cancer type falls into. In the insurance drawer, particle beam therapy is item M001-4 of the 2024 fee schedule: 187,500 points for rare diseases, 110,000 points for other specified diseases, at ¥10 a point[1] (the points after the FY2026 revision are something I have not been able to check). That is the total medical cost, and it does not include the particle therapy eligibility-assessment add-on or the medical management add-on; what the patient hands over is the co-payment out of it, usually 30%, which works out at roughly ¥330,000 to ¥560,000, and that is in turn protected by the high-cost medical expense cap (the cap itself varies by income band, and I have not checked it). In the Advanced Medical Care A (senshin iryō A) drawer, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's FY2025 (Reiwa 7) annual performance report, covering 1 July 2024 to 30 June 2025, records an advanced medical care total of ¥2,055.08 million for proton therapy across 739 cases, and ¥966.40 million for carbon-ion therapy across 303 cases[2]. The total divided by the number of cases is about ¥2.78 million and ¥3.19 million per case — that is my own arithmetic; the table has only two columns, a total and a case count, and no official per-case average is published. In this segment, the patient pays one hundred per cent, and it is not under the high-cost cap.

The formal name for this drawer is advanced medical care, and its legal identity is "evaluated care" under Article 63(2)(iii) of the Health Insurance Act[3]: not "better medicine", but "medicine on which it has not yet been decided whether it should be covered, and which is being evaluated". How the money is cut is written plainly on the ministry's own site: 「『先進医療に係る費用』は、患者が全額自己負担することになります」 (my translation: the costs relating to advanced medical care are borne in full by the patient); the parts outside advanced medical care — the consultations, tests, drugs and ward stay that are common to ordinary treatment — are handled as ordinary insured practice[4]. This is what is called the combined-billing benefit for care outside insurance. The line the ministry adds needs reading carefully: what the high-cost medical expense cap protects is the insured segment, and the out-of-pocket segment is not inside it[4].

The line between A and B is usually written in Chinese-language coverage as "A is the mature one, B is still experimental". That is wrong. The ministry's classification document uses a hard test: A is 「未承認等の医薬品若しくは医療機器の使用又は医薬品若しくは医療機器の適応外使用を伴わない医療技術」 (my translation: medical technology that does not involve the use of unapproved drugs or medical devices, or the off-label use of drugs or medical devices), so no unapproved drug or device and no off-label use; B is the ones that do, plus a catch-all clause for technologies that need focused observational evaluation[5]. An accelerator is an approved device, so particle beam therapy falls into A as a block; whichever cancer type is judged to need a strictly set control group is carved out into B. A does not mean it has passed evaluation. It means it is being evaluated.

What gives this drawer its actual shape is the facility standards. The ministry's "list of requirements for institutions permitted to perform the technique" sets out items 〔9〕〔10〕〔11〕 in exactly the same wording in both the proton therapy and the carbon-ion therapy sections: 「公益社団法人日本放射線腫瘍学会が作成した同意説明文書及び統一治療方針に基づいた治療を実施していること」「公益社団法人日本放射線腫瘍学会に対して症例の登録及び実施状況を報告すること」「公益社団法人日本放射線腫瘍学会の訪問調査に応じること」 (my translation: treatment must be delivered on the basis of the consent explanation document and the unified treatment policy drawn up by the Japanese Society for Radiation Oncology; cases must be registered with the society and their implementation reported to it; the institution must accept the society's site visits) — treat according to the society's consent explanation document and unified treatment policy, register the cases and report, accept the society's site visits. The requirements set the hospital and the physician separately: on the hospital side, ten or more cases of the therapy must already have been performed and a cancer board must be in place; on the physician side, the responsible doctor must have worked exclusively in radiology for ten years and have performed ten or more cases as lead or assisting doctor, of which five or more as lead[6]. These clauses were pushed in by a restructuring of the system in 2016[7]. The society's "disease-specific unified treatment policy" is therefore not a gentlemen's agreement; it is the legal condition on whether this hospital may take this out-of-pocket money at all[8]. Even what document you explain the treatment with is written into the provision: item 〔9〕 requires treatment on the basis of the consent explanation document the society produced, not one each hospital writes for itself.

What is in this drawer now. The notification (as at August 2026) gives the proton therapy targets as six categories, 「頭頚部腫瘍（脳腫瘍を含む。）肺・縦隔腫瘍、消化管腫瘍、肝胆膵腫瘍、泌尿器腫瘍又は転移性腫瘍」 (my translation: head and neck tumours, including brain tumours; lung and mediastinal tumours; gastrointestinal tumours; hepatobiliary and pancreatic tumours; urological tumours; or metastatic tumours); carbon-ion therapy is missing head and neck and has five. Both carry the same parenthesis, 「いずれも根治的な治療法が可能なものに限る」, limited to people for whom curative treatment is still possible[6]. The society's unified treatment policy cuts the boxes finer: proton therapy is split into seven cancer categories, of which the head and neck box takes only 「頭頸部扁平上皮癌」, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma; carbon-ion therapy has only five categories and no head and neck box at all[8]. Yet on the insurance coverage list both proton and carbon-ion therapy carry 「頭頸部悪性腫瘍（口腔・咽喉頭の扁平上皮癌を除く）」, head and neck malignancy excluding squamous cell carcinoma of the oral cavity, pharynx and larynx[9] — head and neck entered insurance for both modalities at the same time. So the shorter carbon-ion advanced-care list is not a consequence of that 2018 listing; the society's own scope simply never had that box. As to why it does not, I could not find any official explanation of the difference. The 2018 listing left 「口腔・咽喉頭の扁平上皮がん」 outside[10], so head and neck squamous cancer stayed outside insurance, and intensity-modulated proton therapy for it hangs under a registered trial in Advanced Medical Care B[11].

Registration is not an administrative ritual. After 2016 the proton facilities built Proton-Net and the carbon-ion facilities built J-CROS[12]. Five years later, the report the society submitted to the Advanced Medical Care Conference in November 2021 used a systematic review plus all-case registry data on 159 cases treated between May 2016 and June 2018, and traded it for the listing of five cancer types for insurance coverage in April 2022[12]. The registry also grew into papers: in the 2023 supplement of the Journal of Radiation Research, 174 patients with oesophageal cancer had a 3-year overall survival of 60.5%[13] and 36 patients had bladder cancer[14]; the national registry of carbon-ion therapy for locally advanced non-small cell lung cancer had 55 patients, with a 3-year overall survival of 49.1%[15]. Those numbers have to be framed first: every one of them comes from a single-arm prospective registry, with no randomised control group, and 36 to 174 patients. What they can prove is that registration really does produce data, not that particle beams work better. That report screened 409 papers, and the particle therapy papers finally included in the comparison numbered two[12].

Japan's officials write about their own system honestly. When prostate cancer went into insurance in 2018, the document said 「粒子線治療の有効性・安全性が IMRT と比較して上回ることは示されていないものの、同等性については示されており」 (my translation: it has not been shown that the efficacy and safety of particle beam therapy exceed those of IMRT, although equivalence has been shown)[10] — the reason was equivalence, not being more effective. At the 149th Advanced Medical Care Conference in December 2025, the overall evaluation of particle beam therapy across all indications was "comprehensive IIa"; the material for the 150th conference in January 2026 quotes that conclusion as background and records what the preliminary evaluation had pointed out — 「全生存割合(Overall survival, OS)や有害事象は多くが既存の放射線治療と同等で、一部の癌腫で優越性を示している」 (my translation: overall survival and adverse events are for the most part equivalent to existing radiotherapy, with superiority shown in some cancer types) — and on that basis moved to an indication-by-indication review. Only one indication passed in that round: unresectable colorectal lung metastases, three or fewer, and only where the primary tumour has been resected, there is no local recurrence, and extrapulmonary metastases are controlled[16]. The same material writes the other side: 「脱毛･脳壊死の発生確率は粒子線治療で有意に増加した」 (my translation: the probability of hair loss and brain necrosis increased significantly with particle beam therapy), so skin and brain doses need watching[16]. Particle beams are not radiotherapy without side effects.

This piece is not advice to go to Japan for treatment, and the reason is in the system itself. Advanced medical care is coupled to Japan's public medical insurance: Article 86(1) of the Health Insurance Act, which pays the combined-billing benefit for care outside insurance, names 「被保険者」, the insured person, as its subject, and requires that the person 「被保険者であることの確認を受け」, be confirmed as an insured person[3]. The ministry's manual for hospitals receiving foreign patients states that foreign nationals in medium- to long-term residence are compulsory members of Japan's public medical insurance, while foreign nationals visiting short term are not insurance subjects and their care is priced at the fully private rates each institution sets for itself[17]. The line is drawn at insurance status, not at nationality. The ministry has published no dedicated statement on whether someone without Japanese insurance can use advanced medical care, and I am not going to settle that on its behalf; but you can see from the wording of the statute that what someone without Japanese insurance gets by going down this road is not a discount — it is zero discount. What has to be paid is not only that ¥2.78 million, but also the consultations, tests and ward stay that a Japanese patient pays only 30% of. Advanced Medical Care A is a transitional arrangement Japanese people built inside their own insurance system for a treatment that has not been settled. It is not a ticket on sale to the outside world.

On the Taiwan side I use only official numbers. The Department of Medical Affairs of the Ministry of Health and Welfare publishes the national status of medical particle therapy equipment, data as at 30 November 2024: 13 medical institutions in Taiwan have proton therapy equipment, 4 of them operating; 3 have carbon-ion therapy equipment, 1 operating[18]. That is the most recent official count made public, and the data date is already more than a year and a half old; the real number may have gone up, and I could not find an updated official figure. Taiwan's National Health Insurance payment items for proton therapy are coded 36025B to 36027B, and the scope of each begins with the words "patients under nineteen years of age"; adults pay the whole thing out of pocket[19]. As for the middle drawer, I could not find, in the NHI fee schedule or the related announcements, anything equivalent to Japan's unified treatment policy as a nationally uniform treatment standard, nor any statutory all-case registration requirement. That is an observation about systems, not a claim that one country is better — the price of Japan's arrangement is that it is slow, and in 2026 it let one cancer type through. But that drawer lets the words "out of pocket" carry a question afterwards. If someone suggests you pay out of pocket for something, there are two things you can ask: is there a treatment policy for this that everybody follows? And once it is done, will my data be registered, and who will analyse it? If there is no answer, what you paid for is one treatment, not one piece of evidence.

From the physician

This piece is about a system, not about treatment advice. Whether particle beam therapy suits you is for your own treating team to judge from tumour site, stage, previous treatment and overall condition; please do not change, delay or stop current treatment because of this article. Japan's mechanism, in which the common parts stay insured, is written in the statute for insured persons; the ministry has published no dedicated statement on whether someone without Japanese insurance can use advanced medical care, and I am not going to settle that here either. The one thing that is certain is that the cost structure quoted in this article is the cost structure for an insured person in Japan, not for someone arriving from outside. This article does not recommend travelling to Japan for treatment and does not help arrange it.
