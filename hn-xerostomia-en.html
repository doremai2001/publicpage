Dry mouth, and the decay that follows

Parotid-sparing radiotherapy cut dry mouth at two years from 83% to 29%. What a dose threshold can and cannot promise, what pilocarpine does, and the truth about acupuncture.

Head & Neck Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 14 references
#Xerostomia#Dental Care#Side Effects#Late Effects#NHI Coverage

The number that tells you what to expect is not in any article. It is the mean dose your own parotid and submandibular glands received. This page covers what that number can promise, and how the rules for looking after teeth change once saliva is short.

Six months after treatment ends, the commonest complaint at follow-up is not pain. It is "my mouth is so dry I get up three times a night for water" and "I can't eat a lunchbox without soup". Dry mouth is one of the longest-lasting and most intrusive things radiotherapy leaves behind, and it is not only a matter of comfort — once the saliva goes, the rules of tooth decay are rewritten. This piece covers both halves.

Saliva can be saved, and not at the cost of tumour control

The UK PARSPORT phase 3 trial settled this in 2011. Ninety-four patients with pharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma were randomised between conventional radiotherapy and parotid-sparing intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), median follow-up 44 months[1]:

  • At 12 months, grade 2 or worse xerostomia: 38% with IMRT against 74% with conventional radiotherapy (p=0.0027).

  • At 24 months the gap was wider: 29% against 83% (p<0.0001).

  • Recovery of saliva secretion and quality of life both differed significantly at both time points.

  • At 24 months there was no significant difference in locoregional control or overall survival — sparing the glands did not cost tumour control.

The trial also reported its bad news honestly: grade 2 or worse fatigue was more common with IMRT (74% against 41%, p=0.0015)[1]. Technical progress is rarely free.

Those dose numbers are planning goals, not promises

Eisbruch and colleagues measured saliva output in 88 patients and 152 parotid glands out to 12 months. Glands with a mean dose at or below 26 Gy (stimulated saliva) or 24 Gy (unstimulated) kept substantial function and went on improving; above the threshold, most produced little and did not recover. Their conclusion: "a parotid gland mean dose of ≤26 Gy should be a planning goal"[2].

QUANTEC puts it this way: severe xerostomia — long-term salivary function under 25% of baseline — is usually avoided if at least one parotid is held to a mean dose below about 20 Gy, or both below about 25 Gy; a lower mean dose gives better function; and sparing the submandibular glands also significantly reduces the risk. Then the sentence in that same passage I always read out to patients: "the currently available predictive models are imprecise"[3].

The submandibular glands have a line of their own: stimulated flow falls by roughly 1.2% per Gy up to a mean dose of 39 Gy, beyond which it flattens near zero; below 39 Gy, flow recovers at about 2.2% per month, measured out to 24 months[4].

So do not read 24 Gy off a plan report and conclude you will not get a dry mouth. That is a goal, and a probability. It is not a guarantee.

Does it come back? Yes, but usually not all the way

2.2% a month gives a concrete answer: recovery is gradual and runs over roughly two years — as long as that gland's dose stayed under the threshold[4]. Above it, "most glands produced little saliva with no recovery over time"[2]. And the PARSPORT curves between 12 and 24 months diverge rather than converge[1].

Chang Gung's handout says most patients notice a little saliva returning after about 3 months, that the clearest recovery falls in the first two years, and that what comes back tends to be thicker[5]; Taichung Tzu Chi says there is a chance of gradual recovery within 6 months to a year, but that most patients will still have less saliva than before treatment[6].

So I say it plainly in clinic: the goal is managing dry mouth down to something that does not run your day, not getting back to how it was.

Pilocarpine: for dryness you already have, not for preventing it

These are two separate bodies of data, and merging them misleads.

Treatment. The randomised trial published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 1993 enrolled 207 patients who had all received at least 40 Gy. After 12 weeks, dryness had improved in 44% on 5 mg three times daily against 25% on placebo (p=0.027); overall improvement was 54% against 25% (p=0.003). The main adverse effect was sweating, and the proportion withdrawing because of adverse effects was 6% on 5 mg and 29% on 10 mg[7]. Read both sets of numbers together: about half notice something, and once the dose goes up, more than a quarter cannot tolerate it.

Prevention. A Cochrane review of 39 studies and 3,520 participants looked at pilocarpine given during radiotherapy to prevent dry mouth, and concluded there was insufficient evidence, and of very low quality, to determine whether pilocarpine performed better or worse than a placebo — on dryness, salivary flow, survival or quality of life; alongside low-quality evidence that it roughly triples sweating. The review's own words: "there was insufficient evidence to show that any other intervention is beneficial"[8].

In Taiwan, pilocarpine (SALAGEN, 舒樂津錠劑) carries the NHI drug code BC23548100, and one hospital's drug information page gives the indication as dry mouth from salivary hypofunction after head and neck cancer radiotherapy (>26 Gy), noting that for cancer patients continued supply requires symptom improvement to be documented every 2 months[9]. That 26 Gy happens to be Eisbruch's planning goal. But be clear what that page is: one hospital's drug information page, not an NHI Administration reimbursement document, so the actual coverage conditions and the requirements for continuing the prescription have to be confirmed with your hospital pharmacy or your case manager (個管師); the NHI Administration publishes its benefit and reimbursement schedule for anyone checking[10]. Taichung Tzu Chi's page notes that around half of patients improve after some weeks, suggests a trial of at least 12 weeks, and lists sweating, nausea, urinary frequency and dizziness as side effects[6]. It is a prescription drug with cholinergic effects. It does not suit everyone, and you must not adjust the dose yourself or borrow someone else's.

Artificial saliva, chewing gum, and the one everyone asks about

Another Cochrane review, 36 randomised trials and 1,597 participants with only one at low risk of bias, is blunt: "there is no strong evidence from this review that any topical therapy is effective for relieving the symptom of dry mouth". Sugar-free chewing gum does increase saliva in people with residual function, but has not been shown better or worse than a saliva substitute[11]. Should you use artificial saliva? You can, and many find it subjectively helpful — but understand it as making the present moment more comfortable, not as restoring moisture. I could not verify its NHI coverage; many of these products are self-pay, so check with the pharmacy.

Acupuncture is the item here I most need to state carefully, because traditional Chinese medicine is so easy to walk into in Taiwan. The largest evidence is a two-centre phase 3 trial run jointly by MD Anderson and Fudan University: 399 patients randomised, 339 analysed, comparing true acupuncture, sham acupuncture and standard care, primary endpoint a dry-mouth questionnaire score at one year[12]:

  • True acupuncture 26.6 against standard care 34.8, a significant difference (of moderate size).

  • True acupuncture against sham: p=.06, not statistically significant.

  • Clinically significant xerostomia at one year: true acupuncture 34.6%, sham 47.8%, standard care 55.1% (p=.009).

  • The sham results did not behave consistently across the two countries: true acupuncture beat sham only in Shanghai, and sham beat standard care only at MD Anderson. The authors say further studies are needed to confirm clinical relevance and generalisability.

The honest reading: it is better than doing nothing, but it did not clearly beat a placebo needling procedure, so how much of the effect is non-specific cannot be said at present. Trying it is not unreasonable, but do not treat it as a guarantee, and do not let it displace mouth care and dental follow-up.

Once the saliva is gone, decay is not a question of brushing harder

Saliva is the mouth's own buffering and remineralising system, and without it the teeth lose that protection. Fluoride is the most solid thing available. A systematic review of 14 studies concludes that "fluoride prevents radiation caries", with different formulations effective to a variable extent up to 70%, depending on the intervention, adherence and length of follow-up; sodium fluoride is the most commonly used, and no significant difference was found between it and other fluoride agents[13].

Taiwanese practice here is worth copying directly. Chang Gung advises resuming brushing about 3 months after treatment, when the tissues have stabilised, with a soft brush plus interdental brushes and floss, and fluoride gel once the mucosa has recovered[5]; Taichung Tzu Chi advises a dental review every 3 to 4 months, with tooth pain or a loose tooth dealt with immediately[6]. I could not verify NHI coverage of fluoride gel either — ask your case manager (個管師), or the hospital's medical affairs or billing office, who can look up what is actually charged and what is covered.

One sentence to keep: extracting a tooth from an irradiated mandible is the classic trigger for osteoradionecrosis. The dental article on this site carries the full numbers; here, only the conclusion — after your course ends, do not have a tooth pulled at whichever clinic you happen to walk past, but go through the cancer team and a dentist who knows your radiation history. In the 2024 ISOO-MASCC-ASCO osteoradionecrosis guideline, the recommendations judged to rest on strong evidence are precisely those on prevention and surgical management[14].

When to get back to clinic quickly

  • Tooth pain, a loose tooth, or exposed bone anywhere in the mouth.

  • An ulcer that has not healed for several weeks.

  • Numbness or swelling of the jaw.

  • Sudden severe pain and swelling of one salivary gland — inflammation or a blocked duct.

  • Fever.

Also: tell the team before you start any mouthwash, gel, chewing gum or Chinese herbal preparation. Sugared sweets and acidic products used to stimulate saliva will accelerate decay in an already high-risk mouth.

Ask to see your dose report

First: what was the mean dose to each parotid and to my submandibular glands? That sets how much recovery it is reasonable to expect, and whether I meet the conditions for pilocarpine.

Second: am I a candidate for a trial of pilocarpine? If I try it, how will we judge whether it is working, and when should I stop? Twelve weeks is the length of trial commonly suggested.

Third: how often should I have a dental review, and when do I start fluoride? While you are there, ask which details of your treatment you should volunteer to any dentist you see from now on.

From the physician

The dose figures are planning goals, not guarantees, and the guideline itself says the prediction models are not precise. At your next visit, ask for the mean dose to both parotids and both submandibular glands; that number will tell you more about what to expect than any article can.
