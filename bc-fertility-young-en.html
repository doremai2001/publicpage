Young patients: fertility preservation and ovarian function

The window for fertility preservation is before the first chemotherapy; Taiwan has had a subsidy scheme since September 2025, but its eligibility, its amounts and its deadlines are all written in fine detail.

Breast Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 16 references
#Fertility#Sexual & Fertility#Endocrine Therapy#NHI Coverage

The Ministry's subsidy scheme carries a caveat of its own: there is not yet sufficient evidence on the safety of ovarian stimulation in patients with hormone-receptor-positive breast cancer, and fertility preservation should be undertaken with caution. This one sets the text of the scheme, the live birth and utilisation figures, and the design limits of POSITIVE side by side.

This article has one timeline that is not negotiable: fertility preservation has to be dealt with before the first cycle of chemotherapy. Not "once treatment has settled down", and not "once I've thought it through". Past that point, more than half of what could have been done is no longer available. So it goes first, and the methods, the live birth rates and Taiwan's subsidy come after it.

There is only one window, and it sits before the first chemotherapy

ASCO's fertility preservation guideline, updated in 2025, puts it plainly: people with cancer should have their reproductive risk assessed and be counselled at the time of diagnosis, and the options for preserving fertility should be discussed before cancer-directed treatment. The established methods for women include embryo freezing, oocyte freezing, ovarian tissue freezing, ovarian transposition and conservative gynaecological surgery; a GnRH agonist "should not be used in place of" the established methods of fertility preservation, though it can be offered as an additional measure for women with breast cancer[1].

Taiwan's own system draws that line harder still: the Ministry of Health and Welfare's Trial Scheme for Subsidising Medical Fertility Preservation requires an application and an approval before the treatment cycle can begin[2]. Between "it occurs to you to ask" and "the eggs are actually collected" sit a referral, a consultation and an approval process. After the diagnosis and before systemic treatment starts, this is the sentence to get out: "Do I need a referral to reproductive medicine?" Male breast cancer and breast cancer during pregnancy follow a different logic, and this series does not cover them.

Whether chemotherapy stops your periods is mostly decided by age

The French CANTO cohort enrolled 1,636 women under 50 with stage I to III breast cancer who received chemotherapy and no adjuvant ovarian function suppression, with a mean age at diagnosis of 42.2. The proportion with chemotherapy-related amenorrhoea was 83.0% in year 1, 72.5% in year 2, and still 66.1% in year 4. Taking the 18 to 34 age band as the reference, the adjusted odds ratios were 1.84 for ages 35 to 39 (95% confidence interval 1.32–2.56), 5.90 for ages 40 to 44 (4.23–8.24) and 21.29 for age 45 and above (14.34–31.61) — an odds ratio is not a multiple of probability, but the direction is unmistakable: age is the strongest predictor[3].

The other half of the message is that periods may come back very late: among women aged 18 to 34 who were still without periods in year 2, 11/21 (52.4%) recovered between years two and four[3]. Chinese data come from Hong Kong: among 286 premenopausal Chinese patients diagnosed under the age of 45 and given adjuvant chemotherapy, 255 (91.1%) developed chemotherapy-related amenorrhoea, of whom 66.7% later recovered; at study entry 137 (48.9%) had already entered chemotherapy-induced menopause, and in multivariable analysis only age was associated with it[4].

Taiwan does now have a subsidy, and the conditions are written in fine detail

The scheme has been in operation since 1 September 2025 and was amended once, announced on 6 November 2025. To be eligible you have to meet two sets of conditions at once: hold Republic of China nationality and be aged 18 or over and under 41; and be under the care of a hospital accredited for quality in cancer care, with a certificate of diagnosis issued by a doctor, with breast cancer of stage 0 to 3 (or specified haematological cancers), where the treatment to follow may impair reproductive function. The amounts are up to NT$70,000 per oocyte retrieval cycle for women and up to NT$8,000 per sperm preservation procedure for men, with at most 2 cycles in a lifetime per person[2]; the Ministry's press release carries the same set of conditions[5].

The dates are worth memorising: once approved, the cycle has to be completed within the approved treatment period (2 months from the date of approval), and the claim for the costs has to be submitted within 6 months of the end date of that period, with no subsidy for late claims; one extension of up to 2 months is possible for a natural disaster or for reasons not attributable to you[2]. Three more things are easy to trip over: the institution carrying out the procedure has to be both a hospital accredited for quality in cancer care and an assisted reproduction institution contracted with the Health Promotion Administration; for the same cycle you have to choose between this and the in vitro fertilisation subsidy scheme, not both; and ovarian tissue freezing is not among the subsidised items (what the scheme lists is oocyte retrieval cycles for women and sperm retrieval and preservation for men), so whether to do it and how it is charged is a question for the assisted reproduction institution[2]. This is a Health Promotion Administration subsidy scheme, not NHI cover.

The text of the scheme carries a caveat of its own, which I am copying across word for word because it suits this place better than any foreign paper does: "there is not yet sufficient evidence on the safety of ovarian stimulation in patients with hormone-receptor-positive breast cancer, and fertility preservation in patients with hormone-receptor-positive breast cancer should be undertaken with caution", and it requires doctors to assess the risk of recurrence carefully and to inform the patient of the risk beforehand[2]. The same document also requires the medical team, when explaining the treatment plan, to explain at the same time how urgent the cancer treatment to follow is and what the risk of delaying it would be[2] — this is not a choice between two things, it is a trade-off to be handled inside one conversation.

Most of what gets frozen is never used

Better said in advance than afterwards. A 2025 meta-analysis of 9 studies and more than 2,126 women who froze embryos before breast cancer treatment gives a clinical pregnancy rate of 50% (95% confidence interval 35–65) and a live birth rate of 33% (22–46); but only 18% (9–32) actually came back to have them thawed and transferred, and 39% (29–51) had at least one live birth[6].

A retrospective series from one Taiwanese centre covering 2012 to 2022 included 42 breast cancer patients who underwent fertility preservation, mean age at diagnosis 33.4, 90.4% stage 2 or below: the overall live birth rate was 63.2%, higher in the embryo freezing group; the utilisation rate was 7.69% for oocytes and 52.94% for embryos[7]. Forty-two is a very small sample, but that gap is worth remembering: freezing does not mean using, and it certainly does not mean there will be a child. BRCA carriers have a further layer to consider, in "Family history: should you test for BRCA now?".

Injections to protect the ovaries: what they proved, and what they did not

An individual patient data meta-analysis of 5 randomised trials and 873 premenopausal women with early breast cancer compared chemotherapy alone with chemotherapy plus a GnRH agonist: premature ovarian insufficiency occurred in 14.1% against 30.9%, adjusted odds ratio 0.38 (0.26–0.57, p<0.001). But at least one pregnancy after treatment occurred in 37/360 (10.3%) against 20/364 (5.5%), an incidence rate ratio of 1.83 (1.06–3.15, p=0.030) — 57 pregnancies in total. Disease-free survival and overall survival did not differ significantly between the groups[8].

The line is this: the evidence for "keeping periods going and lowering the rate of ovarian insufficiency" is clear, the evidence for "actually having a baby" is much smaller, and ASCO 2025 says outright that it should not replace freezing eggs or embryos[1]. On the Taiwan side, I searched the full text of section 9 of the NHI drug reimbursement regulations: goserelin and leuprorelin each return zero hits[9] — whether "injections during chemotherapy to protect the ovaries" are covered or self-pay is to confirm with your case manager or the pharmacy department.

No periods does not mean you cannot get pregnant

That sentence is not mine; the tamoxifen label says it itself: the drug does not necessarily cause infertility, even when periods are irregular, and it requires effective non-hormonal contraception during treatment and for 2 months after the last dose[10]. How long to use contraception after stopping differs a lot from drug to drug: trastuzumab is during treatment and for 7 months after the last dose[11]; T-DXd is 7 months for women, and 4 months for men with female partners of reproductive potential[12]; abemaciclib is at least 3 weeks for women[13]; olaparib is 6 months for women and 3 months for men, who must not donate sperm during that period[14]. Those numbers differ widely, so the action is concrete: take your prescription list to the pharmacist and write down the contraception period for every one of them. Besides the contraception periods, each of these drugs has symptoms to report the same day, and that table is in "Which side effects mean phoning the same day".

If you want to become pregnant, what POSITIVE did and did not say

POSITIVE is a single-group trial whose comparator is an external cohort, not a randomised allocation — that comes first. It enrolled 516 women aged 42 or under with stage I to III disease who had taken adjuvant endocrine therapy for 18 to 30 months and wished to become pregnant, median age 37, 93.4% stage I or II. Among the 497 with pregnancy follow-up, 368 (74.0%) had at least one pregnancy and 317 (63.8%) at least one live birth, with 365 babies born. On safety, at 1,638 patient-years of follow-up (median follow-up 41 months) 44 patients had a breast cancer event, which did not exceed the safety threshold of 46 events set in advance; the three-year cumulative incidence of breast cancer events was 8.9% in the interruption group against 9.2% in the external control cohort[15].

The limitations the trial states itself matter just as much: the conclusions apply only to "selected" women with hormone-receptor-positive early breast cancer, and longer follow-up is "critical"[15]. What it supports is that "pausing after 18 to 30 months of the tablets to try for a pregnancy, under trial conditions" has been studied — not that "endocrine therapy can be paused on your own initiative". The decision about the length of the course is in "Five years of endocrine therapy, or ten?", and whether to pause is in every case the treatment team's decision.

And pregnancy itself? A systematic review of 39 studies found that breast cancer survivors were significantly less likely to become pregnant than the general population (rate ratio 0.40, 0.32–0.49); among obstetric outcomes, low birth weight (odds ratio 1.50), preterm birth (1.45) and caesarean section were all higher, particularly in those who had had chemotherapy, but congenital anomalies were not increased. Those who did become pregnant had better disease-free and overall survival[16] — these are observational data, and a healthy-mother effect cannot be excluded: the women who go on to conceive are the ones in better shape with more stable disease, so this cannot be written up as pregnancy lowering recurrence.

Three timescales, one thing to do on each

Today: write your age, stage and receptor status on one sheet of paper, add the sentence "I still want children", and take it into your next appointment — if that sentence is not said out loud, usually nobody asks it for you. This week: ask "Do I need a referral to reproductive medicine?" and "Is my hospital accredited for quality in cancer care, and does it have a contracted assisted reproduction institution?" — both conditions have to be met for the subsidy. Next month: get your proof of identity, the certificate of cancer diagnosis and the informed consent form together, and in the same conversation ask exactly how long preservation would delay treatment and what the risk of that is.

If you have already started chemotherapy, this article is not here to make you regret anything. Whether your periods will come back, whether to keep using contraception, and how a future pregnancy would be assessed are all still questions you can ask now, and should.

From the physician

"My periods have stopped" does not mean you cannot get pregnant — the tamoxifen label says so itself. If you still want children, that sentence has to be said before the first systemic treatment; if you have already started chemotherapy, the contraception periods and the assessment of ovarian function can still be asked about now.
