AFTER THE DIAGNOSIS

Glioblastoma: what it is, and what it is not

The 2021 classification changed the rules: IDH-wildtype is not the same thing as glioblastoma, and there are three doors to get through before that. This article lines the wording on your report up against the rules in force.

Glioblastoma Guide · After the Diagnosis · 6 references
#Molecular Testing#Staging#Decisions

The line I hear most often in clinic is "doctor, what stage is this?" — brain tumours have no stages, only a grade and a name. And since 2021, where that name came from decides every step after it. This article carries no survival numbers.

That line on your report — glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, CNS WHO grade 4 — is one I end up explaining almost every week in clinic. Nobody arrived at it by glancing at a scan, and it is not the vague thing people take it for, "the most malignant kind of brain tumour". Since 2021 that name has a very specific set of conditions it has to meet; and a few of the older names you may have heard stopped being used in that year. This article does one thing: it lines the wording on the report in your hand up against the rules currently in force.

Three doors to get through first, and only then those five options

The 2021 World Health Organization classification of tumours of the central nervous system — usually called WHO CNS5 — compressed adult-type diffuse gliomas down to three types: astrocytoma, IDH-mutant; oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-codeleted; and glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype. The 2016 edition had 15 entries in that same slot[1].

So the first step is not testing IDH. The first step is establishing that this is an adult-type, diffuse, astrocytic glioma — those three words are the entry condition, and only once you are through that door does the rest of it apply[1]. Then you ask about IDH: if IDH is not mutated (IDH-wildtype), you look at five conditions — microvascular proliferation, necrosis, TERT promoter mutation, EGFR gene amplification, and gain of the whole of chromosome 7 combined with loss of the whole of chromosome 10 (written +7/−10). Any one of the five holds, and the name is glioblastoma[1].

Turned around, this matters a great deal: the single fact that IDH is not mutated is not enough to make it grade 4. The cIMPACT-NOW consensus document that set out those three molecular conditions says it plainly — plenty of much less aggressive tumours are IDH-wildtype too[2]. The same document adds two technical limits: what EGFR requires is focal high-level amplification, and one extra copy of chromosome 7 (trisomy 7) does not count; and immunohistochemical staining cannot be used to call EGFR amplification[2]. Younger patients have one more door to clear before any of this: paediatric-type diffuse glioma has to be thought of first, because CNS5 no longer applies the word "glioblastoma" to paediatric-type tumours[1].

It can look unalarming down the microscope and still end up with a grade 4 name

CNS5 allows exactly that: an IDH-wildtype diffuse astrocytic glioma, once a TERT promoter mutation, EGFR amplification or +7/−10 is found, can be labelled glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, CNS WHO grade 4 even when the histology looks like a lower grade[1]. In clinic that sentence is nearly always read as "my disease got worse during these two weeks". It did not. cIMPACT-NOW's reasoning ran in the opposite direction: they first observed that this group's clinical course was already the same as glioblastoma's, and only then changed the name to the one that matched the course[2]. What was upgraded is the name, not the illness.

One test can unsettle the name even further. Of the DNA methylation-based classifier published in Nature in 2018, the authors write that the method changed the diagnosis in up to 12% of prospective cases[3]. Why a report changes its name, what each layer is testing, and why nobody will hand you a halfway answer that might have to be taken back, is in "Two weeks for the report: what is it waiting for".

Where did the name "anaplastic astrocytoma" go

CNS5 took the modifier anaplastic out of the naming system altogether; anaplastic astrocytoma and anaplastic oligodendroglioma no longer appear[1]. The old anaplastic astrocytoma splits into two roads by IDH status: with an IDH mutation it becomes Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, CNS WHO grade 3; without one, and carrying any single one of those three molecular markers, the name is simply Glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, grade 4[1][2].

The same revision deleted the name "Glioblastoma, IDH-mutant" as well. Everything on the IDH-mutant side is now called Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, covering grade 2 through grade 4; and grading is now done inside a type, no longer by comparing sizes across different diseases[1]. If your report says IDH-mutant, you are on a different road, and that article is "The report says IDH-mutant: you are on a different road".

Two smaller changes are visible on the report itself. Grades moved from Roman to Arabic numerals (grade 4, not IV), and the reason CNS5 gives is entirely practical: II and III, III and IV are easy to misread, and a typing error nobody catches has clinical consequences[1]. Then two suffixes: NOS means the molecular testing that should have been done was not done or failed, NEC means it was done but the results will not assemble into a standard WHO diagnosis[1]. NOS does not mean "we cannot find out"; it means "the testing is not finished".

The diseases it gets mistaken for most often

Brain metastases are cancer cells from another organ that have travelled into the brain. The American cancer registry collects only tumours arising in the brain and spinal cord; something that has spread to the brain or cord from another primary site does not count as a brain tumour on that registry at all[4]. What gets treated is the original cancer, not this disease; there is a separate article on the site, Brain metastases are not the end.

Meningioma grows on the membrane that wraps the brain, and CNS5 places it in an entirely different type, with grades running from 1 to 3 — the text states outright that there is no grade 4 meningioma[1]; it is the commonest non-malignant brain tumour, 42.6% of all brain tumours[5]. That is another disease, and the author is giving it a topic of its own. Pituitary tumours were renamed pituitary adenoma / PitNET in CNS5[1], and in the American data 99% of them are non-malignant; acoustic neuroma belongs to the schwannomas, incidence 2.02 per 100,000[4]. Neither of those is what this topic is about.

Who it happens to, and how often

From the American brain tumour registry (CBTRUS), 2018 to 2022: glioblastoma is 13.7% of all brain tumours and 52.2% of malignant brain tumours, and it is commoner in men than in women[5]. The previous edition (2017 to 2021) breaks the fields down further: incidence 3.27 per 100,000 (men 4.07, women 2.58), median age at diagnosis 66, the age band with the highest incidence 70 to 74, and 61.0% of all gliomas; the same report also states that the incidence of glioblastoma has not changed significantly over time[4]. That last sentence establishes one thing only: it is not becoming more common, and not becoming less. As for "why me" — the phone, the diet, where you live — that is a different question, and the numbers in this section cannot answer it.

In Taiwan: the long-term trend data file from the Taiwan Cancer Registry shows 742 new cases of brain cancer in 2023 (410 men, 332 women), with an age-standardised incidence of 2.41 per 100,000[6]. How many of those 742 were glioblastoma I could not find an official breakdown I could cite, so I am not going to multiply the American 13.7% by a Taiwanese headcount and hand you the product.

Who wrote these rules, and will they change again

One last thing you are entitled to know: this classification did not come out of randomised trials, it came out of expert consensus. cIMPACT-NOW left a passage in its own document saying so — the literature underneath is mostly retrospective cohorts, of patients treated at different institutions, in different eras, under different histological classifications[2]. That does not make it unreliable. It means it will be revised again.

Reality is still catching up too. Among cases registered as glioblastoma in the American registry from 2018 to 2021, 1,194 (2.4%) were still coded under "Glioblastoma, IDH-mutant", a code CNS5 has abolished, and in a further 14.6% the IDH status was not recorded at all[4]. Even a national return is still bedding these rules in. So if what you are holding is a report from a few years ago with a different name on it, that does not mean your disease changed; it means the naming rules did.

If you take only one question to your doctor, I would make it this one: on my report, which condition is this name resting on — necrosis, microvascular proliferation, or which molecular marker? This article carries no survival numbers, deliberately; how those numbers should be read, and why I will not treat one as your deadline, is in "That number, and how I am going to tell you about it". And if you are waiting for surgery or have just had it, what should bring you back to hospital the same day is in "When to come back the same day: three tiers".

From the physician

That name is not a thermometer for how bad things are; it is the output of a set of rules only settled in 2021. So do one thing when the report arrives: ring every line you cannot read, and ask your doctor to point at the condition your name is resting on. An older report carrying a different name is common, and it means the rules changed, not that your disease did.
