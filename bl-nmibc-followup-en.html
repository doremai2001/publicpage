How long the cystoscopies go on

Low risk can consider stopping after five years; for high and very high risk the guideline's word is life-long, and the upper tract CT urography goes on long-term as well.

Bladder Cancer Guide · Non-muscle-invasive · 6 references
#Follow-up#Active Surveillance#Imaging#Recurrence

This article puts together two things that are rarely said in the same breath: every one of those surveillance schedules is only a Weak recommendation, and the guideline itself uses the word burden for cystoscopy — people are trading discomfort for certainty.

"How long does this go on for?" People with non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer ask me that more often than they ask about survival. The honest answer is stratified: one layer has a five-year answer, and for another layer the official answer is life-long. Telling either layer's answer to the other as though it were a general rule misleads somebody.

Where the material in this article comes from, first: every schedule below is from the 2026 edition of the European Association of Urology guideline on non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer. How the risk stratification itself is worked out is in "Low, intermediate, high: what decides whether you get instillations", and I am not going over it again here.

The first cystoscopy is always at three months

This part is not stratified; it is the same for everyone. The guideline says the first cystoscopy three months after the resection is an important prognostic indicator for recurrence and progression, at evidence level 1a, and that it should therefore always be performed three months after the resection in all patients with TaT1 tumours and carcinoma in situ[1].

It is more than one examination. What is seen at three months goes back and changes the rhythm of the whole schedule that follows, and it can change what treatment you are on. So this is not the one to push back.

Three schedules, and every one of them is a Weak recommendation

Table 8.2 and the recommendations that go with it set out three schedules[1]:

  • Low risk: once at 3 months, once at 12 months, then once a year, for five years. No urine cytology, and no routine imaging.

  • Intermediate risk (excluding the high-grade group within it): once at 3 months, then every six months for two years, then once a year, for ten years.

  • High and very high risk: every three months for two years, then every six months out to five years, then once a year, for life; urine cytology at the same frequency as the cystoscopy; and upper tract CT urography once a year to five years, then every two years to ten years, again for life.

Two things belong with that table. First, those recommendations are all rated Weak; the guideline writes that recommendations for follow-up are mainly based on retrospective data and that there is a lack of randomised trials investigating whether the frequency of surveillance cystoscopy can safely be reduced[1]. These are not rules; they are the best arrangement anyone can put on the table at the moment, and the people putting it there say themselves that the evidence behind it is not strong.

Second, one sentence that often gets dropped: the high-grade patients within the intermediate-risk group should be followed up as high risk[1]. And one thing that is easy to get the wrong way round: the schedule follows the risk group you were in at diagnosis, not "this one looked clean". As for the American side, the American Urological Association and the Society of Urologic Oncology updated their guideline in 2026[2], but the full follow-up schedule sits behind a members' login and I cannot quote it word for word, so I am not using it here as a comparison. When you see two sources giving different schedules, the first question is which guideline's stratification each is using.

Only the low-risk layer has a stopping point

The guideline's reasoning for low risk is set out plainly: a small, low-grade Ta recurrence does not present an immediate danger to the patient, early detection is not essential for successful therapy, and the chance of a recurrence after five recurrence-free years is low. So in low-risk tumours, after five years of follow-up, discontinuation of cystoscopy, or its replacement with less invasive methods, should be considered[1].

Note the wording: should be considered — not "stop". The same passage mentions that in low-risk cohorts a negative haematuria dipstick had a reasonable negative predictive value (0.89 and 0.94), which might serve as a low-cost way of monitoring[1]. Intermediate risk takes a lower-intensity schedule based on one small randomised trial, run out to ten years[1].

So "five years and you can stop" hangs on the low-risk layer alone. Passing it on to somebody else as a general rule is the commonest way this subject gets mistold.

For the high-risk layer, the guideline's words are life long

The reason is in the guideline as well: "Recurrences after ten years tumour-free are not unusual." The surveillance strategy for this layer therefore includes frequent cystoscopy and voided urine cytology at the start, and life-long follow-up[1]. Coming back after ten clear years is not a curiosity in this layer.

The upper tract axis is long-term too. The recommendation says to take yearly and long-term upper tract imaging (CT urography) for high-risk and very-high-risk tumours. The figures with their denominators: the ten-year incidence of an upper tract tumour varies widely between populations, from 1.9% in T1 tumours to 25% in multiple, recurrent high-risk non-muscle-invasive disease; in one population-based study, high-risk patients (Ta G3 and carcinoma in situ) had a ten-year rate of 3.6%[1].

Anyone with carcinoma in situ should look hard at this passage: in a cohort of 138 patients with carcinoma in situ, 63% had involvement outside the bladder — upper tract or prostatic urethra — either at diagnosis or during follow-up, and those patients had worse survival[1]. The bladder being clean does not mean the whole urinary tract is clean.

When the cytology is positive and the cystoscopy is clean

This is the result most likely to frighten somebody and least likely to have been explained in advance. The guideline's recommendation for it is rated Strong — the schedules above are only Weak: in patients with positive cytology and no visible tumour in the bladder, mapping biopsies or photodynamic-guided biopsies are recommended, together with investigation of locations outside the bladder, meaning CT urography and prostatic urethral biopsy[1]. Which is to say this result has a defined next step; it does not hang in the air until the next appointment.

The words used in a cytology report are worth knowing in advance too. Under the Paris system, the categories "atypical", "suspicious for high grade" and "high grade" correspond to a high-grade malignancy rate of 40%, 81% and 91% respectively[1]. And urine cytology is not recommended in low risk or in intermediate risk other than high grade in the first place, so not everybody will get this report at all[1].

Urine markers can save you a few cystoscopies, but they cannot replace them

Where they stand at the moment is as an adjunct, or in low and intermediate risk as a marker-guided way of postponing or even replacing some cystoscopies. Two randomised trials showed non-inferiority: one in low and intermediate risk, testing every six months after a negative three-month cystoscopy, which detected more than eight in ten recurrences overall; and one in high-grade tumours, alternating a urine marker with cystoscopy through the first and second years of follow-up, which significantly reduced the number of cystoscopies without affecting the detection of any recurrence within two years. But the guideline says straight afterwards that which marker, or which combination of markers, should be adopted remains unclear[1]. Non-inferiority means "not much worse", not "just as good".

The price goes in the same paragraph. Take one of those markers: its sensitivity for high-grade disease is 91%, but its positive predictive value is only 16%, and the guideline writes in brackets that this is a false positive result in nearly one out of three patients — the denominator of the guideline's sentence is everyone tested, while the denominator of the 16% positive predictive value is everyone who tests positive, which works out at roughly five false positives in every six positive reports; the two cannot be read side by side as the same thing[1]. The guideline also gives its own trap about negative predictive value: a marker that misses every single recurrence would still compute a negative predictive value of 0.85 in a cohort with a 15% recurrence rate, despite not being clinically useful at all[1]. As for blue-light flexible cystoscopy, a meta-analysis of two randomised trials worked out that using it continuously during follow-up finds one extra tumour per 100 examinations and one extra carcinoma in situ per 50, which is why the guideline recommends using it according to risk group[1].

The discomfort of cystoscopy is not a small thing

I do not want to pass over this lightly, because it is the real long-term burden of this disease. The word the guideline itself uses is burden: "Patients prefer certainty over the burden of cystoscopic surveillance"[1] — people would rather put up with it in exchange for certainty. That sentence concedes two things at once: that it is a burden, and that most people choose to go through with it.

It has been measured. A cross-sectional study of 151 men undergoing cystoscopy in the operating theatre (2021 to 2022) scored them at two points during the procedure on a scale of 0 to 10: anxiety 1.79 and 1.94, embarrassment 2.77 and 2.32, pain 1.44 and 3.46; regression analysis showed that pre-procedure anxiety explained 38.6% of the variance in pain during the procedure[3]. That is a male population in an operating theatre and cannot be read across to the experience of flexible cystoscopy in an outpatient clinic; but the finding that anxiety beforehand magnifies pain during has been measured. Time is a cost as well: a single-centre analysis of 133 patients found that a single bladder instillation takes 179 to 192 minutes of process alone — that is the figure for an instillation, not the time taken by one cystoscopy[4]. Coming in for an appointment charges you something; it just is not money.

The 2026 edition added a chapter 10 that lists "reduced frequency of surveillance cystoscopy and CT urography" as one of the things that can be scaled back, with the condition written in the same paragraph: it rests on a shared decision-making process in fully informed and consenting patients[1]. That chapter is your grounds for saying out loud that you are finding this hard; it is not grounds for quietly coming in less often.

The next twelve months, laid out now

Two things about the Taiwan side first. Cystoscopy is item 28019C in Taiwan's National Health Insurance fee schedule, 1,800 points[5]; as for whether there is a cap on the number in a surveillance setting, I did not see one in the remarks column of the fee schedule, and if there is one it would sit in the notes for medical expense review, which I did not manage to check this time — that goes to the hospital's medical affairs office or your case manager, not to your own inference. Separately, cancer falls within the scope of Taiwan's catastrophic illness provisions; the insurance administration's page on catastrophic illness categories states that there are 30 categories in all, and the actual category and the validity period of the certificate follow the administration's announcements and what your treating doctor issues[6].

Then the thing you can do today: put that three-month appointment, and every appointment for the following year, into the calendar on your phone with an alert, and write in the notes field which layer you are in and how many years it runs for. At the next clinic visit, ask three questions — which layer am I in, does my schedule have a stopping point, and do I need the upper tract CT every year. For the layer whose follow-up is life-long, what holds it together is not willpower; it is turning it into something you do not have to decide about again each time.

From the physician

Do not push back the three-month appointment; it sets the rhythm of the whole schedule after it. Before your next visit, put every appointment for the coming twelve months into the calendar, with a note saying which layer you are in and how many years it runs for. If you are finding it hard, say so out loud — the chapter added to the 2026 guideline exists so that this can be discussed.
