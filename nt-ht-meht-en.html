HYPERTHERMIA: DEVICES AND THERMOMETRY

It does not claim to work by temperature

The class of hyperthermia with the most installations in Taiwan makes a different claim about its mechanism from the machines used in those randomised trials. That decides whether the evidence can be borrowed.

Next-Generation Therapy Guide · Hyperthermia: devices and thermometry · 9 references
#Hyperthermia#Evidence#Decisions

This article deals with a naming problem whose consequences are real: two treatments share the words “hyperthermia”, and the randomised trial results of one of them cannot automatically be credited to the other. It matters particularly in Taiwan, because the two are common in exactly opposite proportions.

Warning 1 (what this displaces): Most of the treatments in this topic are paid out of pocket, and most sit outside standard treatment. Before you decide, you need to know that what they displace is usually not time but three things of real value: standard treatment you have not yet used up, money you will need for later lines, and your eligibility for clinical trials (many trials exclude people who have recently received other experimental treatments). And there is one more, harder to see — physical reserve. The strength burnt through in a course that does not work may no longer be there when a genuinely useful option appears. So the question is not whether this is worth trying — it is what you give up by doing it.

This kind of course is also charged per session and also means a trip to hospital every week. Before you decide, what you need in your hands is its own evidence, not another machine's.

Warning 2 (finding the right doctor): Do not make this kind of decision alone, and do not make it on the word of the person offering the treatment. The doctor you are looking for is one willing to tell you "this is not right for you" — if they never once say who this is NOT for, that is not a consultation, it is a sale.

My position first: proton therapy and hyperthermia are both technologies within my own field, and the hospital I work at has the equipment for them. So I draw every line in this topic tighter, not looser.

The randomised trials in the previous articles were all run with machines whose target is to heat the tumour to forty-one to forty-three degrees Celsius. There is another class of equipment, called hyperthermia in Chinese as well, whose account of why it works is a different one. This article is about modulated electro-hyperthermia, usually abbreviated mEHT.

What the method says about itself

The research group behind this method describes it in the literature as follows: a 13.56 MHz radiofrequency current with amplitude modulation, using the differences in conductivity and metabolism between tumour tissue and normal tissue so that the electric field concentrates selectively at the level of the malignant cell membrane[1]. The same authors, in another review, describe it as selectively acting on the malignant cell membrane and thereby heating malignant tissue and sensitising it to treatment[2].

But there is something here worth knowing: its own literature is not consistent about the role heat plays in it. Some papers state that the temperature is controlled at forty-two degrees while there are additionally ionic-current and membrane-level effects[1]; and in recent years there are papers written by the method's principal researcher arguing explicitly that what distinguishes it from conventional hyperthermia is its use of non-thermal bioelectromagnetic mechanisms[3]. Another review of its clinical validation says that it uses both the thermal and the bioelectromagnetic heterogeneity of tissue[4].

I am not going to adjudicate between them here. What I want to point out is the inconsistency itself, because it has several very practical consequences.

Consequence one: thermal dose is not a metric that applies to it

The CEM43 thermal dose of the sixth article rests on a premise — that the effect is decided by how many degrees were reached and for how long[5]. If a method holds that a substantial part of its action does not come from bulk temperature, then demanding that it reach some CEM43 value does not hold logically; it never said it would. And the other way round: the check “was the thermal dose enough this time” cannot be applied to it either. The thermometry questions the ninth article teaches you to ask have to be put differently to this class of equipment.

Consequence two: results from trials in which temperature was the intervention cannot be extrapolated to it

This is the most important of them. The trials cited in the third article — the five randomised breast trials, the Dutch pelvic trial, the head and neck meta-analysis — compared “the same radiotherapy, plus heating the tumour to a target temperature” against “radiotherapy alone”[6][7]. What those trials answer is whether reaching that temperature is worth it, not whether adding this particular machine is worth it. So if someone brings those numbers out in support of a device that claims a non-thermal mechanism, two different interventions are being treated as one.

And the sentence holds in reverse. You cannot say a device does not work on the grounds that it did not heat the tumour to forty-three degrees. It never said it would reach that temperature. Refuting it by someone else's standard and crediting it with someone else's results are the same mistake.

What its own evidence looks like

The heaviest single item at present is a phase III randomised controlled trial conducted at a public hospital in South Africa: locally advanced cervical cancer, comparing chemoradiotherapy with and without mEHT, given twice a week before external beam radiotherapy, with local disease control at six months after treatment as the primary endpoint, stratified by HIV status[8]. Two-year and three-year results were reported subsequently: disease recurrence significantly reduced (hazard ratio 0.67 at two years, 0.70 at three years), no significant difference between the two arms in late toxicity, and better quality of life[9]. This is a real randomised trial, in 210 patients, and I am not going to understate it.

But two things need to be seen clearly. First, this is a single centre and a single trial, and it was run in a resource-limited health-care setting — the authors write that plainly themselves. The result of a single trial is not settled until other centres reproduce it. Second, apart from this trial, the method's clinical data are mostly single-arm studies, and the method's own clinical validation paper acknowledges it: because most of the patients are already unable to receive standard treatment and a control group is hard to assemble, many studies can only recruit single-arm, and single-arm means a lower level of evidence[4].

Why this matters particularly in Taiwan

The machine written into those randomised trials and the one on offer at the hospital nearest you are not necessarily the same class. I could not find an official compilation of the distribution of machine types in Taiwan, so I am not going to tell you which class is more common. What I want to say is something else: which class of machine an efficacy figure belongs to, and which class of machine you are about to be treated with, have to be confirmed separately. The nineteenth article deals with the situation in Taiwan, but I am still checking official sources for that article's data, and I will not cite any machine counts that have not been verified.

So how am I supposed to judge it

My advice is to assess it as a treatment in its own right, rather than as “a kind of hyperthermia”. Assessing it in its own right means asking about its own clinical trials, its own indications, its own course and its own costs. The questions the earlier articles teach — which study, was it randomised, how many people, what was the endpoint — work perfectly well here[6]; they simply have to be answered with its data.

As for the thermal dose and thermometry set of questions: if the mechanism the provider claims does not have bulk temperature at its centre, then ask directly — by what means do you confirm that this session achieved what it was meant to? Every serious treatment ought to have some form of check on itself; only the form differs. Being unable to answer that is where the problem would lie, not the number of degrees.

The line on the consent form

One last thing, and it is a legal one. What a patient signs is consent to the treatment they were told about. When a method's account of its own mechanism of action changes, the content of the explanation should change with it — because the explanation is what the patient weighed things up on. This is not a problem unique to this class of equipment, but in a field where the mechanistic claim is itself still moving, it deserves to be taken more seriously.

So, in one sentence

They are two different treatments sharing one Chinese name. Next time someone tells you that “hyperthermia has randomised trial evidence in such-and-such a cancer”, the thing to ask is not whether they are lying, but: the kind you are going to give me — is it the kind those trials used? If it is not, then ask them to produce that kind's own evidence, which is an entirely reasonable thing to ask for.

From the physician

One question is enough: “the kind you are going to give me — is it the kind those trials used?” There is nothing offensive in it, and what it asks about is not efficacy but comparability, which is what you actually need in order to judge for yourself.
