WHO IS TREATED, AND GETTING READY

Which cancers pelvic radiotherapy is used for

Bladder, cervical, endometrial, rectal and prostate cancer: where radiotherapy stands in each of the five, and whether what it buys is local control or survival — one evidence anchor per cell.

Pelvic Radiotherapy Guide · Who Is Treated, and Getting Ready · 12 references
#Decisions#Staging#NHI Coverage#Evidence

The rectal cancer cell carries an extra institutional shape in Taiwan: the NHI has a code for preoperative radiotherapy paid as a bundle of five fractions, reimbursed once in a lifetime per person. This article puts the evidence anchors for all five cancers on the same page as the rules that can actually be looked up at the Taiwan end.

One linac, one morning: five different kinds of patient lie down on it in turn — bladder, cervical, endometrial, rectal and prostate cancer. The diagnoses on their letters have nothing in common, but the block of anatomy being treated is the same one, and the organs caught in the field are the same set: bladder, rectum, small bowel, bone marrow. This article is not about how each of those cancers should be treated; that belongs to each disease's own topic. It answers one question only: where radiotherapy stands in your cancer, and what it buys you.

My position first: pelvic radiotherapy is what I do every day, so every good word about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it. Implants such as the rectal spacer are not something my department provides; I write about them as an option you can go and ask about, and for cost and for who performs them, please ask that department and the medical affairs office.

Bladder cancer: a route that keeps the bladder, on evidence that stops at a matched analysis

Muscle-invasive bladder cancer has two main routes: take the whole bladder out, or scrape the tumour away endoscopically first and then give radiotherapy together with chemotherapy — generally called trimodality bladder-preserving therapy. Adding the chemotherapy has randomised evidence behind it: the UK multicentre BC2001 trial randomised 360 patients to radiotherapy alone or radiotherapy with fluorouracil and mitomycin C, and 2-year locoregional disease-free survival was 67% against 54%, hazard ratio 0.68 (95% confidence interval 0.48 to 0.96, p=0.03) — a hazard ratio below 1 means fewer events, and a confidence interval is the range in which the true value plausibly sits. Five-year overall survival of 48% against 35% did not reach statistical significance (p=0.16), and grade 3 to 4 adverse events during treatment were 36.0% against 27.5%[1].

"Is keeping the bladder worse?" has no randomised answer. Three North American centres took patients treated between 2005 and 2017 whose circumstances were comparable and matched them by propensity score — that is, matched on measured characteristics so that the two groups look alike: a single lesion under 7 cm, no extensive carcinoma in situ, no bilateral hydronephrosis, in other words the group in whom either treatment was possible. Five-year metastasis-free survival was 74% after radical cystectomy against 75% after trimodality therapy, subdistribution hazard ratio 0.89 (0.67 to 1.20, p=0.40)[2]. That paper says at the outset that the earlier randomised trials comparing the two closed because they could not accrue, and that "no further trials are foreseen" — no new trials are expected. The evidence stops at the matched-analysis cell and will not climb any higher; and the cystectomy patients who were included made up only 29% of the cystectomies performed over the same period, so anyone who did not meet the criteria is not in this dataset. The European Association of Urology's 2025 guideline on muscle-invasive and metastatic bladder cancer points the same way[3]. A separate bladder cancer topic is being planned; I am not writing the disease introduction here.

Cervical cancer: what adding chemotherapy buys is 6 percentage points

Cervical cancer is one of the cancers where the pelvic radiotherapy evidence has accumulated most completely. Pooling the individual patient data of 18 randomised trials, concurrent chemoradiotherapy against radiotherapy alone at the same dose added 6 percentage points to 5-year survival, hazard ratio 0.81 (p less than 0.001); the survival benefit was visible with platinum-containing and non-platinum regimens alike, and the price was a rise in acute haematological and gastrointestinal toxicity[4]. That meta-analysis is honest about its own limits: the late-toxicity data were too sparse to analyse — it can answer "what did adding chemotherapy buy", and it cannot answer "what will my bowel be like in ten years". How surgery and radiotherapy are chosen between, why chemotherapy still has to run every week during radiotherapy, and those brachytherapy days, are in "Surgery or radiotherapy: not operating is not giving up", "Why chemotherapy runs alongside radiotherapy" and "Brachytherapy: the scariest name, the decisive days"; the rhythm of the course itself is in "Getting through five or six weeks of pelvic radiotherapy".

Endometrial cancer: whether to treat, and where, comes out of the risk stratum

Postoperative radiotherapy in endometrial cancer is a very clean demonstration of a set of randomised trials, three PORTEC trials each answering one question. PORTEC-1 enrolled 715 stage 1 patients and randomised them to postoperative pelvic radiotherapy of 46 Gy or to no further treatment: 5-year locoregional recurrence was 4% against 14%, 5-year overall survival 81% against 85% (p=0.31), and what the paper concluded was that radiotherapy reduces locoregional recurrence and makes no difference to overall survival; treatment-related complications were 25% against 6%[5]. PORTEC-2 randomised 427 high-intermediate risk patients to pelvic external-beam radiotherapy of 46 Gy or to vaginal brachytherapy: 5-year vaginal recurrence was 1.8% against 1.6%, non-inferiority was established — that is, brachytherapy was not worse by more than the margin set in advance — and acute grade 1 to 2 gastrointestinal toxicity was 12.6% with brachytherapy against 53.8% with external beam[6] — the same control of vaginal recurrence, with a fourfold difference in side effects. PORTEC-3 enrolled 660 high-risk patients and added chemotherapy to the pelvic radiotherapy; at a median follow-up of 10.1 years, 10-year overall survival was 74.4% against 67.3%, adjusted hazard ratio 0.73 (0.54 to 0.97, p=0.032)[7]. Put those three numbers side by side and they make one sentence: low-risk patients do not live longer for being irradiated, high-intermediate risk patients need only the small volume, and high-risk patients need chemotherapy added before any survival difference appears. A separate endometrial cancer topic is being planned.

Rectal cancer: what irradiating before surgery reduces is local recurrence

Preoperative radiotherapy for rectal cancer comes in two lengths, each with its own randomised evidence. Short course, 5 fractions of 5 Gy: the Dutch TME trial, 1,861 patients, 12 years of follow-up, cumulative 10-year local recurrence 5% against 11% (p less than 0.0001), with no difference in overall survival between the arms[8]. Long course with chemotherapy: the German CAO/ARO/AIO-94 trial, 823 patients, comparing preoperative 50.4 Gy with 5-FU against the same regimen given postoperatively — 5-year local recurrence 6% against 13% (p=0.006), 5-year overall survival 76% against 74% with no difference, while acute grade 3 to 4 toxicity was 27% against 40% and long-term toxicity 14% against 24%, both lower in the preoperative arm[9]. How the two lengths are chosen between is in "Five days or six weeks of your life", and looking after the diarrhoea is in "Ten trips to the toilet and nothing feels finished".

This cell has a definite institutional shape in Taiwan. The full table of the NHI fee schedule contains 36024B, "preoperative hypofractionated radiotherapy for rectal cancer", 204,966 points, effective 1 July 2023; the payment rules state that the course is 5 fractions, that it is paid as a bundle, that the frequency of performance is limited to once in a lifetime per person, and that it may not be performed in someone who needs a further salvage pelvic course for recurrence after surgery[10]. The points are the tabulated value in the fee schedule, not the amount you pay; whether the code applies to you, and which kind of course you should have, is a conversation for the clinic.

Prostate cancer: two routes, and the cancer mortality is equally low on both

Prostate cancer is the only one of these five cells that already has a complete topic of its own. The ProtecT trial randomised 1,643 men with localised prostate cancer found on PSA testing to active monitoring, radical prostatectomy or radiotherapy, and followed them for 15 years with 98% completeness: deaths from prostate cancer were 3.1%, 2.2% and 2.9% respectively (overall comparison p=0.53), and metastases 9.4%, 4.7% and 5.0%[11]. The paper's own concluding sentence says that whichever group a man was assigned to, prostate-cancer mortality was low, so the choice of treatment is a weighing of the benefits against the costs. As for whether to irradiate immediately after surgery or wait until the PSA rises, a prospectively planned meta-analysis of three randomised trials and 2,153 men could not separate them: immediate adjuvant radiotherapy was not shown to be superior to early salvage radiotherapy[12]. The detail is in "Surgery or radiotherapy: different bills" and "How many visits does radiotherapy actually take", and the long-term bowel and urinary numbers are in "That 13.7% is being read wrong".

Three sentences you can take from this map straight into clinic

Read the five cells and the same thing keeps happening: what radiotherapy buys in the pelvis is mostly local control and organ preservation, and only a few cells — cervical cancer with chemotherapy, high-risk endometrial cancer with chemotherapy — have a number in the survival column at all. That is not bad news; it is what a decision is made of. Knowing what you are buying is how you know how much side effect is worth putting up with, and when to say stop. Three sentences you can say out loud at your next appointment. One: is the goal of my course local control, organ preservation, or survival? Two: which course length applies to me, and what is the reason? Three: how is my cell claimed under the NHI, and are there self-paid items I have to decide about myself? The first two I answer almost every week in clinic; for the third, ask the medical affairs office as well. As for how the technical terms stratify, and what each of those "better" claims actually buys, this topic's "Not three machines — three layers" and "What you buy is side effects, not survival" take that up next.

From the physician

This is a map, not a decision document. For the detail of your own cell I have pointed you to the topic that already covers it; the bladder cancer and endometrial cancer topics are still being planned.
