Breathless after treatment — which pneumonitis is it

Radiation, checkpoint and infective pneumonitis look identical on day one and are treated in opposite directions. This is how to recognise it.

Lung Cancer Guide · Side Effects, Emergencies and Follow-up · 15 references
#Lung#Pneumonitis#Urgent#Side Effects#Immunotherapy

This is not an article for working out which one you have. It is for the person who notices a new cough at nine at night and is deciding whether to wait until morning. The answer is three thresholds, and not one of them asks you to make a diagnosis.

The conclusion first: after chest radiotherapy, or after starting immunotherapy or a targeted drug, a new cough and new breathlessness are not a cold. Radiation pneumonitis, checkpoint-inhibitor pneumonitis and infection look identical on day one and are treated in opposite directions — the first two need the immune response damped down, the third antibiotics. So this article will not teach you to tell them apart. You have one job: recognise that this is new, and come in the same day.

"You can't tell from the symptoms" is not me stalling. It is what the paper says

Radiation pneumonitis presents as fatigue, dry cough, low-grade fever, breathlessness and chest pain. A 2021 review of radiation-induced lung injury is blunt: these symptoms are non-specific, and other causes — infection, cardiac, pulmonary embolism, drug toxicity — must be excluded[1]. It defines it as a diagnosis of exclusion: radiotherapy history, imaging change, typical symptoms, counted only once infection, pulmonary embolism, heart failure, drug-induced pneumonitis and tumour progression are ruled out[1]. That is a review, not a guideline, but the order of work is real.

Read backwards, that is what you cannot do at home. Another sentence worth memorising: radiation-induced lung injury runs a slow course, and any acute decompensation should prompt a workup for infection, heart failure and thromboembolic disease[1]. "It was like this last time and it settled by itself" is exactly why you come in this time: sudden worsening is by definition not plain radiation pneumonitis. Pulmonary embolism is on that list, and "A swollen calf: can it wait until tomorrow?" uses the same threshold — sudden breathlessness, chest pain, a fast heartbeat, same day, without you sorting out which.

Timing gives a clue, but the clue is for me to read

Knowing when lets you recognise this without diagnosing it. Radiation pneumonitis appears most often 3 to 12 weeks after treatment, and any time in the first year[1]. An exploratory PACIFIC analysis measured it finely: grade 2 or worse pneumonitis at a median of 70 days from the end of radiotherapy (range 21 to 433 days), about 80% within three months[2]. Checkpoint-inhibitor pneumonitis is wider: in a series of 43 cases, day 9 to month 19.2[3]; a median of 82 days in a cohort of 205[4]. In Japanese post-marketing surveillance of 3,578 patients on osimertinib (second line or later, T790M-positive), interstitial lung disease came at a median of 63 days, range 5 to 410[5].

Those windows overlap. In week eight to ten after chemoradiotherapy, one person can sit at the peak of radiation pneumonitis, two months into durvalumab, and still immunosuppressed from chemotherapy[1][2]. That overlap is the evidence for "you cannot tell them apart".

One thing to settle now, so you do not misread a later scan: radiation fibrosis is not pneumonitis; it does not begin until 6 to 8 months after irradiation, and it goes on progressing for years[1]. That is a different thing, and "One kind of change that is not a recurrence" is about nothing else.

Not rare — and not a reason to refuse treatment

In an international individual-patient-data meta-analysis of 836 people given concurrent chemoradiotherapy, radiation pneumonitis needing treatment (grade 2 or above) occurred in 29.8% and fatal pneumonitis in 1.9%[6]. With consolidation durvalumab, PACIFIC's grade 2 or above figure was 19.8% against 14.1% on placebo — that 14.1% is the radiotherapy itself; grade 3 or above was 4.6% and 4.7%, and those authors concluded the risk should not deter eligible patients from durvalumab[2]. In real-world PACIFIC-R, 19.4% of 268 patients were affected, with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) an independent risk factor (odds ratio 2.60, 1.21–5.65; an odds ratio divides one group's odds of the event by the other's, not the probability becoming 2.6 times larger)[7].

Checkpoint pneumonitis ran at 19% in that 205-patient lung cancer cohort, above the 3% to 5% reported from clinical trials[4]. Targeted drugs do it too: the same Japanese surveillance of 3,578 patients reported 6.8% with 29 deaths — that population was second line or later and T790M-positive, and first-line users are not in that denominator[5]. Anyone with pre-existing interstitial change sits higher again: in 242 such patients given stereotactic body radiotherapy, fatal pneumonitis occurred in 6.9%[8] — a selected, high-risk group. Their thresholds must come from their own team, not the general figures. Before you go home, ask "how low does my oxygen saturation have to go before I phone?", and write that number beside your case manager's (個管師) number.

What counts as same-day: three thresholds you can act on

First, what counts as breathlessness having changed. Not an absolute value — you against yourself last week: the corridor you walked in one go, the stairs you climbed without stopping, and now you stop halfway; or you slept flat and now need another pillow. That is the change, and it is a same-day appointment. Do not wait until you need oxygen — on the grading scales that is already grade 3[1]. Markedly worse within hours, or unable to finish a sentence, is the emergency department — and call 119, do not drive yourself and do not let family drive you; if your oxygen drops another step on the way, nobody in that car can do anything. Fluid, coughing blood and superior vena cava compression have their own thresholds in "Breathlessness, blood, fluid: which one is today", on the same lines.

Second, the saturation reading. It is a plus sign, not a pass. The British Thoracic Society emergency oxygen guideline calls oxygen saturation the fifth vital sign, with a target of 94–98% for most acutely ill patients and 88–92% or a patient-specific range for those at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure, such as people with COPD[9]. A new drop below 94% at rest, or out of the range your team set for you, is same-day. One sentence from the same guideline goes with the number: oxygen is a treatment for hypoxaemia, not breathlessness; in patients who are not hypoxaemic, oxygen has not been proven to have any consistent effect on the sensation of breathlessness[9]. A good number on the machine does not cancel a symptom: a normal reading does not rule pneumonitis out. That is a UK guideline; Taiwan has no equivalent.

Third, fever. The threshold is the one from "The fever that cannot wait until morning": one ear or axillary reading of 38.0°C or above counts, with no waiting for it to be "sustained for an hour" and no waiting for 38.3°C. On chemotherapy, that is the emergency department. And do not take an antipyretic, an over-the-counter remedy or a cough suppressant before deciding whether to come in — they flatten the temperature and the cough for hours without touching what is underneath, and I meet a version of you that looks fine, with half the information gone. Low-grade fever is itself a symptom of radiation pneumonitis[1], so fever is a plus sign, never a minus sign: no fever is not reassurance, and a fever does not prove infection.

Cough counts on its own. A new dry cough, or your existing cough behaving differently — more often, coughing until you are breathless, waking you at night — brings you back.

Why not one of the steroid tablets left in the cupboard

The reason is not abstract. In that series, 5 of 43 patients with pneumonitis died, and 3 died of infection caused by immunosuppression, not of the pneumonitis[3]. The review states that oral steroids continued beyond a month require Pneumocystis prophylaxis alongside them[1]. Push the immune system down and infection comes up behind it, with somebody watching throughout. If what you had was an infection, a steroid taken first makes it worse; and at the fibrotic stage steroids do not help[1].

None of this makes steroids frightening: used at the right moment they pull someone back. ASCO's 2021 guideline on immune-related adverse events works at the level of principle: when immunotherapy is held or stopped, and when a physician starts steroids. It says of itself that, the evidence being limited, its recommendations rest on expert consensus[10]. Whether to use them, how much, for how long, how to come off them — this article writes not one word of it: that is decided by a physician after the scan, and the answer differs for each of the three pneumonitides. Antibiotics are the same — in practice a doctor often treats for infection first while arranging imaging, which is not a delay but the necessary order[1].

What I do when you come in the same day

A CT scan. A chest X-ray is not specific for radiation pneumonitis and can look like heart failure[1]. What separates them is shape: in 82 patients with post-treatment pneumonitis, checkpoint pneumonitis was more often bilateral (65% against 28%) and less often sharply bordered (17% against 59%), radiation pneumonitis more often unilateral with an edge following the treatment field[11]. That was a single-centre retrospective study, presented by its authors as an exploratory triage tool for clinicians — this is what I am looking at on the scan, not a table for you to check your report against. Bronchoscopy is done to exclude infection, bleeding or tumour spread, not to prove radiation pneumonitis[1].

None of this is "extra tests". In the National Health Insurance (健保) payment schedule (支付標準), CT without contrast is 33070B at 3,800 points, with contrast 33071B at 4,560 points (effective 2020-09-01), bronchoscopy 28006C at 1,680 points (effective 2023-10-01)[12]; the full schedule is the 114.03.13 version, verified 2026-08-30, and please confirm the current version with your case manager or the hospital billing office (醫務課). Why stage III meets this timetable: section 9.69 of the NHI drug reimbursement rules (藥品給付規定) limits durvalumab to unresectable stage III disease with PD-L1 ≥ 1%, with further conditions including wild-type driver genes and no progression after chemoradiotherapy, for up to 12 months (clause effective 1 August of ROC year 114; amendment dates on the combined text run to 115/8/1, verified the same day)[13]. Those conditions exclude some people; not everyone with stage III disease has it. Consolidation survival figures are in "Stage III: the year after chemoradiotherapy".

There is also radiation recall pneumonitis: long after radiotherapy ends, a new drug or a vaccination can reinflame the old treatment field; reported triggers include chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy and COVID-19 vaccines[14], and diagnosis rests on the radiotherapy plan, the timing and the CT[15]. Not a reason to skip vaccination — "A fever the night after a vaccine" finished that argument. It is a reason not to file new breathlessness after a vaccination under "just the vaccine".

Three sentences you can prepare today

First, two dates: the last day of radiotherapy, and the day immunotherapy or the targeted drug started. They decide how I read your symptoms, and at night you will not recall them. Second, ask at clinic: "after this course, what change brings me back the same day, and which emergency department at two in the morning?" Put it in your phone beside your case manager's number — that is what you reach for at night, not this article.

Third, the sentence to say at the desk, out loud: "I am having, or have just finished, radiotherapy / immunotherapy / targeted therapy for lung cancer, and I have a new cough and new breathlessness." Then hand over the dates. Those two decide which category you go in and what gets ordered first. Saying it or not is the whole difference.

From the physician

Your own thresholds may differ from the ones here — anyone with COPD or pre-existing interstitial change should have numbers set by their own team. Before your next appointment ends, ask what change brings you back the same day and which emergency department to use at night, and write it beside your case manager's number.
