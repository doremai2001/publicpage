Eighty years old: treat, or not?

ASCO 2023 sets the geriatric assessment threshold at 65. Three randomised trials: fewer severe side effects and admissions, no difference in survival.

Situation Guides · The First Month · 13 references
#Older patients#Decisions#Family#Getting Ready

What geriatric assessment buys is not what most people expect: fewer severe side effects, fewer falls and admissions, better quality of life, not a longer life. The cost of doing less, the evidence on dose reduction, the limits of those short questionnaires and one thing a family can do today are all here.

"He's eighty. Should we still treat him?" That question usually comes from an adult child with the patient out of the room, and it has two sides: age itself is not a contraindication; functional status and comorbidity are. Both readings of it cause harm.

One background figure first: in 2022 there were 130,293 new cancers in Taiwan, and the median age at onset across all cancers was 64[1] (I did not obtain the analysis for 2023 onwards this time). More than half of patients, in other words, were already past 64 when they were diagnosed. Treatment decisions in old age are not a special case. They are the ordinary case.

Doing less has a price

A systematic review and meta-analysis took in 30 studies of relatively high quality, covering 230,200 patients with breast cancer, 77,573 with lung cancer and 12,407 with colorectal cancer, comparing the chance of surgery in those over 75 against those under 75: in all three cancers the older group was less likely to have it, and the gap persisted after adjusting for stage, comorbidity, performance status, functional status and even the patient's own choice. The authors take this to be one possible reason why older patients survive less well, while noting that how much of it genuinely amounts to under-treatment cannot yet be quantified[2]. This is a British team's synthesis of the international literature and does not cover Taiwan; whether older patients in Taiwan are under-treated, I could not find a statistic to cite, so I will not draw a conclusion in either direction.

Running the young person's protocol has a price too

The 2023 ASCO guideline update puts it directly: geriatric assessment should be used for all cancer patients aged 65 and over, to find the vulnerabilities or functional impairments routine oncology assessment does not capture; and for patients on systemic therapy in whom the assessment finds deficits, the care plan should incorporate assessment-guided management. The seven high-priority domains are physical function, cognitive function, emotional health, comorbidity, polypharmacy, nutrition and social support[3].

There are randomised trials behind that recommendation, and the results have to be read together with what did not change.

  • GAP70+: a cluster-randomised trial across 40 community oncology practices in the United States, 718 patients aged 70 and over with impairments, about to start a new regimen for advanced cancer. Grade 3 to 5 toxic effects within three months, 51% of the intervention group against 71% of usual care (relative risk 0.74); falls within three months, 12% against 21%[4].

  • GAIN: a randomised trial at one cancer centre, 605 analysable. Grade 3 or higher chemotherapy toxicity 50.5% against 60.6%, an absolute reduction of 10.1%; completion of advance directives was also higher. But there was no significant difference in emergency department visits, unplanned admissions, length of stay, dose modification or early discontinuation, or overall survival[5].

  • INTEGERATE: a pragmatic randomised trial at three Australian hospitals, 154 patients. The intervention group had better quality of life at 24 weeks and significantly fewer unplanned admissions (incidence rate ratio 0.60 — the number of events per person per unit of time in the intervention group was about 60% of the control group's); there was no statistically significant difference in overall survival between the two groups[6].

What the three trials share is that last clause: no difference in survival. What geriatric assessment buys is fewer severe side effects, fewer falls and admissions, better quality of life — not a longer life. Putting it as "having the assessment makes people live longer" overstates it; reading it the other way round as "then there is no point assessing" is wrong too, because these trials are about whether treatment can be delivered safely to the end, not about whether to treat.

Those short questionnaires cannot decide whether to treat

The G8 and questionnaires like it, commonly used in clinic, do pick up the people at high risk: a Danish university hospital prospectively screened 1,398 patients aged 70 and over with solid tumours, 65% of whom fell on the frail side, and that side was associated with a higher risk of death within a year (the absolute proportion dying within a year, 40% against 10%) — an association measured within the same group of people, not something the screening itself caused[7].

But they cannot be used to rule out. A comparative analysis of 349 patients with a median age of 80.3 measured four commonly used screening tools at once, and concluded: apart from the modified G8 used to rule out the particular situation of "four or more domains abnormal", no tool reached a negative predictive value of 0.80 on any outcome, and none was good enough to identify the patients whose treatment plan would not change after assessment, or whose geriatric assessment would not be abnormal[8]. These questionnaires do not discriminate finely enough; their use is deciding whether to do a full assessment, not whether to treat. So I am not printing the items and the scoring here — a score worked out at home cannot decide anything.

Reducing the dose is not giving up

That sentence needs somewhere concrete to land, or it is just consolation. The British GO2 trial randomised patients with advanced gastro-oesophageal cancer judged unsuitable for full-dose combination chemotherapy on grounds of age or frailty into three dose levels: full dose, 80% dose and 60% dose, with 514 patients in this comparison, median age 76, 58% severely frail. Both the 80% and the 60% dose were non-inferior to full dose for progression-free survival; the 60% group had less toxicity, and overall treatment utility (combining efficacy, toxicity, quality of life and the patient's own acceptance of it) was actually better than in the other two groups, and no subgroup benefited from a higher dose — not even the younger and less frail[9].

That is a result in one cancer, one drug combination, and a group of patients judged unsuitable for full dose. It is not a general rule that chemotherapy should be reduced for older patients; whether it applies to your situation is a question for the treating physician.

The conditions for "subtracting" are narrower than you think

Old age has its own evidence for active treatment. A randomised trial in newly diagnosed glioblastoma at 65 and over, 562 patients, gave a median overall survival of 9.3 months for short-course radiotherapy plus concurrent and adjuvant chemotherapy against 7.6 months for short-course radiotherapy alone, with similar quality of life in the two groups[10]. That is a trial in that one cancer, and how many treatments a course runs to, and how much dose each carries, are decided by the treating team according to cancer type, stage and the aim of treatment. They are not parameters a patient chooses.

The one most easily over-read is PRIME II: women aged 65 and over, hormone-receptor positive, node negative, tumour 3 cm or less, clear margins after breast-conserving surgery and receiving adjuvant endocrine therapy, 1,326 of them randomised to radiotherapy or no radiotherapy, median follow-up 9.1 years. Ten-year local recurrence was 9.5% in the no-radiotherapy group against 0.9% in the radiotherapy group — markedly higher; but ten-year overall survival was almost identical, 80.8% against 80.7%[11]. Those two halves have to be read together. It is a trade-off inside a very narrowly defined group, not a general rule that older people do not need radiotherapy.

The medicine list is the thing a family member can actually move

In a secondary analysis of those 718 GAP70+ patients, the median number of medicines was 5 (half the patients were on more than 5), 28% were taking 8 or more at once, 67% had at least one potentially inappropriate medication and 25% had at least one major drug interaction. Those on 8 or more had more moderate or worse side effects within three months; those with a major drug interaction were likelier to stop treatment early[12].

There is only one action available: put everything he is taking — prescription medicines, over-the-counter medicines, supplements, Chinese herbal preparations — on one list, take it to clinic and hand it to the doctor or the pharmacist. Whether to change anything and what to drop first is their decision. Do not stop anything yourselves. While you are there, ask whether the hospital has geriatric medicine or a pharmacist who can go through the medicines with you. Taiwan has no cancer-specific geriatric oncology assessment item under the NHI (I could not find one anywhere in the full fee schedule), and two other groups of NHI items relating to frail older people and to comprehensive assessment are not cancer-specific and must not be conflated with it[13]. Whether your hospital has a corresponding clinic or pathway is for the pharmacist or the geriatric medicine clinic. The institutional material above was verified on 30 August 2026, and the NHI fee schedule cited is the 114.03.13 edition of the full table.

An emergency in an old person does not look like an emergency

Infection, dehydration and electrolyte disturbance in older patients often present not as fever or pain but as suddenly becoming very tired, not eating, confused speech, being unable to walk, falling. When these changes appear, do not wait for the next clinic appointment — contact the treating team or go to hospital. Falls are worth noting separately: something the family can see, and something the family can act on.

Three questions worth asking at the next visit: at my age and in my physical condition, do I need a full geriatric assessment? Is my current regimen full dose or already adjusted, and on what grounds? If my strength drops away part-way through treatment, what is our fallback? From the physician

Every trial here comes tied to its own population: GO2 is one cancer and one drug combination, PRIME II's entry was as narrow as a tumour of 3 cm or less. Neither is a general rule. What a family can do is list the prescription medicines, over-the-counter medicines, supplements and Chinese herbal preparations, take it to clinic, and leave what to cut to the doctor or the pharmacist.
