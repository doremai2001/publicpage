AFTER THE DIAGNOSIS

The first few days after they find it

Everything in these few days has an order to it: what the imaging is answering, when a biopsy alone is the right move, why steroids are started early, and why you must not adjust them yourself.

Glioblastoma Guide · After the Diagnosis · 6 references
#Getting Ready#Treatment Pathway#Tumour Board#Steroids

Families often know the diagnosis before the patient does, and then ask me the same thing in the corridor: what are we actually supposed to be doing now? Here is what happens in the first few days, in order, plus two things you can get moving today.

People who arrive here through the emergency department or a health check were usually still at work the week before. A doctor says a string of words — contrast, oedema, biopsy, multidisciplinary meeting — and then says "let's admit you first". Every single thing that happens over these few days has an order to it, and that order is not arbitrary. This article is about what gets done in the first few days, which question each step is answering, and which of them you can start on yourself right now.

Contrast-enhanced MRI lets us guess very accurately, and guessing is still not enough

The 2021 European Association of Neuro-Oncology (EANO) guideline on adult diffuse gliomas puts it plainly: the diagnostic gold standard for detecting a brain tumour is brain MRI, and it should include T2, T2-FLAIR, and 3D T1 sequences before and after contrast[1]. Perfusion MRI and amino-acid PET have one narrow but very concrete use at this stage: marking the metabolic hotspots so the team can decide where to put the needle — particularly useful when a biopsy rather than an open resection is on the table[1]. The same guideline puts their other use much later in the story: helping to tell pseudoprogression from true progression down the line, which is covered in "How the follow-up scans are scheduled, and why bigger is not always worse".

Could a blood test replace all of this? The guideline's answer: a great deal of work has already detected cell-free tumour DNA in the plasma and cerebrospinal fluid of glioma patients, but the benefit of liquid biopsy for screening, early detection or preoperative assessment remains to be proven[1]. So imaging can let us guess accurately, and it can help decide where the needle goes, but it cannot stand in for that piece of tissue. EANO's principle is that treatment decisions in glioma rest on a tissue diagnosis, including the molecular markers relevant to the diagnosis[1]. The 2021 WHO classification went further and wrote molecular testing into the diagnostic criteria themselves[2] — getting tissue is not only about confirming that this is malignant, it is about knowing which disease it actually is. How the name is settled is in "Glioblastoma: what it is, and what it is not".

Why they are still scanning your chest and sending off all that blood

Plenty of people get stuck here: the thing is in the brain, so why test the lungs, the liver, and half a rack of blood tubes. EANO gives two purposes for the physical work-up: one is to find a systemic cancer, so that a primary brain tumour can be separated from brain metastases, and the other is to find contraindications to surgery[1]. Brain metastasis is an entirely different disease, and what gets treated is the original cancer — the American cancer registry does not even count it as a brain tumour[3]; there is a separate article on the site, Brain metastases are not the end.

For deciding whether to operate, and how big an operation, the guideline lists these: the Karnofsky performance scale (a score from 0 to 100 describing how much of daily life you manage yourself), neurological function, age, and the individual risks and benefits[1]. Age is one item on that list, not the whole of it. Two other lines are worth settling here: glioma has no meaningful role for screening or prevention, so your family do not need to go and book brain scans; but where there is a relevant germline variant or a suspected hereditary cancer syndrome, the guideline recommends genetic counselling[1].

Biopsy first, or straight to the operation?

The default is to do both jobs at once: EANO writes that upfront surgery usually carries both a diagnostic and a therapeutic purpose[1]. The condition for switching to biopsy alone is spelled out just as clearly: when microsurgical resection cannot be performed safely — because of where the tumour is, or because the patient's clinical condition at that moment does not allow it — a stereotactic biopsy should be done[1]. The technique itself has a rule too: serial sampling along the needle trajectory, to avoid sampling bias[1].

"What if the needle misses" is the commonest anxiety at this step. For the markers that matter most, the guideline's answer is reassuring: IDH mutation, 1p/19q codeletion and MGMT promoter methylation are relatively uniformly distributed within the tumour, so the risk of sampling error for these markers is low[1]. For the risk of the biopsy itself the guideline's words are "low risk of complications and high diagnostic accuracy" — low is not the same as none[1]. How much an operation can remove, and what "taking it all out" actually means, is in "What taking it all out actually means".

Going the other way, the bar for deciding on treatment without tissue is very high. EANO's recommendation reads: clinical decision-making without a tissue diagnosis can only be a very exceptional situation; and even where no tumour-directed treatment is planned, a definite tissue diagnosis is still helpful for discussions with the patient and family[1]. On the question of transferring hospitals, the guideline offers one citable principle: surgery should be carried out in a specialised, high-volume centre[1]. Which hospitals qualify I am not going to name in an article; that is for your doctor to answer specifically, given where your tumour is.

A roomful of people will have looked at your scans

EANO's recommendation for the preoperative process is: follow a written institutional standard operating procedure, and hold a multidisciplinary discussion — ideally handled by a standing tumour board whose members include neuroradiology, neuropathology, neurosurgery, radiation oncology, and a neuro-oncologist from neurology or medical oncology[1]. In the language patients actually use: your scans and your report will have been looked at by a roomful of people, not decided by one. The word the guideline uses is "ideally", so this is a standard, not a guarantee that every hospital has it; you can ask directly — has my case been through a multidisciplinary discussion?

Steroids push down the swelling, not the tumour

This is the most misread thing in the first few days. EANO's text is a single sentence: before surgery, steroids may be given to reduce symptomatic tumour-associated oedema, unless primary central nervous system lymphoma or an inflammatory lesion is suspected; alternatives such as osmotic agents are rarely needed[1].

Two points follow. First, what is being treated is the swelling: two days in, the headache goes, walking steadies, and the family say "he's completely back" — that is oedema subsiding, not a tumour shrinking. I have watched a family try to push the operation back because of that misunderstanding. Second, when lymphoma or an inflammatory lesion is suspected, hold off, because steroids can make lymphoma temporarily "disappear" on imaging and on the slide, and then it cannot be diagnosed. The guideline's wording is "may be given", not "should be given" — someone with no symptomatic oedema does not have to have them. Dose, how it comes down, what happens if it comes down too fast, and the long-term side effects are all in "Steroids: the drug that saves you, and has to come down"; until then, do not adjust the dose yourself.

Nobody starts an anti-seizure drug in someone who has never had a seizure

The guideline's two sentences here run together: patients who have already had a seizure should receive anti-seizure medication before surgery; and in glioma patients with no history of seizures, primary prophylaxis does not reduce the risk of a first seizure[1]. So the current guideline does not support routinely starting the drug in someone who has never had one. That is a different statement from "anti-seizure drugs don't work" — people who have had a seizure need them, and which one matters a great deal.

The Society for Neuro-Oncology (SNO) 2024 consensus review on brain tumour-related epilepsy goes into more detail: the choice of first-line drug is critical for seizure control and tolerability, and these drugs carry the potential for drug interactions, so their individual mechanisms of action need to be understood[4]. Which drug, which side effects, and the business of driving, are in "Seizures, anti-seizure drugs, and driving". One more thing while we are here: after surgery there is usually an immediate repeat MRI, and the guideline recommends including diffusion-weighted sequences, to detect perioperative ischaemia[1] — that scan is not only about "did they get it all".

The one piece of paperwork you can get moving this week

The catastrophic illness certificate. In the NHI Administration's Regulations for Exemption of Insured Persons from Copayment under National Health Insurance, Article 2, Appendix 1 (promulgated 16 September 2024, ROC year 113; applying from 1 January 2025, ROC year 114), "cancers requiring aggressive or long-term treatment" is the first category, and item (5) within it covers malignant tumours other than thyroid, stage I oral cavity/oropharynx/hypopharynx, stage I breast and stage I cervix, spanning C00.0 to C96.9 (excluding C73, C94.4 and C94.6), with a certificate valid for five years[5]. Malignant tumours of the brain and central nervous system (ICD-10-CM C71) fall under that item. The clause is in Chinese; the wording above is my rendering of it.

And one line almost nobody knows: Article 8 of the same regulations provides that where a catastrophic illness certificate is valid for two years or more, it can be re-applied for in the three months before it expires[6]. On a five-year certificate that means you can start getting ready at the end of the fourth year, rather than waiting until it has lapsed. As for which documents to attach and who files them, I could not get through to the NHI Administration's application-notes page this time and did not obtain an official version I could cite — which is not the same as "I could not find it": the page exists, I could not reach it. Ask your case manager or the hospital's catastrophic illness desk directly.

The last thing you need to know today is that this illness comes with a set of situations that cannot wait, and they are not all urgent in the same way — some mean call an ambulance, some mean come back the same day, some can keep until the next clinic. Which symptom sits in which tier is not something I will re-sort here; that is what "When to come back the same day: three tiers" does, in three tiers. Open that one now, while nothing has happened, rather than looking it up once something has.

From the physician

You are mostly being pushed along in these few days, but there are two things you can push yourself. The catastrophic illness certificate: ask your case manager today how it is filed, and remember it runs for five years and can be re-applied for in the three months before it expires — do not let it lapse first. The other is to read "When to come back the same day: three tiers" once now; that article is written to be read before anything happens.
