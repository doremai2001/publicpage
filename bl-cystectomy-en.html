MUSCLE-INVASIVE: TWO ROADS

Cystectomy and urinary diversion

90-day complications and mortality, readmission, kidney function, sexual function, and what the days are like with each of the three urinary diversions — the price written in full, and why this operation is still the standard treatment.

Bladder Cancer Guide · Muscle-invasive: two roads · 8 references
#Surgery#Urostomy#Sexual & Fertility#Rehabilitation

For one and the same operation, 90-day mortality has been reported anywhere from 2.5% to 8%. The difference is not in the surgery; it is in who was in the cohort being reported — and that is what decides how you read every percentage that follows.

When someone is told the bladder has to come out, the first question is almost never the survival rate. It is "so how do I pass urine after that?" This article does two things at once: why this operation is the standard treatment for muscle-invasive bladder cancer, and how large a price it charges you — plus what the days look like with each of the three urinary diversions. How the two roads compare is in "Remove the bladder, or keep it"; this article does not compare outcomes.

Start with why it is the standard

EAU 2026 makes a strong recommendation for T2–T4a N0M0 disease: radical cystectomy[1]. The reason is not tradition, it is how this disease behaves: with this operation alone, about half of patients live beyond five years[1] — which is precisely why neoadjuvant chemotherapy exists ("Why chemotherapy comes before the operation"). And it cannot be left sitting: a 19-study meta-analysis cited by the guideline shows that delaying surgery beyond three months has a negative effect on overall survival (hazard ratio 1.34, 95% confidence interval 1.18–1.53; a hazard ratio divides the rate at which events occur in one group by the rate in the other, and a confidence interval means that if you ran the same study many times over, the answer would land in that range)[1].

How well the operation was done has two visible quality markers. In that multi-institutional matched study, the 440 patients who had a radical cystectomy had a median of 39 lymph nodes removed, and a soft-tissue positive margin in only 1% (5 patients)[2]. Those two numbers are things you can ask about out loud.

The 90-day figures turn on who was in the cohort

For one and the same operation, 90-day mortality has been reported anywhere from 2.5% to 8%, and the difference is not that surgery is getting more dangerous — it is that the cohorts are different. A selected university-centre population: 2.5% (11/440)[2]. A systematic review pooling 66 studies: 4.7% (0.0% to 7.0% across studies), and all 66 of them were retrospective, with not one randomised trial among them[3]. The Dutch national cohort of 9,287 patients from the cancer registry, 2008 to 2018: 5.6% (518 patients)[4]. And in the randomised SWOG S1011 trial, 7% (19/292) in the extended-lymphadenectomy arm against 2% (7/300) in the standard arm[5]. EAU's own stated ranges are 2.1% to 3.2% at 30 days and 3.4% to 8.0% at 90 days[1].

The complications column: the pooled 90-day complication rate is 58.5% (36.1% to 80.5%), and 34.9% during the hospital stay[3]; the Clavien-Dindo figures EAU cites are 50% to 88% for grade I to IV complications, 30% to 42% for severe complications of grade III or above, and a readmission rate of about 25% within 30 days of discharge[1]. Setting those numbers out is not an attempt to talk you out of it — the price and the reason have to be read on the same page. The reason is the sentence from the first section: with this operation alone, about half of patients live beyond five years. That is the result EAU reports for muscle-invasive patients who have had a radical cystectomy, and it is the operation EAU makes a strong recommendation for in T2–T4a N0M0 disease[1]; the same guideline also writes that a delay of more than three months pulls overall survival down[1]. So the way to read this section is: these risks are the price of the standard treatment, not evidence against it. To push that half upwards, the answer the guideline gives is adding chemotherapy before the operation ("Why chemotherapy comes before the operation"), not doing less of it.

More is not better

SWOG S1011 is a rare piece of randomised evidence on this question: cT2–T4a disease with at most two positive nodes, 592 patients randomised, 36 surgeons at 27 centres, median follow-up 6.1 years. Extended lymphadenectomy (up to the common iliac, presciatic and presacral nodes) against standard dissection gave five-year disease-free survival of 56% against 60% (hazard ratio 1.10, 0.86–1.40, p=0.45) and five-year overall survival of 59% against 63% (hazard ratio 1.13, 0.88–1.45), while grade 3 to 5 adverse events were 54% against 44% and 90-day mortality 7% against 2%[5]. The authors conclude that extended dissection did not improve survival and that perioperative morbidity and mortality were higher instead[5].

Three urinary diversions, and the guideline itself refuses to rank them

The most important permission in this section comes from two sentences of EAU's own: "Randomised controlled trials comparing conduit diversion with neobladder or continent cutaneous diversion have not been performed." and "Currently, it is not possible to recommend a particular type of urinary diversion."[1] No randomised trial has compared the three, and the guideline therefore cannot recommend any one of them. EAU also writes that the type of diversion does not affect the cancer outcome[1]. NICE puts it this way: people who choose radical cystectomy should be offered a urinary stoma or a continent diversion, unless there is a strong contraindication such as cognitive impairment, impaired renal function or significant bowel disease[6].

What the days look like with each of the three

  • Ileal conduit (a stoma on the abdominal wall into a urine bag): EAU describes it as a mature technique with predictable results. Complications at 30 days run to 48%, mostly urinary tract infection, pyelonephritis, and leakage or stricture at the ureteroileal junction; long-term stoma-related complications reach up to 24%, and changes in upper tract function or shape up to 30%. And its late complications are the fewest of the three[1].

  • Orthotopic neobladder (a pouch made of bowel joined back to the urethra, emptied by abdominal straining and relaxing the sphincter): registry data from the Netherlands, Germany and Spain show roughly one to two in ten of both men and women having this. The rate of neobladder-related problems EAU tabulates is 30% to 64%. In a prospective assessment in men, daytime continence rose from 59% within the first three months after surgery to 92% at 12 to 18 months, and night-time continence from 28% to 51% at 18 to 36 months. In a series of 56 women with a neobladder, daytime continence was 70.4% and night-time 64.8%, but difficulty emptying is particularly common in women — about two-thirds needed clean intermittent self-catheterisation and close to 45% could not void spontaneously at all[1].

  • Ureterocutaneostomy (the ureter brought directly to the abdominal wall): EAU describes it as the simplest cutaneous diversion, with shorter operating time and lower complication rates, blood loss, transfusion rates, intensive care stay and hospital stay than an ileal conduit; the price is the narrow calibre of the ureter, so stomal stenosis and ascending urinary tract infection are more frequent than with a stoma made from bowel. For frail patients, and for those with a single kidney who need an upper tract diversion, it is the first choice[1].

Some of the complications listed there do not wait for the next appointment: a fever with pain on one side of the back (which is what pyelonephritis or a stricture at the ureteric join tends to look like); urine output from the stoma suddenly dropping, or a whole day with no urine at all; fluid leaking persistently from the wound or around the stoma (which is what an anastomotic leak looks like); or, with a neobladder, being unable to pass urine with a distended abdomen. If any one of those appears, contact your surgical team the same day or go to the emergency department — do not hold out until the scheduled clinic. There is no citable quantitative threshold for any of these, so what I have written is symptoms, not numbers.

Read those three together and one thing stands out: the one least like the original bladder has the fewest late complications; the one most like the original bladder still has only half of patients dry at night after a year and a half; and the simplest one is some people's first choice rather than a compromise. So this is not a ranking. It is three different sets of days.

Kidneys, vitamin B12 and bone: the long bill

The bill after a diversion runs for years. Among 3,360 people who had a radical cystectomy for muscle-invasive bladder cancer, 29% progressed to advanced chronic kidney disease within 12 months; benign ureteroenteric strictures run up to 20%; low vitamin B12 occurs in 17% of people with a bowel diversion, and EAU recommends checking it annually; and SEER data show radical cystectomy associated with a 21% increase in fracture risk, presumed to relate to long-term metabolic acidosis and bone loss — that is an association, not a proven cause[1]. A retrospective dataset of 1,383 patients, meanwhile, shows no significant difference between ileal conduit and orthotopic neobladder in the risk of glomerular filtration rate decline; what the decline was actually associated with was age and anastomotic stricture[1].

The timeline matters too: 45% of diversion-related complications are detected within the first five years of follow-up; in a series of 131 patients, that rose to 94% among those who lived beyond 15 years, with 50% showing upper tract changes and 38% having urinary stones at 15 years[1]. Getting through the days themselves is in "A stoma, a neobladder, and getting back to your days".

Sexual function, and why "how it is chosen" outweighs "which one"

The standard operation in men removes the bladder, prostate, seminal vesicles and distal ureters; historically the standard operation in women took the whole urethra with the adjacent vagina and the uterus, and EAU writes plainly that pelvic floor disorders and sexual and voiding dysfunction are "prevalent" in women after surgery, requiring that a gynaecological history be taken beforehand and that the effects on sexual function and vaginal prolapse be explained[1]. Sexuality-preserving techniques (12 studies, 1,098 patients, mostly open surgery with an orthotopic neobladder, enrolling mostly people with organ-confined disease who had sexual function beforehand) gave significantly better postoperative sexual function than the standard operation: 80% to 90% with prostate sparing, 50% to 100% with prostate capsule sparing, 29% to 78% with nerve-sparing, and no difference in cancer outcomes among the studies that compared them; but the EAU evidence summary states explicitly that no sexuality-preserving technique has been shown to be superior and that no particular approach can be recommended[1]. In that Danish randomised trial from 1991, every man who had a cystectomy lost erectile function[7] — that is a 1980s ruler, to be read as history and not as the present.

And EAU records something more worth recording: letting the patient make the diversion decision after being fully informed is associated with less decisional regret afterwards, and that holds "independent of the method selected" — regardless of which one was eventually chosen[1]. NICE requires a stoma care nurse to be available to talk both before and after the operation[6].

The NHI items, the cell I could not find, and surgical volume

All three approaches have their own items in the NHI fee schedule: 78011B radical cystectomy at 13,799 points, 78013B with pelvic lymphadenectomy at 21,450 points, 78012B with orthotopic neobladder reconstruction at 27,464 points, 78014B with both pelvic lymphadenectomy and orthotopic neobladder reconstruction at 34,992 points, 77032B and 77033B ureteroileal cutaneous diversion (12,960 points unilateral, 17,040 bilateral), and 77018B and 77019B ureterocutaneostomy (8,231 points unilateral, 10,148 bilateral)[8]. For the robot-assisted approaches (78050B, 78051B), the clauses set out certification and roster-reporting requirements for the operating surgeon[8]. Which hospital does which is a question for your own doctor; this article does not write about in-house arrangements.

What I could not find also needs saying: for a subsidy towards stoma supplies (bags, base plates), the 48 hits for 「造口」 I found in the full fee schedule file are all procedure items, with no listed item for supplies at all, and 「尿袋」 returns only 2 hits, neither of them a subsidy[8]. For this cell, please ask the medical affairs office, your case manager or a social worker; I am not going to infer whether it exists.

Last, volume. EAU recommends at least 20 radical cystectomies per hospital per year[1]; but the Dutch national data on 9,287 patients, adjusted for age, stage and neoadjuvant treatment, found that postoperative mortality risk actually rose slightly across annual volumes of 0 to 25 cases and only declined steadily from about 30 cases onwards, which led the authors to write that this threshold "might therefore be reconsidered"[4]. So what can be asked at the next visit is this: how many do you do a year? Which diversion do you recommend for me, and why that one? Roughly how many lymph nodes will be taken? And how many weeks of thromboprophylaxis afterwards (EAU's strong recommendation is low-molecular-weight heparin started on the first postoperative day for at least four weeks)[1]? Write those four on a piece of paper and take it in.

From the physician

The three diversions cannot be ranked, and the guideline itself says there is no randomised trial by which to recommend one. So what really needs preparing is the conversation: make sure you see a stoma care nurse before the operation, tell them about your work, your eyesight, how nimble your hands are and whether anyone is around to help, and settle the diversion after that.
