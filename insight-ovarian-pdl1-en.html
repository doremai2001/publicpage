Immunotherapy in ovarian cancer finally has a result

Immunotherapy has underperformed in ovarian cancer for years. KEYNOTE-B96 combined it with chemotherapy and restricted it to PD-L1-positive disease — a real but modest gain.

Insights · Breast & Gynaecologic · 7 references

This piece is a demonstration of how to read the word "effective". Progression-free survival gained about a month, overall survival about four months, and grade 3 or higher adverse events rose from 55.3% to 67.5%. Both sets of numbers belong in the same sentence.

Ovarian cancer has no reliable screening test, and its early symptoms are easy to mistake for a digestive complaint. In Taiwan roughly three-quarters of patients are already at stage III or beyond when they are diagnosed, and five-year survival in advanced disease sits below 20%[1]. The harder problem comes at relapse. Patients whose disease progresses within six months of platinum chemotherapy are classed as platinum-resistant, and their remaining options are few and rarely durable. Immunotherapy became standard treatment in melanoma and lung cancer years ago, yet it has repeatedly disappointed in ovarian cancer: a decade of trials found that PD-1 or PD-L1 blockade alone produced limited responses in both untreated and recurrent disease, with no validated biomarker to predict who might benefit[2].

The multinational phase 3 ENGOT-ov65/KEYNOTE-B96 trial changed two things about that approach: it paired pembrolizumab with chemotherapy rather than testing it alone, and it read out the PD-L1-positive group separately. The trial enrolled 643 platinum-resistant patients who had received one or two prior lines of therapy and randomised them to pembrolizumab or placebo, each given with weekly paclitaxel, with or without bevacizumab. Among the 466 patients whose tumours expressed PD-L1 at CPS≥1, median progression-free survival was 8.3 months versus 7.2 months (HR 0.72) and median overall survival was 18.2 months versus 14.0 months (HR 0.76); the results appeared in The Lancet[3]. On February 10, 2026, the FDA approved the combination for adults with CPS≥1 disease after one or two prior therapies, and cleared the PD-L1 IHC 22C3 pharmDx assay as its companion diagnostic[4]. Toxicity was meaningfully higher too. Grade 3 or higher treatment-related adverse events occurred in 67.5% of the pembrolizumab group versus 55.3% on placebo, and two patients died of immune-related colitis and pneumonitis[5].

The size of that benefit is worth stating plainly. Progression-free survival improved by about one month and overall survival by roughly four, a real gain but a modest one, and it rests on a single trial that no second phase 3 study has yet confirmed. The eligible group is narrow: platinum-resistant disease, PD-L1 CPS≥1, and only one or two prior lines of systemic therapy. Front-line treatment and platinum-sensitive relapse are not covered. In Taiwan, NHI reimbursement for immune checkpoint inhibitors currently centres on non-squamous non-small cell lung cancer, metastatic colorectal cancer, and early triple-negative breast cancer; ovarian cancer is not yet included[6]. None of KEYNOTE-B96's 187 trial sites were in Taiwan, and the study has closed to enrolment[7]. What is worth doing now is asking whether your tumour has been tested for PD-L1, whether your disease currently meets the definition of platinum-resistant, and which later-line drugs or trials remain on the table. Work through those questions with your gynaecologic oncologist, and do not stop or switch treatment on your own.

From the physician

What you can do now is establish three things: whether your tumour has been tested for PD-L1, whether you currently meet the definition of platinum-resistant disease, and what later-line drugs or trials are worth evaluating. These are judgements to make with your gynaecologic oncologist, not reasons to stop or switch on your own.
