HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

Stage III: the year after chemoradiotherapy

The last session of chemoradiotherapy is half-time. What the consolidation year involves, and why stopping in its second month matters.

Lung Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 11 references
#Lung#Chemotherapy#Immunotherapy#NHI Coverage#Decisions

The year that follows is a course of treatment in its own right — appointments, pauses, restarts — and it carries one reimbursement condition in Taiwan that decides eligibility before any of it begins.

The commonest misunderstanding I meet in stage III clinic is treating the last session of concurrent chemoradiotherapy as the finish line. That day is half-time. A whole year of consolidation therapy follows it, and the situation I find hardest to watch is not the treatment failing. It is someone who stopped on their own in the second month of that year.

Where that year of treatment came from

The trial that established this approach enrolled patients with unresectable stage III non-small cell lung cancer who had not progressed after at least two cycles of platinum-based chemotherapy given concurrently with radiotherapy; immunotherapy started 1 to 42 days after chemoradiotherapy ended, given intravenously every two weeks, for up to 12 months[1]. The survival figures from that trial, and the news from the trials that followed it, are covered on this site in "A failed trial that proved the current standard is good enough", so I will take just one line from it: at the five-year update, median survival was 47.5 months against 29.1 months, and five-year survival 42.9% against 33.4%[2]. Those are medians for a whole group of people. They are not a forecast issued to any one person.

What that year looks like outside a trial comes from an observational dataset of 1,399 patients across 11 countries: median duration of use 11.0 months, meaning most people did complete most of the course; 16.5% stopped because of an adverse event, and 9.5% because of pneumonitis or interstitial lung disease[3]. The same programme later reported an interim survival analysis in 1,154 patients from 10 countries, with three-year survival of 63.2% — remembering that this is observational data and cannot be lined up alongside randomised figures[4].

The size of the side effects belongs in the same paragraph: in the founding trial, grade 3 to 4 adverse events were 29.9% against 26.1% on placebo, and discontinuation for an adverse event 15.4% against 9.8%[1]. Most people are not flattened by this year. But roughly one in six or seven will reach the junction where the question "should I stop" gets asked.

What happens if you stop halfway: honestly, there is no randomised evidence

Nobody has run a randomised trial on this and nobody is going to. What can be consulted is two retrospective single-hospital series. In one of 113 patients, 30 (26%) stopped because of adverse events, at a median of 2.1 months; splitting that group in half gave a median duration of 0.9 months in the shorter half and 4.3 months in the longer, with median progression-free survival of 10.7 against 32.9 months, and duration of treatment was associated with progression-free survival in multivariable analysis (hazard ratio 0.52, 0.31–0.88; a hazard ratio compares how fast progression accrues over the same stretch of time, not a multiple of probability — the numbers worth carrying away are the two month-figures before it). What the authors say themselves matters more: patients who got to around four months had outcomes comparable to those who completed the full year, and for those who stopped inside two months they suggest considering re-treatment[5]. In a second series of 59 patients, 69% stopped early, most often because of disease progression (14 patients) and pulmonary toxicity (10)[6].

How to read those two datasets, and I want to be exact about it: the commonest reason for stopping early is that the cancer is already progressing, so people who stopped early did worse partly because they were already doing worse. Data of this shape cannot prove that stopping caused the decline.

It does support one practical sentence: whether to stop is decided with your team, not decided at home. Pausing because of a side effect, sorting it out and restarting is one of the things this treatment does routinely. Not turning up, not coming back to clinic, and telling no one is a different thing — nobody can assess whether you should restart. On the chart those two look nothing alike.

In this year, the thing to report the same day is breathing

The same real-world study analysed the 268 patients with complete risk-factor data: 19.4% developed symptomatic pneumonitis during consolidation (grade 2 or above, or requiring steroids); risk was higher with a higher mean lung dose and in those with pre-existing chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)[7]. That is an exploratory analysis of 268 people, not an overall incidence; the odds ratios and confidence intervals for each risk factor are in "Breathless after treatment — which pneumonitis is it". The only sentence you need to hold on to: during this year, a change in your breathing is first priority.

New or worsening breathlessness, cough or fever — contact the team the same day. Do not wait for the next scheduled visit, and do not take an antipyretic first and watch. Radiation pneumonitis, checkpoint-inhibitor pneumonitis and infection are managed in different directions; telling them apart and what to do is in "Breathless after treatment — which pneumonitis is it", and I am not repeating it here.

A year is twelve months, not twelve blood tests

You will come back to the hospital more often in this year than during the two months of chemoradiotherapy, but each visit is short. What most people struggle with is not the side effects. It is the feeling of "I finished treatment, so why am I still coming in". I have no figure to cite for this part, only what I see in clinic: treating the year as one course to be organised — how leave gets booked, who drives you, which weeks to keep clear of anything important — is far easier than deciding visit by visit. Pausing for a side effect and restarting is a normal part of this treatment, not a reset of the clock.

If your tumour carries an EGFR mutation, this section is for you

Among lung adenocarcinoma patients in Taiwan, this section reaches a lot of people. The EGFR-mutant subgroup pulled out of that founding trial after the fact was 35 patients (24 on treatment, 11 on placebo), with a progression-free survival hazard ratio of 0.91 (0.39–2.13) and an overall survival hazard ratio of 1.02 (0.39–2.63). The authors write it themselves: the two arms looked similar, the confidence intervals are very wide, the numbers are too small, and the results must be interpreted with caution[8]. That is "has not been shown to work", not "has been shown not to work", and the difference between those two is large.

A separate randomised trial was run in this group: 216 patients randomised to a targeted drug or placebo, median progression-free survival 39.1 months against 5.6 months, hazard ratio 0.16 (0.10–0.24). The overall survival data are immature (only 20% of events), with three-year survival 84% against 74% and a hazard ratio of 0.81 (0.42–1.56), not statistically significant. Grade 3 or higher adverse events were 35% against 12%; radiation pneumonitis, mostly grade 1 to 2, occurred in 48% of the targeted arm and 38% of the placebo arm[9]. That 48% is a reason to report symptoms, not a reason to refuse treatment. How to read a gene report and how long it takes is in "Those two weeks of waiting are not wasted".

One condition in the reimbursement rules that people usually learn about too late

Section 9.69 of the National Health Insurance (健保) drug reimbursement rules (藥品給付規定), effective 1 August 2025 (114 年 8 月 1 日), sets the terms for stage III consolidation: restricted to adults with locally advanced, unresectable stage III disease whose tumour PD-L1 expression is 1% or above, and where non-squamous patients must be EGFR / ALK / ROS-1 wild-type and squamous patients EGFR / ALK wild-type, who have not progressed after definitive concurrent radiotherapy with at least 2 cycles of platinum-based chemotherapy, for a maximum of 12 months[10]. Reimbursement runs for one year from the first prescription, with i-RECIST assessment every 12 weeks while on treatment[11]. Verified 2026-08-30.

Put plainly: under this version of the rules, a patient in Taiwan with non-squamous disease and an EGFR mutation does not fall inside the reimbursed scope for this consolidation. But that is my reading of the version effective 1 August 2025, not a determination about your case — the text gets revised, and how your pathology and gene results are recorded also affects the decision. Please confirm with your case manager (個管師) or the hospital billing office (醫務課) (verified 2026-08-30). I would much rather you asked this before chemoradiotherapy begins than heard it after the final session.

And one gap to state as it is: for the targeted consolidation in that EGFR subgroup above, I could not find a corresponding clause in the current drug reimbursement rules — that is "I could not find it", not "I found that it is not covered". Whether there is another route, what it costs, and whether there is a clinical trial: please confirm with your case manager or the hospital billing office.

On the day chemoradiotherapy ends, these questions need answers

First, what are my PD-L1 and gene test results, and do they meet the conditions for reimbursed consolidation? If they do not, what other options or clinical trials exist for my situation?

Second, what was my mean lung dose? Do I have COPD as a risk factor? Those two set how alert I need to be.

Third, if treatment is interrupted by a side effect, what are the conditions for restarting and who decides? Write the answers down — on the day you need them, you will not want to be asking from scratch.

How stage decides the purpose of treatment is in "Ask whether surgery is possible before you ask about chemotherapy".

From the physician

Whether to pause or stop is decided with your team; pausing for a side effect and restarting afterwards is a normal part of this treatment. Ask about your PD-L1 and gene results, your mean lung dose and the conditions for restarting before the final session, not after it. New breathlessness, cough or fever in this year is a same-day phone call.
