DURING TREATMENT

What those six weeks of radiotherapy are actually like

The mask, the daily routine at the hospital, when the hair starts to go, the time curve of the fatigue, and what that weekly tube of blood is really watching.

Glioblastoma Guide · During Treatment · 15 references
#IMRT#Treatment Pathway#Daily Life#Side Effects

Nine in ten people develop some degree of somnolence over this stretch, and its peak falls in a different place in each of two prospective studies; this article puts both sides down side by side, and blocks the common misreading that a dropping white cell count tells you whether the treatment is working.

"Six weeks" sounds long. What it actually is: thirty sessions, each of them under twenty minutes lying on a machine. The hard part is not those twenty minutes, it is having to come every day for six weeks running, with your body going wrong somewhere in the middle. This article is about what actually happens across those thirty sessions.

The mask is not there to tie you down, it is there to put you back in the same place every day

You are not irradiated on the first visit; first comes planning. A thermoplastic mask fixes the head and neck, and then a simulation CT is taken (a slice thickness of 3 mm is commonly used)[1]. What the mask solves is not that you will fidget; it is that thirty fractions have to come back to the same position, so that the errors do not accumulate.

The same logic extends to the imaging. A prospective study from India describes the method in a way that sits very close to the patient's experience: even the MRI is scanned in the treatment position, with the same mask and the same head rest, so that it can be matched to the planning CT[2]. Once the two image sets have been fused, the American Association of Physicists in Medicine's TG-132 report recommends a manual adjustment after the automatic registration, and lists the anatomical landmarks you can align on: the orbits, the optic nerves, the brainstem, the ventricles, the external auditory canals[1].

Five days of beam, seven days of tablets — the weekend cell is the one that gets missed

The rhythm of the course comes from the 2005 randomised trial that established standard treatment, and the protocol is rigid about it: 2 Gy a day, five days a week, 60 Gy over six weeks; the concurrent temozolomide is taken from the first day of radiotherapy to the last, seven days a week, weekends included[3]. That specification was produced by 573 people across 85 centres. It is not a scheduling convenience.

So the correct state of a weekend is: you do not come to the hospital, but the tablets carry on. "The weekend is a rest, so everything rests" is a misunderstanding I have met more than once. The three stretches that make up the whole course are set out in "Six weeks together, then six cycles".

What that weekly tube of blood is actually watching

During the concurrent stretch you usually come back weekly for bloods — counts, kidney function, liver function; and it is checked again before maintenance starts[2]. This gets misread as "seeing whether the tumour has shrunk". It is not. It is watching whether the drug has pushed the marrow too far down.

In the randomised trial, grade 3 or 4 haematological toxicity during the concurrent stretch was 7%[3]. In a single-centre audit of 52 patients, grade 3 or 4 thrombocytopenia was 19%, with a median duration of 32 days, 17% of people stopped the drug because of it, and the word the authors use is "possibly irreversible"[4]. In a more recent cohort of 206 people, thrombocytopenia of any grade occurred in 61.1% of patients[5]. And one thing women should know in advance: in a cohort of 492 glioma patients on concurrent chemoradiotherapy, severe myelosuppression occurred in 44% of women against 18% of men[6].

The lymphocyte line is the one most easily over-read. There is a pooled analysis that has examined its relationship with survival[7]; but the analysis that put five clinical trials together, 2,073 people, came out forked: during the concurrent stretch, low-grade lymphopenia leaned towards better overall survival and high-grade towards worse; during maintenance, both grades leaned towards better[8]. The directions do not agree, so do not take it as a report card. White cells dropping does not mean the treatment is working, and does not mean it is not working. How to look at survival is written in "That number, and how I am going to tell you about it".

There is one more tablet worth asking about properly: the one that prevents pneumocystis pneumonia. The threshold in the 2018 guideline from the American Society of Clinical Oncology and the Infectious Diseases Society of America is "prophylaxis is recommended where the risk exceeds 3.5%", and the examples given include people taking the equivalent of 20 mg of prednisone or more daily for longer than a month[9] — brain tumour patients on long-term steroids fall squarely inside that (the full discussion of steroids is in "Steroids: the drug that saves you, and has to come down"). And a 2026 systematic review of 35 papers and 13,637 people works out a pooled incidence during concurrent chemoradiotherapy of 0.74%, which is below that threshold; the same review also records that the drug label and some guidelines still require everyone to be given prophylaxis[10]. This rule is being recalculated, but you are not the one doing the recalculating: do not stop it on your own; take "why am I on this, and until when" into clinic and ask once.

Two ways of drawing the target, and the only difference is whether the oedema counts

You will hear about the EORTC way and the RTOG way, and they fork at exactly one point: whether the rim of peritumoural oedema on T2/FLAIR goes inside the target or not. EORTC is single-phase, taking the surgical cavity plus residual enhancing tumour with a margin, straight through to 60 Gy; RTOG is two-phase, first including the oedema to 46 Gy and then shrinking the field for a further 14 Gy[2]. A dosimetric study took the images of the same 20 patients and one set of planning parameters and drew all five definitions on each: median PTV volume ran from 220.4 cm³ for ESTRO/EANO and 299 cm³ for EORTC to 519.7 cm³ for the early RTOG/NRG definition[1]. Same tumour, and the volume can differ by more than a factor of two.

Which one is better? There is no answer strong enough to give. The study that took the two ways of drawing and compared them at random failed to accrue during the pandemic, and ended with only 18 people in the analysis; the primary endpoint was quality of life, and there was no significant difference between the arms[2]. Eighteen people prove nothing; all it says is that we do not know yet. And that dosimetric study only measured dose, not clinical outcome — a smaller volume does not mean fewer side effects[1].

Nine in ten people get tired, and the tiredness has a time curve

Fatigue is not laziness. It has a shape. A prospective study of 70 patients receiving radical cranial irradiation measured from baseline through to week 10 after treatment: 90% developed at least grade 1 somnolence, with scores climbing from week 3 to week 12, peaking as the course ended and then improving over roughly six weeks; the authors could not find any patient, disease or treatment characteristic that predicted who would get it, or how badly[11].

Another prospective study, of 33 people, has a different shape: falling for the first three weeks, then flat, rising only after week 10, with 43.3% at grade 2 in weeks 11 to 12[12]. The two peaks do not line up, so I am not going to tell you which week will be the worst. What they share: it has a time curve, it may be at its heaviest after the course has finished, and it gets better by itself. Not being able to get out of bed once treatment has ended is usually this curve, not the disease turning worse. As for stimulants, there has been a randomised placebo-controlled trial[13] — that is a question to raise in clinic, not one to settle for yourself at a pharmacy counter.

What falls out is the patch of scalp the beam passes through

In a prospective study of 48 patients with primary brain tumours (prescription doses 50.4 to 60.0 Gy), 32 lost hair, of whom 27 temporarily and 5 permanently; the earlier literature the paper quotes says temporary hair loss appears about 2 to 3 weeks after radiotherapy starts, and most of it grows back within 3 to 6 months[14].

How much comes out, and whether it grows back, depends on the dose that patch of scalp received: the thresholds calculated in the same study were 22.15 Gy for temporary and 36.81 Gy for permanent loss (using scalp D2, the highest dose received by 2% of the volume); other data the paper cites put the probability of permanent loss at about 50% once the follicles receive 43 Gy[14]. That population is a mixture of primary brain tumours, not pure glioblastoma, so discount the numbers accordingly. But it hands you a concrete clinic question: which patch of scalp do my beams enter and leave through? That is the patch that goes, not the whole head.

"Can you avoid the memory area" — in this disease there are no data behind it

People often ask me whether the hippocampus can be protected. Honestly: the target delineation guideline from the European SocieTy for Radiotherapy and Oncology and the European Association of Neuro-Oncology puts the hippocampus in the group of "non-critical normal organs" and does not recommend sacrificing PTV coverage for it; the text states plainly that neurocognitive data supporting hippocampal avoidance in glioblastoma radiotherapy are currently lacking (I have not obtained the full guideline; this is as reported in the dosimetric study above)[1].

The large randomised trial usually brought out in support of it enrolled patients with brain metastases, not this disease — what that trial proved and where its limits lie, together with the whole question of cognition, is in "Memory, concentration, and changes in who he is". Glioblastoma is infiltrative, and the target often sits right beside the temporal lobe and the hippocampus; avoiding it means treating less tumour.

Try not to miss the fractions you are booked for — but not coping is not yours to carry alone

Dragging the course out has a price. In a single-centre retrospective analysis of 166 patients with IDH-wildtype glioblastoma, the median overall treatment time was 43 days; after adjustment, going beyond 48 days was an independent adverse prognostic factor (hazard ratio 1.41); more interruptions was worse, and clinical interruptions caused by toxicity were worse than a one-off technical interruption such as a machine breakdown[15]. A hazard ratio of 1.41 divides the speed at which deaths occurred in the two groups: in the beyond-48-days group they occurred faster.

This is retrospective single-centre data, and "being interrupted" is thoroughly tangled up with "being in worse shape" to start with, so it cannot be read as "hold out without interruptions and you will do better". Its real use runs the other way round: the group interrupted by toxicity did worst, so toxicity has to be reported early and dealt with early — do not hold out until stopping is the only option left. Fever, a convulsion, sudden weakness, one leg swelling up are not things to save for next week's appointment; they are in "When to come back the same day: three tiers".

And one more thing to leave here for later: the first follow-up MRI after the course ends can look bigger, and that is not necessarily progression — but neither is it that bigger never matters. That line is drawn in "How the follow-up scans are scheduled, and why bigger is not always worse". As for these six weeks, ask one thing while you are still coping: if I cannot cope this week, how will you adjust it — stop for a few days, or deal with the side effect first? Having heard the answer in advance is how you avoid gritting your teeth alone on the day you genuinely cannot go on.

From the physician

The real risk in these six weeks is not the twenty minutes on the machine, it is the few days you spend gritting your teeth — the group interrupted by toxicity did worst of all. So report side effects early rather than holding out until stopping is the only option. The marks on your mask, how the target was drawn, and how much dose your scalp received are three things you can ask about specifically in clinic.
