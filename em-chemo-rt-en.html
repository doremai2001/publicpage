TREATMENT AND THE EVIDENCE

Adding chemotherapy: what it buys and what it costs

PORTEC-3 gained seven percentage points of 10-year overall survival, with the benefit concentrated in stage III and serous disease; the price is grade 3 or worse adverse events, 12% against 60%.

Endometrial Cancer Guide · Treatment and the evidence · 9 references
#Chemotherapy#Evidence#Side Effects#Decisions

Three randomised trials each answer one question: chemotherapy added on top of radiotherapy, radiotherapy added on top of chemotherapy, and pelvic radiotherapy swapped for brachytherapy plus chemotherapy. The three answers point different ways, and not one of them collects into "so you should have chemotherapy". What this article does is get the question right, and get it to the right people.

This question usually arrives in a hurry: "Doctor, do I need chemotherapy or not?" I understand the hurry, because chemotherapy sounds like a step up a level. But in endometrial cancer there is no general answer to it — the benefit of adding chemotherapy is concentrated in a few very particular groups, and the price is paid by everybody. So I am not going to hand you a "yes" or a "no" here. What I can give you is who the benefit is concentrated in, how big it is, and who pays for it.

My position first: vaginal brachytherapy and pelvic radiotherapy for endometrial cancer are treatments I deliver myself, every week; the other side of every comparison in this topic is my gynaecological-oncology colleagues' surgery and my gynaecological-oncology and medical-oncology colleagues' drugs. That is why every comparison comes with its primary source, so you can take it back to the gynaecological oncology clinic for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

PORTEC-3: one benefit, one price, read together

PORTEC-3 was a randomised trial across 103 centres in 7 countries in 660 women at high risk. Eligibility used FIGO 2009: stage I grade 3 with deep myometrial invasion and/or lymphovascular space invasion, stage II or III, or stage I to III serous or clear cell histology. One arm had pelvic radiotherapy 48.6 Gy; the other had chemotherapy added to the same radiotherapy — two doses of cisplatin during radiotherapy, then four cycles of carboplatin plus paclitaxel. At a median follow-up of 10.1 years, 10-year overall survival was 74.4% (69.8 to 79.4) against 67.3% (62.3 to 72.7), adjusted hazard ratio 0.73 (0.54 to 0.97), p=0.032. 10-year recurrence-free survival was 72.8% against 67.4%, adjusted hazard ratio 0.74 (0.56 to 0.98), p=0.034[1]. A hazard ratio of 0.73 means that deaths occurred in the arm that had chemotherapy added at about 0.73 times the rate in the other arm; the brackets hold the 95% confidence interval, the range within which the estimate plausibly sits.

The price is in the same trial, and it has to be read together with the paragraph above. Grade 3 or worse adverse events during treatment were 60% (198/330) in the chemoradiotherapy arm and 12% (41/330) with radiotherapy alone (p less than 0.0001). At three years, grade 2 or worse neuropathy was 8% against 1%[2]; at five years, grade 2 or worse adverse events 38% against 23% (p=0.002)[3]. Seven percentage points of 10-year overall survival is what that whole row of numbers buys. Whether the exchange is worth making depends on which group you are in — and that is the next section.

The benefit is not shared out evenly

Split the same trial up and the gap is wide. FIGO 2009 stage III (295 women): 10-year overall survival 69.5% against 56.1%, adjusted hazard ratio 0.66 (0.45 to 0.97), p=0.033. Serous (105 women): 10-year overall survival 57.1% against 41.8%, adjusted hazard ratio 0.55 (0.31 to 0.98), p=0.044[1]. And in FIGO 2009 stage I to II, 5-year overall survival was 83.8% against 82.0%, hazard ratio 0.84 (0.52 to 1.38), p=0.50 — no benefit visible[3]. The 2025 update of the European three-society guideline (ESGO/ESTRO/ESP) uses the same sentence: the overall survival difference from adding concurrent and adjuvant platinum-based chemotherapy "was seen in stage III carcinomas and in serous carcinomas, regardless of stage"[4].

One more thing is worth knowing, because it changes how you read "is the evidence strong". In PORTEC-3's 2018 final analysis, 5-year overall survival was 81.8% against 76.7%, adjusted hazard ratio 0.76 (0.54 to 1.06), p=0.11, which did not reach statistical significance; failure-free survival was the endpoint that was significant (hazard ratio 0.71, 0.53 to 0.95, p=0.022)[2]. Significance for survival appeared only with the 2019 follow-up update (hazard ratio 0.70, 0.51 to 0.97, p=0.034) and with the 10-year analysis[3][1]. The two earlier European randomised trials (NSGO-EC-9501/EORTC-55991 and MaNGO ILIADE-III, 540 women between them) have the same shape when pooled. The pooled hazard ratio for progression was 0.63 (0.44 to 0.89, p=0.009); but overall survival was 0.69 (0.46 to 1.03), p=0.07, which did not cross the threshold[5].

GOG-258: what radiotherapy buys runs the opposite way to what people expect

PORTEC-3 asked whether chemotherapy added on top of radiotherapy helps. GOG-258 asked the reverse question: women with FIGO 2009 stage III or IVA disease are having six months of chemotherapy anyway, so does adding radiotherapy make it better? 813 were enrolled, 736 were analysable, and the primary endpoint was recurrence-free survival. The answer was no: the proportion alive and free of recurrence at 60 months was 59% (53 to 65) with chemoradiotherapy against 58% (53 to 64) with chemotherapy alone, hazard ratio 0.90 (90% confidence interval 0.74 to 1.10)[6].

But the three directions among the recurrence sites have to be written out separately, because they point at different things. Vaginal recurrence: 2% with chemoradiotherapy against 7% with chemotherapy alone, hazard ratio 0.36 (0.16 to 0.82); pelvic and para-aortic nodal recurrence: 11% against 20%, hazard ratio 0.43 (0.28 to 0.66); distant recurrence: 27% against 21%, hazard ratio 1.36 (1.00 to 1.86), and this is the box where the chemoradiotherapy arm had more. Grade 3 to 5 adverse events were 58% against 63%[6]. The lower bound of the interval on that distant box sits right on 1.00, and it is an observation on a secondary endpoint, so it cannot be read as "radiotherapy does harm". Equally, the first two boxes leave "radiotherapy is useless" with nowhere to stand. The 2025 guideline turns this into an option: "chemotherapy alone is an alternative option based on the GOG-258 results for stage III-IVA disease"[4].

GOG-249: swapping pelvic radiotherapy for brachytherapy plus chemotherapy was not better

The third trial asked about early-stage women at high-intermediate or high risk: could "vaginal brachytherapy plus three cycles of chemotherapy" replace pelvic radiotherapy? GOG-249 enrolled 601 women (median age 63, 74% FIGO 2009 stage I; histology endometrioid 71%, serous 15%, clear cell 5%). The result was 60-month recurrence-free survival 0.76 against 0.76, hazard ratio 0.92 (with 90% limits of 0.69 to 1.23); 60-month overall survival 0.87 against 0.85. Pelvic or para-aortic nodal recurrence was 9% in the brachytherapy-plus-chemotherapy arm and 4% in the radiotherapy arm, and acute toxicity was also higher with brachytherapy plus chemotherapy, while late toxicity was similar in the two arms. The paper's own conclusion is "Superiority of VCB/C compared with pelvic RT was not demonstrated" — not shown to be better, which is not the same as shown to be worse (how the risk groups are defined is listed in "After the operation, do you still need radiotherapy")[7]. The guideline's wording for the high-intermediate-risk group is equally direct: "The unselected use of chemotherapy in this group seems not justified"[4].

Carcinosarcoma and serous: two more sets of figures to read on their own

Carcinosarcoma now counts as a high-grade endometrial carcinoma in the current classification, and its chemotherapy has a randomised trial of its own. GOG-261 enrolled 449 eligible women with uterine carcinosarcoma and compared paclitaxel plus carboplatin with paclitaxel plus ifosfamide. The result: median overall survival 37 months against 29 months, hazard ratio 0.87 (with 90% limits of 0.70 to 1.075), and non-inferiority was met. Non-inferiority proves that the difference did not exceed a line agreed in advance; it does not prove that the two are "the same". Median progression-free survival was 16 months against 12 months[8].

The serous box needs care over one common confusion. In PORTEC-3's 10-year data, 48 of the 65 serous carcinomas (74%) were p53-abnormal, and 48 of the 99 p53-abnormal cancers (48%) were serous[1]. The overlap is large, but the two are not synonyms and they are not saying the same thing. How molecular classification enters this decision is in "How molecular classification changes your treatment". Every efficacy figure for immunotherapy sits in "Immunotherapy: who does it actually help", and is not repeated here.

Taiwan's clauses mention endometrial cancer in exactly one place

In the NHI drug reimbursement regulations, the carboplatin clause 9.2 opens with "restricted to". Its first eight items are ovarian cancer, impaired renal function, and various lung and breast cancer settings in combination with immunotherapy drugs. Item 9 (added with effect from 1 August of Republic of China year 115) is the one that mentions endometrial cancer, and what it covers is dostarlimab and paclitaxel "…in combination…for the first-line treatment of adult patients with mismatch repair deficient (dMMR) or microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) primary advanced or first recurrent endometrial cancer (EC)". The paclitaxel clause 9.5.1 reads "reimbursed, within the range of indications recorded on the product licence, for cases of ovarian cancer, non-small-cell lung cancer, breast cancer and Kaposi's sarcoma". Cisplatin, doxorubicin and ifosfamide have no clause of their own in the reimbursement regulations[9]. Those quotations are my translations of the clause text.

I copy the clauses out here because they cannot be turned into "so adjuvant chemotherapy after surgery is not covered" or "so you will have to pay for it yourself". A clause says the thing it says; for the rest, I could not find a listed item spelling it out — ask your medical affairs office or your case manager.

Whose decision this is

By now you will have noticed that adding chemotherapy or not is not a question a patient weighs up on her own. It depends on stage (and which version), histology, molecular classification, whether the nodes were staged, and whether your heart, lungs, liver and kidneys can carry that 60% rate of grade 3 or worse adverse events. The proper place for this decision is the multidisciplinary team meeting, with gynaecological oncology, medical oncology and radiation oncology having all seen your material.

Your job is to ask the question properly. Take your pathology report and ask these four. First, which PORTEC-3 group do my stage and histology fall into (it used FIGO 2009) — am I in the stage III group or the serous one? Second, if I am stage I to II, which item on my report is your reason for adding chemotherapy, rather than which trial? Third, which grade 3 or worse side effects do you expect me to meet, and roughly how likely is each? Fourth, if I have fewer cycles, or radiotherapy alone, how much do you estimate I lose? Asking those four before you decide is far more use than searching the internet for "chemotherapy or not".

From the physician

Whether you can finish chemotherapy matters as much as whether it works. I have seen too many women hold on to the fourth cycle before saying "actually I have felt terrible since the second" — said three months earlier, the conversation about reducing the dose would have started three months earlier.
