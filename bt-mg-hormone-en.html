Progestogen drugs and meningioma

The signal sits with a few specific progestogens, not with contraceptives generally; and whether to stop is the decision of the clinic that prescribed it, not of this article.

Benign Brain Tumour Guide · Meningioma · 18 references
#Endocrine Therapy#Regulation & Coverage#Evidence#Decisions

One person, one year, in the box that carries the most risk: about 2.4 in 10,000. Hold that against the "six times" and "twenty times" multiples before either of them frightens you. Of the high-risk molecules only one can still be obtained in Taiwan, and the Taiwan FDA put "meningioma" into its contraindications section by order back in 2022.

"Doctor, I've taken the pill — is that why I've got a meningioma?" That is the commonest question I hear in a meningioma clinic, and the one most easily answered too quickly. There are two fast answers and both are wrong: one is "it makes no difference, they're all the same", the other is "yes, stop it right away". The correct answer starts by working out which molecule you were actually taking.

Clear the biggest misunderstanding first: ordinary oral contraceptives show no association

A meta-analysis of 12 case-control and 6 cohort studies found no significant association between oral contraceptive use and meningioma risk, with a relative risk of 0.93 (95% confidence interval 0.83 to 1.03) — relative risk divides one group's probability of the event by the other's, and a confidence interval is the range within which the estimate plausibly sits[1]. In the same analysis, menopausal hormone therapy had a relative risk of 1.19 (1.01 to 1.40), and 1.32 (1.07 to 1.64) in postmenopausal women[1]; a separate meta-analysis looking only at menopausal hormone therapy (11 studies, 1,820,954 people, 3,249 meningiomas) gave a pooled odds ratio of 1.29 (1.03 to 1.60), significant at 1.27 for current users and not significant at 1.12 for past users, and the authors themselves write that whether this association is causal is for further research to establish[2]. An odds ratio divides one group's ratio of happening to not happening by the other group's, and is not a multiple of probability; and all of this is observational data, where association is not causation.

So "taking the pill gives you a meningioma" does not stand up on the evidence. The clear signal is in a few other molecules.

The ones with a signal, and what the absolute risk actually looks like

The main evidence comes from the French national health insurance database. One thing first, because it changes how you read every number that follows: in these studies the outcome is defined as "underwent surgery or radiotherapy for an intracranial meningioma", not "was diagnosed with a meningioma" — so what is being counted is the fraction severe enough to need treating, which is lower than the true prevalence[3][4][5].

The strongest signal is high-dose cyproterone acetate. That study looked at more than 250,000 girls and women aged 7 to 70, counting a cumulative dose of 3 grams or more in the first six months as exposed. Among those not taking it, 4.5 cases occurred per 100,000 people per year; among those taking it, 23.8 — an adjusted hazard ratio of 6.6 (4.0 to 11.1). A hazard ratio divides the rate at which events occur in one group by the rate in the other; it is not the same ruler as the relative risk above, which divides one group's probability by the other's, and the two kinds of number should not be compared directly. And the higher the dose, the wider the gap: in those with a cumulative dose above 60 grams, the hazard ratio jumped to 21.7 (10.8 to 43.5)[3].

The nomegestrol acetate study covered 1.06 million women. Among those not taking it, 7.0 meningiomas occurred per 100,000 people per year; among those taking it, 19.3 — an age-adjusted relative risk of 2.9 (2.4 to 3.7); this study reports a relative risk, not the hazard ratio of the one above. In those with a cumulative dose above 6 grams, the relative risk jumped to 12.0 (9.9 to 16.0)[4].

The chlormadinone acetate study ran to more than 820,000 people, with the same shape: 6.8 per 100,000 person-years in the comparison group and 18.5 in the treated group, an age-adjusted relative risk of 3.1 (2.4 to 4.0); those with a cumulative dose above 8.64 grams approached 47 per 100,000 person-years, an age-adjusted relative risk of 6.9 (5.1 to 9.2)[5]. Three molecules, one shape: the longer it is taken and the more accumulates, the wider the gap.

It is worth putting the absolute numbers on the table. In the highest-risk box, 23.8 per 100,000 person-years works out at roughly 2.4 in 10,000 for one person in one year. The European Medicines Agency's own wording runs the same way: this side effect is rare, affecting roughly 1 to 10 people in every 10,000 depending on dose and duration of use, with the risk rising with cumulative dose[6]. The multiples are frightening and the baseline is low — both have to be held in your head at once.

The other half of the answer: which molecules, and which not

A French national case-control study (18,061 people who had surgery for an intracranial meningioma between 2009 and 2018, matched 1:5 by birth year and place of residence to 90,305 controls) took the molecules apart one at a time[7]. Excess risk was seen for medrogestone (odds ratio 3.49, 2.38 to 5.10), injectable medroxyprogesterone acetate (5.55, 2.27 to 13.56) and promegestone (2.39, 1.85 to 3.09), and that excess was driven by people who had used them for more than a year. No excess risk was seen for progesterone, dydrogesterone, or levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine systems; for dienogest and hydroxyprogesterone the numbers were too small to conclude anything. The three already known to carry risk came out in the same dataset as cyproterone 19.21 (16.61 to 22.22), nomegestrol 4.93 and chlormadinone 3.87[7].

A 2026 analysis reviewing 78 studies, of which 14 entered the meta-analysis, pulls the overall picture together: cyproterone acetate pooled odds ratio 12.36 (7.47 to 20.45), certainty of evidence moderate; depot injectable medroxyprogesterone acetate 2.68 (1.72 to 4.19), certainty low; chlormadinone, nomegestrol, promegestone, medrogestone and desogestrel showed signals but with sparse data and high heterogeneity, so they were not pooled; levonorgestrel and progesterone-type agents showed no signal[8]. The authors' own assessment of overall certainty is that the observational design, residual confounding, heterogeneity and imprecision in some exposure measures all limit the strength of the evidence[8].

What happens after stopping is finer-grained than you would expect

At population level: a year or more after nomegestrol is stopped, the risk is the same as the comparison group (relative risk 1.0, 0.8 to 1.3)[4]; a year after cyproterone is stopped the hazard ratio against the comparison group is still 1.8 (1.0 to 3.2) — clearly down, but not all the way back to baseline[3].

At tumour level: a series of 52 consecutive patients and 137 meningiomas diagnosed during cyproterone use, volume-measured on every scan available. Counting by tumour, 47% responded and ended at a low volume, 41% responded but ended at a volume still high, and 12% did not respond; counting by patient, 14% did not respond[9]. Which is to say, after stopping, most stabilise or shrink, and a bit over one in ten do not. The same study found that adding the early volume change at three months after stopping brought the prediction error, counted by patient, down from 23.1% to 3.8%[9] — so that scan three months after stopping does mean something; it is the first bend in the trend.

There is a natural experiment at population level as well. After France introduced national risk-minimisation measures in August 2018 and June 2019, the number of high-dose cyproterone users fell from 55,000 to 7,900 (a drop of 85%), and brain imaging screening among cisgender women rose from 11% to 70%; the annual rate of meningioma surgery attributable to exposure to this drug fell by 93% in cisgender women and 86% in men between 2017 and 2021[10].

Pregnancy, receptors, and tamoxifen

Pregnancy is a question that regularly gets told backwards. A Swedish national cohort (2,204,126 women born between 1958 and 2000, linked to the birth register and the cancer register) found that meningioma was more common in women who had not given birth than in those who had (standardised incidence ratio 1.73, 1.52 to 1.95 — a standardised incidence ratio divides the number of cases observed by the number expected in a matched general population); that meningiomas detected during pregnancy were fewer than expected (0.40, 0.20 to 0.72); and that risk was not raised in the year after delivery (1.04, 0.74 to 1.41). The authors conclude that pregnancy is not a risk factor for meningioma, and that the low detection rate during pregnancy may simply reflect less imaging being done[11]. That has to be kept separate from a different point: the International Consortium on Meningiomas consensus puts it that pregnancy does not appear to be a risk factor for developing a meningioma, but that a meningioma already present growing faster during pregnancy has long been described in the literature[12]. Whether one appears, and whether an existing one speeds up, are two different sentences.

At the mechanism level: an individual-patient-data meta-analysis of 114 studies, 5,810 patients and 6,092 tumours found the proportion positive for progesterone receptor to be 0.76 (0.72 to 0.80), with oestrogen receptor positivity only 0.06 to 0.11 depending on the assay[13]. That the signal sits with progestogen drugs rather than oestrogen is partly why — but receptor expression is not the same as a drug causing disease.

Tamoxifen points the other way. A Taiwanese National Health Insurance database cohort (50,442 women with breast cancer taking tamoxifen against 30,929 not taking it) gave an overall adjusted hazard ratio of 0.64 (0.40 to 1.02), and 0.42 (0.19 to 0.91) in those using it for more than 1,500 days[14]; in a Swedish cohort of 227,535 women with breast cancer, the standardised incidence ratio was significantly raised at 1.54 (1.30 to 1.81) in those not exposed to tamoxifen, and not significant at 1.06 (0.84 to 1.32) in those exposed[15]. Both are retrospective cohorts, and what they will support is "no signal of an increase was seen".

Taiwan: of the three, only one is still on the shelf

Searching the Taiwan FDA's complete drug marketing authorisation dataset (72,043 records) record by record[16]: cyproterone acetate has several authorisations currently in force; nomegestrol acetate has only one record in the whole table, cancelled, its validity ending on 15 January 2020; chlormadinone acetate likewise has only one, cancelled; medrogestone and promegestone return zero current records; and the current authorisations for medroxyprogesterone acetate are all oral formulations, the injectable having been cancelled with validity ending on 9 May 2020. Most of the highest-risk molecules in the French studies are not obtainable by a reader in Taiwan. The one still here is cyproterone.

And the Taiwanese product label says so, because the regulator ordered it. The Taiwan FDA's announcement of 15 September 2022 (衛授食字第1111407692號), made under Article 48 of the Pharmaceutical Affairs Act, requires the Chinese-language labels of high-dose (50 mg) cyproterone-containing products to add, in the contraindications section — this is my translation of the order's wording — "patients who currently have a meningioma or have a history of meningioma", and to add meningioma-related safety information to the warnings and precautions and the pharmacological properties sections; the indication was at the same time changed to "for use only where there is no other treatment option or where other treatments have been ineffective"; the dosage section gained "once the clinical symptoms have improved, the lowest effective dose should be used to maintain the effect"; and the low-dose (2 mg) preparations carry the same additions[17]. In Europe, products containing nomegestrol or chlormadinone are listed as not to be used by patients who have or have had a meningioma, with patients asked to watch for changes in vision, hearing loss or tinnitus, loss of smell, headache, memory loss, seizures and weakness in the arms or legs[18].

Who to take this question back to

The thing not to do, first: do not stop the drug on your own. Whether it is stopped or switched is the decision of the doctor who prescribed it, and it has to be weighed together with why you were put on it in the first place — hirsutism, acne, endometriosis, menopausal symptoms all have different alternatives, and the original problem does not disappear by itself once the tablets stop.

Take the pack back to the clinic that prescribed it. The first thing to establish is which molecule you are actually on. Read the English ingredient name on the pack, not the brand name. Then how long you have taken it and roughly how much has accumulated; then ask whether, given where you are now, a brain magnetic resonance scan should be arranged[10], and if the decision is to stop, whether a repeat scan three months after stopping is worth doing to see the trend[9].

One last sentence for people who already have a meningioma. Everything linking these drugs to meningioma comes from observational data, and the outcome counted was "a meningioma severe enough to need surgery or radiotherapy"[3]. What it can tell you is that this question is worth taking to someone, not that your tumour was caused by it. Whether to treat it and how to follow it up are in "Watching is a form of treatment" and "How follow-up is scheduled, and when it stops".

From the physician

Please do not stop your tablets because you read this. The decision belongs to the doctor who prescribed them; your job is to take the pack to that clinic and let them know what you have read and which molecule you are on.
