DURING TREATMENT

The chemotherapy you take at home

Section 17 of the label gives patients thresholds they can count: how many stools, what temperature, how far the hands and feet have gone. This article sets that alongside the same-day warnings for five other commonly used drugs, naming each drug one by one.

This is where the whole series keeps its emergency warnings: from the fever, diarrhoea, dehydration, mouth ulceration and hand-foot syndrome thresholds for the oral 5-FU-type drug capecitabine, through to which single symptom means a phone call the same day for oxaliplatin, irinotecan, bevacizumab and cetuximab — every line written with the drug's name and a number you can count.

When you're on a drip there is a nurse beside you watching. When the drug goes home in a bag, the first person to notice something is wrong is you. That is the hard part of oral chemotherapy — it hands you the first step of the judgement. So this article isn't about how the drug works. It gives you numbers you can count: count to here and hold today's tablet, count to here and phone the same day.

Hand-foot syndrome is not "my hands are a bit dry"

X-ACT was a randomised adjuvant trial in stage III colon cancer: 1,987 patients received either oral capecitabine or injected 5-FU plus leucovorin, for 24 weeks[1]. In the safety table carried in the label, 60% of the 995 patients on capecitabine developed palmar-plantar erythrodysaesthesia syndrome — hand-foot syndrome — and 17% reached grade 3 or 4 (painful enough to interfere with everyday movements, or the skin already broken)[2]. The same table gives diarrhoea 47% (12% grade 3 or 4) and stomatitis 22%[2].

A randomised trial of 605 patients with metastatic colorectal cancer puts it more directly: compared with injected 5-FU, capecitabine came out lower on almost every toxicity, with grade 3 hand-foot syndrome the one clear exception, where it was markedly higher[3]. The impression that "the oral one is gentler" does not hold on this item. Prevention? The meta-analysis of vitamin B6 for preventing hand-foot syndrome is negative[4]. I have no preventive method I can promise you.

The label itself gives patients numbers they can count

Section 17 of the Xeloda label is one of the few regulatory documents written directly for the patient, and what it gives is countable thresholds. The action under every line below is the same: stop that day's dose, and contact the treatment team the same day[2].

  • Diarrhoea reaching grade 2: four to six more bowel movements a day than usual, or movements starting at night. Severe bloody diarrhoea with severe abdominal pain and fever means contacting them immediately.

  • Dehydration at grade 2 or worse — you can't drink, urine drops off noticeably, you're dizzy on standing. The label requires that you not restart the drug on your own until fluids have been replaced and the cause of the dehydration corrected.

  • Hand-foot syndrome at grade 2 or worse: hands and feet red and swollen, painful enough to interfere with everyday movements, peeling or broken skin.

  • Stomatitis severe enough that you cannot swallow — not food, and not even your own tablets. The label lists mucositis as one of the earliest severe toxicities when DPD activity is inadequate; not being able to eat, drink or swallow your tablets is a same-day call, not something to keep for the next clinic visit[2].

  • Jaundice, or a marked drop in the amount of urine you pass.

  • If you also take warfarin or another vitamin K antagonist, any sign of bleeding is a same-day call — black stools, gums that won't stop bleeding, large bruises; the label requires more frequent monitoring of clotting with that combination.

Holding today's tablet and stopping your own course are two different things

The section above is about stopping a drug, while "Numb hands and feet: when to speak up" is about not interrupting treatment on your own. The two do not contradict each other, because they are not about the same thing: Capecitabine is a tablet you take at home, and section 17 of the Xeloda label is written for the patient: at defined thresholds you hold today's tablet and phone the treatment team the same day[2]; deciding on your own to miss an infusion, to cut your own dose, or to finish the course early is not yours to decide; when you restart, and at what dose, is always the team's call.

One more line: holding today's tablet does not mean the course is over. A systematic review as far back as 2002 pointed out that adherence to oral anticancer drugs is worse than is generally assumed[5]; the missed doses I see in clinic are mostly not defiance, they are what happens when nobody made "when to stop" and "when to carry on" clear. Write down the date you stopped and why, and hand that over at your next visit.

What temperature earns a phone call: two documents, two numbers

I am not going to give you one easy number here, because two documents say different things and each has its own use. The Xeloda label's action threshold for patients is this: a temperature of 100.5 °F (about 38.1 °C) or above, or any other sign of possible infection, and you contact the team straight away[2]. The IDSA guideline updated in 2010 defines something else — the diagnosis of febrile neutropenia: a single oral temperature ≥38.3 °C, or ≥38.0 °C sustained over an hour, together with an absolute neutrophil count below 500/mm³, or a count expected to fall below 500 within 48 hours[6]. The first is the threshold you use at home; the second is the hospital's diagnostic definition. Don't merge them into one number.

There is a Taiwanese rule here that a lot of people misread. Sections 4.1.2.1 and 4.1.2.2 of the NHI drug reimbursement regulations make the condition for granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) in patients with solid tumours a white cell count that has already fallen below 1000/cumm, or a neutrophil count below 500/cumm, after chemotherapy[7]. In plain terms: in Taiwan this is secondary prevention, given after it has happened, not a routine injection before every cycle.

Before the first tablet, one enzyme is worth a question

Capecitabine and 5-FU are broken down in the body by an enzyme called DPD. In people whose activity is inadequate, the same dose accumulates into severe and even fatal toxicity, and the label names mucositis, diarrhoea, neutropenia and neurotoxicity specifically[2]. In April 2020 the European Medicines Agency recommended testing for DPD deficiency before treatment, either by measuring blood uracil concentration or by testing for gene variants[8]. The US FDA's safety labelling update of February 2026 put "test for DPYD variants before treatment unless immediate treatment is required" into the boxed warning[9]. The two point the same way; the difference is that Europe accepts a uracil measurement while the US points at genotyping.

Is it worth testing? There are numbers. An individual-patient-data meta-analysis of 8 studies and 7,365 patients found that carriers of the c.1679T>G variant had a relative risk of severe toxicity of 4.40 (95% confidence interval 2.08–9.30; a confidence interval is the range within which the estimate can reasonably swing, and here everything from two-fold to nine-fold sits inside it, which is wide), so a little over four times that of non-carriers; for DPYD*2A it was 2.85 (1.75–4.62)[10]. A prospective study in 17 Dutch hospitals and 1,103 patients then showed that genotyping first and reducing the dose according to the result brings carriers' risk back close to that of non-carriers[11]. But the label also states plainly that severe adverse reactions can still occur when no variant is found[2]. And Taiwan? A full-text search for DPYD in section 9 of the NHI's drug reimbursement regulations returns zero hits, and I found no other formal provision either[12] — confirm that one with your case manager or the pharmacy department; I am not going to tell you it is covered or that it isn't.

Each of the infused drugs has its own same-day warning

With oxaliplatin, what you watch for is cold-induced tightness in the throat, and it is a same-day call: it feels as though you cannot swallow or cannot get air in, but there is no wheeze; the label calls it pharyngolaryngeal dysaesthesia, and grade 3 to 4 runs at about 1% to 2%[13]. Numbness in the hands and feet that has reached the point of affecting buttons, handwriting or steadiness on your feet also needs saying, and the full account is in "Numb hands and feet: when to speak up".

Irinotecan has two kinds of diarrhoea and their timing is very different. A runny nose, watering eyes, more saliva, sweating, a flushed face, abdominal cramping, even a slowing heart rate, during the infusion or just as it finishes, is acute cholinergic syndrome; tell the nurse while it is happening, because it can be treated with atropine[14]. Diarrhoea that starts more than 24 hours after the dose is the delayed type; the label requires patients to keep loperamide to hand and to start treating at the first loose or unformed stool, and how to take it is something to settle when you collect the drug[14]. Diarrhoea together with fever, a distended abdomen or an inability to pass wind means going back to the hospital that day.

With the targeted drugs, the gut is not what you're watching

With bevacizumab, the thing that most needs handling the same day is abdominal pain together with fever. The label records that most gastrointestinal perforations occur within 50 days of the first dose, and that the drug is stopped permanently once one occurs[15]; a meta-analysis of 17 randomised trials and 12,294 patients calculated a perforation rate of 0.9%, with a relative risk of 3.10 in the colorectal cancer population (95% confidence interval 1.26–7.63)[16]. Three other things need saying the same day too: blood pressure that medication cannot bring down, a severe headache, a change in vision or a seizure; coughing up blood, or any severe bleeding; a surgical wound that oozes, opens up or will not heal[15]. If you have surgery scheduled, tell the oncology team yourself: the label requires the drug to be withheld for at least 28 days before elective surgery, and for at least 28 days after major surgery and until the wound has healed[15] — the two teams do not automatically know each other's schedules.

For cetuximab and panitumumab the first hurdle is the first infusion: about 90% of infusion reactions happen at that one, even with antihistamine premedication; the symptoms are rapid-onset breathlessness, wheeze, hoarseness, low blood pressure and altered consciousness, and they can also appear some hours after the infusion has finished[17]. After that, watch the skin: among 229 patients on panitumumab alone, skin toxicity accounted for 90%, grade 3 in 15%[18], and an acneiform rash that breaks down or turns purulent, a nail fold that is red, hot and painful, or blistering or peeling over a large area, is dealt with that day. And magnesium: of 98 patients with colorectal cancer receiving EGFR antibodies, 95 (97%) had a fall in blood magnesium over the course of treatment[19], and the label requires monitoring during treatment and for at least 8 weeks after it ends[17]; cramps, palpitations and tiredness you can't explain are worth saying.

One contact card, written today

Put a few things on one sheet of paper beside the pill box: the chemotherapy day unit's number and your case manager's, which number to ring at night and at weekends, and the names of the drugs you are on (write the drug names, not "chemo"). Then take your temperature once a day and note how many bowel movements you have had, and in particular whether you get up at night to go — that is one of the very things the label counts as grade 2 diarrhoea[2].

On the Taiwan side I'll only say what I could look up: capecitabine for adjuvant treatment of stage III colon cancer after surgery is explicitly provided for under the NHI, but there is a ceiling on the number of cycles[12], and that bears directly on the choice between three months and six, which I write about in "Three months or six". What you have to settle here is something else — once the drug is in your hands, which day to stop and which day to carry on is always the treatment team's decision. When you aren't sure who to ring, a 2021 announcement from the Ministry of Health and Welfare lists hospital cancer resource centres at 82 hospitals nationwide and a free phone line, 0809-010580[20]; that is 2021 data, so go by whatever your own hospital currently posts.

From the physician

Practise saying the drug names on your bag out loud, because the first thing you'll be asked on a call at midnight is what you are on. Today, copy the chemotherapy day unit's, your case manager's and the night emergency number onto one sheet of paper and stick it beside the pill box, and check there is a working thermometer in the house.
