AFTER THE DIAGNOSIS

You have two diseases: the cancer and the cirrhosis

Child-Pugh, ALBI, decompensation, competing risk — liver function is the currency of this whole topic, and the vocabulary is covered once, here.

Liver Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 8 references
#Liver Function#Staging#Decisions

In patients whose tumour has been treated successfully, early hepatic decompensation threatens survival (hazard ratio 7.5) more than early tumour recurrence does (2.5). That intuition-inverting number is the starting point for understanding every treatment decision in liver cancer.

At the first appointment, the patient's and the family's eyes are usually fixed on the same number: how many centimetres the tumour is. But when I open the report, what I look at first is the albumin, the bilirubin, whether there is ascites. Because what most liver cancer patients carry is not one disease but two — the liver cancer, and underneath it a liver that is already cirrhotic or has been inflamed for years. Every treatment decision from here on is the result of those two diseases voting together. This article explains, once, the handful of terms the whole topic will keep using, so the other nineteen articles do not have to repeat them.

My position first: stereotactic body radiotherapy and proton therapy for liver cancer are treatments I deliver myself — and much of it is self-paid. The other side of every comparison in this topic is my colleagues' surgery and ablation. That is why every comparison comes with its primary source, so you can take it to the surgical and hepatology clinics for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

Why I call liver function a "currency"

Every treatment for liver cancer pays its bill in liver function. Surgery removes a piece of liver, and what remains has to be enough to live on; ablation and embolisation injure the normal liver next to the lesion; the radiotherapy I deliver myself also puts dose into a portion of normal liver tissue; even some drug treatments require liver function above a certain level before you get through the reimbursement criteria. So "how much liver function you have left" is not a footnote on a health check — it is the currency in your pocket. With a large balance, every road described in these twenty articles stays open; with a small one, the options close off one by one. That metaphor runs through the entire topic, and its units of account are the terms that follow.

Child-Pugh: five items on a table from sixty years ago

The grading you will hear most often in clinic is Child-Pugh. A 2021 review in JHEP Reports sets out both its content and its biography: five items — albumin, bilirubin, clotting function (INR), degree of ascites, degree of hepatic encephalopathy — each scored and summed into grades A, B and C; the prognostic figures the review quotes are two-year survival of roughly 85% for grade A, roughly 60% for grade B and roughly 35% for grade C (these are literature-quoted values, not statistics from a Taiwanese population)[1]. Its origins actually have nothing to do with cancer: it was designed in 1964 to assess the risk of surgery for bleeding oesophageal varices, and in 1973 Pugh modified it into its present form[2]. Its weaknesses are just as well recognised: ascites and encephalopathy are judged by a human being, and both can be shifted by diuretics and ammonia-lowering drugs[1]. A table in which two of the five items are subjective, still in use after sixty years — that fact alone tells you how badly clinical practice needs a common language for liver function.

ALBI: two blood values that split grade A open

The ALBI grade, published in the Journal of Clinical Oncology in 2015, takes the other route: only two objective laboratory values, albumin and total bilirubin, with no human judgement anywhere. It was derived from more than 1,300 Japanese patients across all stages of liver cancer and then validated in more than 6,000 people, and it performs at least as well as Child-Pugh; more usefully, among patients who are all classed Child-Pugh A, it can separate out two groups with clearly different prognoses[3]. The 2022 version of the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer staging system (BCLC) has already changed its liver-function assessment to "has there been a decompensation event" plus finer stratification of the ALBI kind, and the original text says so plainly: decompensation — jaundice, ascites, encephalopathy — means liver function cannot be assumed preserved, whatever the Child-Pugh or MELD score works out to be[4]. Read that correctly: it is not that Child-Pugh has been discarded, but that the staging systems are moving step by step to finer tools; clinics and many reimbursement provisions still use it every day. And one sentence on MELD while we are here: a mathematical formula built from bilirubin, creatinine and INR, originally designed to predict three-month mortality in end-stage liver disease, which later became the tool for ranking patients for liver transplant[5] — transplant belongs to "Transplant: the one option that treats both diseases".

Decompensation is the watershed — and it looks like a one-way street

The word "decompensation", which keeps appearing above, means cirrhosis going from quiet to eventful: ascites, variceal bleeding, jaundice, hepatic encephalopathy — any one of those four happening is decompensation. How much does it matter? An Italian single-centre cohort study that followed patients for twenty-five years (494 people, about 80% with hepatitis C, enrolled in the 1980s) divided cirrhosis into five clinical stages, and the five-year mortality runs: compensated without varices 1.5%, with varices 10%, after a bleed 20%, after a first non-bleeding decompensation event 30%, after a second decompensation event 88%[6]. The era needs labelling: that was a time without modern antivirals, in a population dominated by hepatitis C, so the absolute numbers are pessimistic for a Taiwanese hepatitis B patient today — but the direction is very clear. Two people can both be told "you have cirrhosis" and be in completely different situations depending on which step of that staircase they stand on. This is also why, when I take a history in clinic, "have you ever had ascites drained, vomited blood, been confused" matters as much as "how many tumours".

Two diseases in a race: sometimes the liver reaches the finish line before the cancer

The statistical name for this is competing risk; in plain language, two diseases are competing for the same life, and guarding against one of them does not stop the other from running. An Italian multicentre study put a number on it: in 328 patients with hepatitis C cirrhosis and early liver cancer (BCLC 0/A) who had a complete response to curative treatment, those who developed hepatic decompensation within a year had a risk of death 7.52 times that of those who did not; those whose tumour recurred within a year, 2.5 times — a hazard ratio (HR) is the ratio between the rates at which events occur in two groups, and 7.52 against 2.50 means that in these patients whose cancer had already been treated successfully, the liver's events were deadlier than the cancer's (a hepatitis C population, from the era before all-oral antivirals)[7]. The same sentence exists in official form at guideline level: the AASLD 2023 guidance states that in patients with Child-Pugh C cirrhosis who are not on a transplant list, even surveillance for liver cancer is mostly of no benefit — because the competing risk of dying of the liver disease itself is too high[8]. Even the question "should we go looking for the cancer" has an answer that depends on the liver. That is why looking after the liver is not a side errand to treating the cancer — the role of the antivirals gets a whole article, "Hepatitis B and C: the antivirals matter more now".

At your next visit, ask about the liver before the cancer

Turn this article's vocabulary into three questions you can say out loud. First: "What is my Child-Pugh score and grade right now? And my ALBI?" Ask for a number, not a "it's all right". Second: "Have I ever had a decompensation event — ascites, a bleed, jaundice, an episode of confusion?" That question decides which step of the staircase you stand on, and whether many treatment doors are open. Third: "How much liver function is this proposed treatment expected to cost me? Once it is paid, is the remaining balance enough for the next step?" Any physician taking you seriously can answer all three; the answers themselves are the current score between your two diseases.

From the physician

Take three questions to your next visit: what are my Child-Pugh and ALBI, have I ever had a decompensation event, and how much liver function is this treatment expected to cost. The three answers are the current score between your two diseases.
