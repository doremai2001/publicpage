WHEN YOU ARE THE FAMILY

He refuses treatment, or wants to try something else

In Taiwan's bladder cancer registry, the commonest recorded reason for refusal begins "the patient or the family". So whose no is this?

Situation Guides · When You Are the Family · 9 references
#Family#How to ask#Decisions#Law & rights

"It won't help anyway" is a claim that can be checked. In the study of 1,193 stage IV patients, most did not express the understanding that chemotherapy was not at all likely to cure them. Rather than keep arguing at home, take that sentence back to clinic and ask the physician to say it again, in front of him.

Most people who reach this page have already had one round of it at home. The easily skipped fact goes first: in Taiwan's registry data, the reason written down for "refused treatment" often has more than the patient as its subject. So this is not a piece about winning the argument. It is about whose "no" this is, and where it grew from.

The registry's own wording puts the family inside the refusal

A cohort study on the Taiwan Cancer Registry linked database analysed 1,247 patients with bladder cancer diagnosed between 1 January 2014 and 30 June 2019: 2.1% refused treatment and 4.3% discontinued it. The leading reason recorded for refusal, in the registry's own words, was — "the patient or the family considered the patient's performance status poor (chronic disease or unstable systemic disease), making it hard to bear any physical discomfort that treating the disease might cause"; the leading reason for discontinuation was difficulty getting there[1].

This is not a national, all-cancer picture — one cancer, one database — and it cannot be read as "how many people in Taiwan refuse treatment".

What it does show: on the record, the family is frequently already inside that "no", not outside pulling at it. Worth separating before you open your mouth — how much of this refusal is his, and how much is what you have felt on his behalf.

He may refuse. That is the statute, not my opinion.

Patient Right to Autonomy Act (病人自主權利法) Article 4 I: "A patient has the right to be informed of his condition, the medical options, and the likely efficacy, risks and prognosis of each option. He has the right to choose and decide among the medical options the physician provides." Paragraph II follows: the patient's statutory agent, spouse, relatives, health care agent (醫療委任代理人) or a person in a particularly close relationship with the patient — together, persons with a relevant connection (關係人) — shall not obstruct the medical institution or physician acting on the patient's own decision about the medical options. Article 6 provides that surgery, and the invasive examinations or treatments specified by the central competent authority, require the consent of the patient or a person with a relevant connection and a signed consent form before they may be carried out[2] (current text amended 109-12-29, promulgated 110-01-20, verified 30 August 2026).

The reverse of "before they may be carried out" is: without consent, it cannot be done. I say this to families in clinic because it protects both sides at once. His wishes have legal effect — and getting him into the consulting room is not a legal duty of yours.

The right has a boundary, and the boundary is also in the statute

Patient Right to Autonomy Act Article 7: where a medical institution or physician encounters a patient in critical condition, then except where Article 14 I and II and the relevant provisions of the Hospice Palliative Care Act (安寧緩和醫療條例) apply, appropriate emergency care or necessary measures shall be given first, without unwarranted delay. Article 60 of the Medical Care Act (醫療法) and Article 21 of the Physicians Act (醫師法) carry the same structure of emergency duty[3].

In plain language: "he says he doesn't want any more treatment" does not mean "so there is no need to take him in". Major bleeding, difficulty breathing, altered consciousness, uncontrolled pain, or fever with chills — go in first. Withholding, terminating or withdrawing certain resuscitative measures at the terminal stage is a separate set of requirements, and they are tight; they are in "When he cannot decide, who decides".

First check whether he knows what the treatment is for

This is not suspicion. A US national prospective study interviewed 1,193 patients on chemotherapy for stage IV lung or colorectal cancer: 69% of the lung cancer patients and 81% of the colorectal cancer patients did not express the understanding that chemotherapy was not at all likely to cure their cancer. In the same study, patients who rated communication with their physician as very good were more likely to hold an inaccurate expectation (odds ratio 1.90, 95% confidence interval 1.33 to 2.72; an odds ratio is not a multiple of a probability — 1.90 can be read as a clear direction, not as "1.9 times as likely"); education, performance status and the patient's role in decision-making were all unrelated[4]. This is not somebody explaining badly; understanding leaks in transmission as a matter of course.

When he says "it won't help anyway", one possibility is that he genuinely believes it. That sentence can be taken to the next visit and put to the physician again, in front of him.

How to ask what he is afraid of

A Taiwanese medical centre analysed 14,974 patients in its cancer case management system from 2010 to 2012; 253 of them (1.69%) refused treatment, and in multivariable analysis the factors significantly associated with refusal were worry about side effects, poor performance status, and change in the disease[5]. A Q-methodological study interviewed 39 people who had refused cancer treatment and drew out eight motivating factors, among them "with my physical condition at the time the risk of treatment was too high", "my prognosis was not good enough", "I wanted to heal in a natural way", and the influence of religious and spiritual belief — plus one worth noting: "I do not distrust the medical system or its staff"[6]. Thirty-nine people, no prevalence inference; but enough to block a common misreading — his refusal is not necessarily distrust of you or of the physician.

As for how to ask, I have no Taiwanese intervention study to cite, so this is one way of putting it, not a claim of effectiveness: replace "why don't you want it" with "which is the one thing you least want to happen". Afraid of pain, of vomiting until he cannot get up, of being a drag on you, of the money, of not being himself in the last stretch — the answers are completely different, and some can be adjusted with the team (the schedule, supportive medication, doing one block and then reassessing). Until you have that one thing, everything else is force applied to air.

When he wants to try something else

First, what I will not do: run down what he wants to try on your behalf. That only becomes material for the next argument at home.

The clearest set of figures comes from the US National Cancer Database: patients using alternative medicine alongside conventional treatment (258 matched against 1,032) did not start conventional treatment any later, but refused the conventional treatments at far higher rates — radiotherapy 53.0% against 2.3%, chemotherapy 34.1% against 3.2%, surgery 7.0% against 0.1%. Once the authors put "delay in starting conventional treatment" and "refusal of conventional treatment" into the model, the hazard ratio for death was 1.39 (95% confidence interval 0.83 to 2.33), no longer significant — (a hazard ratio compares the rate at which events occur in two groups, where 1 means the same rate; a confidence interval that crosses 1 means the evidence for the difference does not hold.) In the model without those two, the same comparison is 2.08 (1.50 to 2.90). The distance between the two numbers is the authors' own conclusion: that mortality risk was mediated by refusal of conventional treatment[7]. The numerator is only 258 people, a small group defined by how a registry field was filled in.

That can be said to him unchanged, without dismissing what he wants to try: what the data point at is not "he did something else as well", it is "he therefore did not do this". On doing both, the two questions worth asking are safety (will it affect the treatment or the blood tests) and time cost (will it push the schedule out). Those answers are with the attending physician or the pharmacist, not in the discussion at home.

If he asks what happens if he doesn't

Worth answering honestly, with its limits attached. Of 55,704 patients with non-metastatic rectal adenocarcinoma in the US National Cancer Database, 97.4% had curative surgery and 2.6% refused; five-year overall survival was 61.6% against 35.7%, and after propensity matching median survival was 84.2 months against 43.7 months[8]. The same paper lists what else differed: those who refused surgery were older to begin with, more often on non-private insurance, with larger tumours. So this is association, not causation — how much of the gap comes from the refusal and how much from the two groups already being different, this study cannot separate.

A systematic review in colorectal cancer shows the refusal rate varying a great deal with what is refused: surgery 0.25% to 3.26%, chemotherapy 7.8% to 41.5%[9]. "Not doing it" is not a switch; there are many steps in between, and what he is refusing may be only one of them.

What can be handed over

If his judgement lately looks clearly unlike him — not knowing the time or the place, contradicting himself, a change in temperament — raise it with the medical team directly. It falls within medical assessment and is not yours to judge. Most hospitals have cancer case managers and a medical social work office; I could not find a national document defining what they cover, so what they can take on has to be asked at your own hospital (verified 30 August 2026).

One last thing I will not decide for you: where this line stops. In law he has the right to refuse, and you have the chance to put the information in front of him; both hold. You can ask what he is afraid of, ask the team to adjust the plan, ask for the goal of treatment to be explained again. Then it is his decision. And accepting it does not mean stopping here: how symptoms are controlled, how his strength is kept up, whether to do one block and then reassess — all of that can still be discussed with the team, and none of it needs him to agree to treatment first. His own mood and sleep are in "The kind of sleeplessness that won't lift"; yours are in "The carer breaks first".

From the physician

He has the right to refuse; it is Article 4 of the Patient Right to Autonomy Act. But "he says he doesn't want any more treatment" does not mean "so there is no need to take him in". Major bleeding, difficulty breathing, altered consciousness, uncontrolled pain or fever with chills — go in first. The emergency duty is Article 7 of the same Act and Article 60 of the Medical Care Act.
