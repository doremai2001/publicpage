TWO OTHER DISEASES, AND TAIWAN

How this goes in Taiwan

Every one of these things has a code and a point value in the insurance tables; what actually holds you up is usually not whether it is covered, but the prior review.

Thyroid Cancer Guide · Two other diseases, and Taiwan · 9 references
#NHI Coverage#Regulation & Coverage#Costs & Self-Pay#Tumour Board

"Does the insurance pay for it" has never been a yes-or-no question. A procedure item and a drug payment price are two different things, and the same drug can point at one histological type in an international trial and at another in the Taiwanese reimbursement text.

The other nineteen articles are about the disease. This one is about the system: which clinics you get sent to, which items exist in the National Health Insurance tables, and what I went looking for and could not find.

I transcribe what the text says. I do not infer from the text how much of it actually happens here, or how quickly. The answer about that one injection is here, and so are the drug indications, word for word.

Which specialties you will meet

Under Article 8 of the 專科醫師分科及甄審辦法, the Regulations Governing Specialties and Board Certification of Physicians, Taiwan recognises twenty-five statutory medical specialties, and among them there is no 內分泌科 (endocrinology) and no 一般外科 (general surgery)[1]. Those two are subspecialties, run by their own societies. The statutory specialties that bear directly on thyroid cancer read, in the regulation's own words: 「二、內科。」internal medicine; 「三、外科。」surgery; 「九、耳鼻喉頭頸外科。」otorhinolaryngology and head and neck surgery; 「十七、放射腫瘤科。」radiation oncology; 「十八、解剖病理科。」anatomical pathology; 「二十、核子醫學科。」nuclear medicine.

Mapped onto the people you will meet: internal medicine, which you will hear called the endocrinology and metabolism clinic, handles the blood tests, the tablet, the follow-up number, and thyroid hormone and calcium after surgery; nuclear medicine handles radioiodine (I-131) and the scans; radiation oncology handles external beam radiotherapy; anatomical pathology reads the fine-needle and surgical specimens. Whether thyroid operations are done by surgery or by otorhinolaryngology and head and neck surgery is arranged differently from hospital to hospital, cannot be looked up in the text, and is a question for the hospital you attend.

In the tables, these things have names and codes

The currently effective 診療項目, procedure items, bearing on thyroid cancer, with code, name and payment points copied from the source[2]: 19012C 頭頸部軟組織超音波, head and neck soft tissue ultrasound, 610 points; 15007C 穿刺細胞檢查, needle aspiration cytology, 667; 19007C 超音波導引, ultrasound guidance for needle placement, 1,500; 09111C 甲狀腺球蛋白, thyroglobulin, 90; 12068C 甲狀腺球蛋白抗體, anti-thyroglobulin antibodies, 200; 09112C 甲狀腺刺激素免疫分析, TSH immunoassay, 240; 09115B 降血鈣素免疫分析, calcitonin immunoassay, 240; 26038B 碘-131治療 每1 mci, I-131 treatment per 1 mCi, 478; 26011B 碘-131頸及胸部掃描, I-131 neck and chest scan, 1,766; 26012B 碘-131癌症追蹤檢查, I-131 cancer work-up, 2,693; 26074C 碘-131癌症追蹤檢查-施打Thyrogen, 19,475; 26072B 正子造影-全身, whole-body positron emission tomography, 36,500.

Two things to be clear about. First, the points are not the money you pay. They are the unit in which the insurer reimburses the hospital, which is a separate matter from your co-payment. Second, searching the tables for 全身掃描, whole-body scan, returns nothing; there is no standalone item under that name.

The remarks column is more use than the item name

The remarks on 15007C 穿刺細胞檢查 state that for institutions in the primary care sector the indications are 「(1)甲狀腺結節或囊腫之鑑別良/惡性。(2)甲狀腺癌超音波陰影經治療後不消失。」 — telling benign from malignant in a thyroid nodule or cyst, and a thyroid cancer shadow on ultrasound that does not disappear after treatment. That is part of why a nodule does not automatically get a needle (〈A scan found a nodule. Now what?〉). The indication on 12068C 甲狀腺球蛋白抗體 reads 「用於甲狀腺低下(自體免疫型)及甲狀腺癌病人」 — for hypothyroid (autoimmune type) and thyroid cancer patients, which explains why follow-up draws that number and the antibody together. The remarks on 26072B 正子造影 list thyroid cancer among the indications for staging and suspected recurrence, and carry restrictions including 「不得用於例行之追蹤檢查」, not for routine follow-up[2].

That injection: the procedure item and the drug are two things

〈Getting through those few days〉 left this one to me. The injection that spares you coming off the tablet — does Taiwan have it?

Two layers. First, the fee schedule holds 26074C 「碘-131癌症追蹤檢查-施打Thyrogen」, an I-131 cancer work-up with a Thyrogen injection, at 19,475 points, and its remarks read, word for word, 「1.適應症：(1)甲狀腺癌復發或轉移之患者。(2)不適合停用T4之甲狀腺癌患者進行檢查或治療之準備。2.本項須個案申請事前審查。」 — indications: patients with recurrent or metastatic thyroid cancer, and preparation for investigation or treatment in thyroid cancer patients for whom stopping T4 is unsuitable; and, this item requires a case-by-case application for prior review[2].

Second layer: the drug side is another matter. Searched by ingredient name, 適諾進凍晶注射劑 1.1 毫克, recombinant human TSH 1.1 mg, is listed, but its payment price has been 0.00 since 1 April 2005[3].

Which is why I do not write "the National Health Insurance covers this injection". A procedure item and a drug payment price are two different things, and the name of 26074C is 檢查, an investigation. Add that line about case-by-case prior review, and whether it comes round to you is a question for nuclear medicine or your case manager.

The drugs: four rules, one shared shape

Four sections of the 藥品給付規定, the drug reimbursement rules, bear on thyroid cancer. I transcribe the indication sentences, because this is exactly where things get written back to front.

9.86. Vandetanib: 「1.適用於無法進行手術切除的局部侵犯或轉移性甲狀腺髓質癌，並且為症狀性及疾病侵襲性的患者。」 — for patients with unresectable locally invasive or metastatic medullary thyroid carcinoma who are symptomatic and have aggressive disease[4]. The thyroid cancer paragraph of 9.74. Cabozantinib, effective 1 August 2025, reads 「適用於治療成人及12歲以上兒童曾接受 VEGFR 標靶治療後惡化、放射碘治療無效或不適用放射碘治療的局部晚期或轉移性分化型甲狀腺癌病人。」 — for adults and children aged 12 and over with locally advanced or metastatic differentiated thyroid cancer that has progressed after VEGFR-targeted therapy and is refractory to, or unsuitable for, radioiodine[5].

Read those two lines again. Internationally, the phase III trial that put cabozantinib where it stands was a medullary carcinoma trial. But in the Taiwanese reimbursement text, cabozantinib's thyroid indication is the differentiated type, and the one that names medullary carcinoma is vandetanib. A trial is a trial; a reimbursement clause is a reimbursement clause. I deliberately kept this passage out of 〈Medullary cancer is a different disease〉 for exactly that reason.

The other two are 9.63. Lenvatinib and 9.34. Sorafenib. Both indications read 「放射性碘治療無效之局部晚期或轉移性的進行性(progressive)分化型甲狀腺癌」 — progressive, locally advanced or metastatic differentiated thyroid cancer refractory to radioiodine — and both state in the text that the two are not to be used together[6].

The shape the four share is more use than any sentence beginning "the insurance pays for it": all need approval on prior review before use, all are granted one course at a time (three months for three of them, six for vandetanib), all need imaging submitted, and all are reassessed when the course ends. But "stop on progression" is written into two of the four, not four: the vandetanib clause reads 「出現疾病惡化或無法忍受之藥物不良反應，應立即停用」, stop immediately on disease progression or intolerable adverse reactions[4], and the cabozantinib clause reads 「無疾病惡化方可繼續使用」, may be continued only in the absence of disease progression[5]; the thyroid sections of the lenvatinib and sorafenib rules, as I transcribed them, carry no such sentence[6]. So what really decides whether you can have one is often not whether it is covered, but whether the paperwork can be assembled and whether the timing holds.

Genetic testing: what is covered is the tumour, not inheritance

Since May 2024 the insurance has run next-generation sequencing for solid tumours: 30301B, which is the BRCA1/2 test, at 10,000 points, the small panel 30302B (100 genes or fewer) at 20,000, and the large panel 30303B (more than 100 genes) at 30,000[7]. Two rows of the appendix concern the thyroid, and both may claim only the small or the large panel, not 30301B. The row 「甲狀腺癌(不包含髓質癌)」, thyroid cancer excluding medullary carcinoma, has as its indications papillary and follicular thyroid cancer refractory to I-131 and to antiangiogenic targeted drugs, or anaplastic thyroid cancer assessed by a multidisciplinary team as unable to undergo curative surgery, with BRAF V600E to be performed first. The row 「甲狀腺髓質癌」, medullary thyroid carcinoma, is limited to those assessed by a multidisciplinary team as unable to undergo curative surgery, and must include RET mutation.

One sentence in the rules has to be read exactly: 「除 Germline BRCA1/2 基因檢測使用血液檢體外，其他檢測限使用已確診之腫瘤病理組織」 — apart from germline BRCA1/2 testing, which uses a blood sample, the other tests are limited to pathological tissue from a confirmed tumour[7]. Which is to say: what this item tests is somatic variation inside tumour tissue, not a germline test drawn from blood. Different specimen, different purpose — one is for choosing a drug, the other for judging what may pass to the next generation. Germline testing I found no entry for, in either the fee schedule or the drug reimbursement rules.

The difference between "no entry found" and "not there"

A channel that works and returns nothing, and a channel that will not connect, are two different things.

  • Genuinely nothing: searched by ingredient name, selpercatinib and pralsetinib each return 0 NHI drug items, while the same query returns 10 for lenvatinib and 6 for vandetanib, which shows the channel is working[3]. That only means no item is listed. It does not mean the drug does not exist in Taiwan.
  • Item found, text not found: dabrafenib and trametinib have items and payment prices, but the reimbursement-rules field is empty, and three routes failed to obtain the full text[3]. Their reimbursement conditions for anaplastic carcinoma I write as no entry found.

Two more are the same kind of nothing. Taiwan's official cancer registry report carries no stage distribution for thyroid cancer — its stage statistics cover only the seventeen cancers reported on the long form, and the thyroid is not among them[8]; Taiwanese survival and the inherited proportion for medullary and anaplastic carcinoma are likewise not listed. Whether these drugs hold Taiwanese marketing approval, I could not reach the Food and Drug Administration's query system at all — the channel was unavailable, which is not the same as zero results, and I infer nothing in either direction.

How many people, the sex ratio, and screening

From the 2023 cancer registry report: 6,045 people were newly diagnosed with a thyroid malignancy that year (1,507 men, 4,538 women), against 1,228 in 1999; 225 died in the same year, at an age-standardised mortality rate of 0.39 per 100,000 in men and 0.50 in women (2000 world standard population), and thyroid cancer ranked 23rd among cancer causes of death for both sexes[8].

The sex ratio comes two ways, and the report gives both. On crude case counts it is about 1 to 3.01. On age-standardised rates the incidence ratio is 0.34 male to 1 female, while the mortality ratio is 0.78 male to 1 female[8]. Far more women are diagnosed than men, and yet the gap in deaths is much smaller. What lies behind it is the subject of 〈Why there is suddenly so much thyroid cancer〉.

One thing widely misunderstood: Taiwan's publicly funded cancer screening covers breast, cervical, colorectal, oral and lung cancer, with a separate gastric cancer programme, and thyroid cancer is not in it[9]. So the lump in your neck was not found by a screening programme. How it did get found has no national statistics to look up. This cannot be stretched into "so you should not have an ultrasound". It only says where the thing sits within the system.

What to ask the medical affairs office and your case manager

These are more use read out than looked up:

  • "Which clinic follows me from here? When would I need to go back to the others?"
  • "Is there a matching insurance item for this test? If it needs prior review, roughly how long?"
  • "If a particular drug is to be used, what has to be submitted, and how often is it reassessed?"
  • "Is the genetic test I am having done on tumour tissue or on blood? The two have different purposes."
  • "How do I find the medical affairs office or my case manager? I have a coverage question."

And one action: circle every "no entry found" in this article, take it into clinic, and for each one you circled ask that one — "can this be looked up at this hospital? Who do I ask?"

From the physician

Copy these into clinic: "Which clinic follows me from here?" "Is there a matching insurance item for this test, and if it needs prior review, roughly how long?" "Is the genetic test I am having done on tumour tissue or on blood?" Everywhere this article says "no entry found" is a place to ask the hospital's medical affairs office or your case manager.
