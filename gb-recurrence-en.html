After recurrence, what roads remain

Re-operation, re-irradiation, bevacizumab, clinical trials, and when to talk about palliative care: each road written up with what it improves, what it does not, and what it costs.

Glioblastoma Guide · Afterwards · 17 references
#Recurrence#Decisions#Clinical Trials#Quality of Life

Take the same re-operation data and change the statistical method, and a hazard ratio of 0.66 turns round to point the other way. This article also covers why the phase III trial of early palliative care in brain tumours was negative, and who that six-month line is protecting.

Two sentences get said most often after a recurrence. One is "there are still plenty of roads to take", the other is "nothing works any more". Neither is accurate. The guideline puts it bluntly: there is no clearly defined standard of care for recurrent glioblastoma, and which road gets chosen depends on what treatment has already been given, on age, on performance status, on MGMT status and on the pattern of progression[1]. This article lays each road out.

What is actually doing the deciding

I am not going to hand you a table of "do this first, then that" — that table does not exist, practice differs between hospitals, and it has to be decided at a multidisciplinary meeting on your particular situation. What is actually doing the deciding is four things: where the recurrence is and how far it extends (well demarcated, or diffuse), how long it has been since the last treatment, performance status, and the dose distribution of the previous course of radiotherapy[1].

The things that cannot wait go first. One-sided weakness coming on over minutes to hours, speech suddenly slurred or gone, a change in consciousness, one pupil bigger than the other, a sudden severe headache with vomiting, a seizure — that tier means calling an ambulance. One leg swelling up, sudden breathlessness or chest pain means contacting the team today and leaving the house now: this disease is a high-risk group for deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism. Neither tier waits for the date in your diary, and the list is in "When to come back the same day: three tiers".

That six-month line exists so that nobody takes an operation for nothing

The guideline is specific about re-operation: roughly 20% to 30% of patients are candidates, typically symptomatic recurrence that is well demarcated and no earlier than six months from the first operation. The next sentence matters more — operating earlier than six months increases the risk of an unnecessary intervention prompted by pseudoprogression[1].

You can see it happening in real data: in the 284-patient Norwegian cohort, the two patients with pseudoprogression whose treatment was changed on the strength of imaging both had a second operation, and the pathology showed only reactive tissue and necrosis[2]. The reading rules are in "How the follow-up scans are scheduled, and why bigger is not always worse".

"A second operation buys another six months" — the same data, counted another way, and it is gone

A meta-analysis of 21 studies and 8,630 patients did an honest thing. Treating re-operation as a fixed group label, the hazard ratio for overall survival came out at 0.66 (95% confidence interval 0.61 to 0.71) — a hazard ratio divides the rate at which deaths occur in one group by the rate in the other, so below 1 means they were occurring more slowly, and the range in the bracket is where the estimate plausibly sits, not your personal probability — which looks like a substantial benefit. Recast as a time-dependent event — that is, admitting that a person has to survive to that day before he can be operated on a second time — the benefit does not merely disappear, it reverses direction (hazard ratio 2.19, 1.47 to 3.27). The authors concede it themselves: the survival benefit of re-operation may be overestimated when fixed-covariate analysis is used[3].

An updated meta-analysis in 2025 of 36 studies and 10,738 patients reports mean overall survival of 19.66 months in the re-operation group against 12.56 months for chemotherapy with or without radiotherapy. But all of it is observational data with heavy selection bias — the people who get picked for an operation were in better shape to begin with; and that paper's closing sentence is that the role of re-operation in recurrent glioblastoma remains uncertain[4]. This is the same trap as "scan more often and you live longer" in the follow-up article, wearing a different face.

Re-irradiation: progression was pushed back, survival did not move

There is exactly one prospective randomised trial evaluating re-irradiation at recurrence with modern technique. RTOG 1205 randomised 170 eligible patients to re-irradiation of 35 Gy in 10 fractions with concurrent bevacizumab, or bevacizumab alone: the primary endpoint of overall survival was not improved (10.1 against 9.7 months, hazard ratio 0.98, P=0.46); progression-free survival went from 3.8 months to 7.1 months and the six-month progression-free rate from 29.1% to 54.3% (P=0.001); acute grade 3 or higher treatment-related adverse events were 5%, with no delayed high-grade toxicity[5].

The guideline gives one principle you can take into clinic: the fractionation depends on the size of the tumour, and the larger the lesion the lower the dose per fraction has to be; the total dose figures are planning parameters, not something to assess yourself against[1]. The general logic and the dose discipline are on this site in "Can somewhere already irradiated be treated again?"

Nearly 30% of scans can improve, and nobody lives longer for it

EORTC 26101 randomised 437 patients at first progression to lomustine plus bevacizumab or lomustine alone. The primary endpoint of overall survival was negative: 9.1 against 8.6 months, hazard ratio 0.95, P=0.65; progression-free survival went from 1.5 to 4.2 months, at the price of grade 3 to 5 adverse events rising from 38.1% to 63.6%, and with no improvement in either quality of life or neurocognition[6]. Nor is a response rate survival: in the non-comparative phase II trial BRAIN, bevacizumab alone had a response rate of 28.2%[7].

The guideline's positioning of it, quoted: its main value in routine clinical practice is temporary symptom control, and as a steroid-sparing option in patients with large, symptomatic tumours; the same document also states that its use has not been shown to confer an overall survival benefit[1]. "Steroid-sparing" has never been measured as a primary endpoint in a randomised trial — BRAIN reported only a trend towards stable or falling dose[7].

The Taiwanese provision here is clear. Clause 9.37, paragraph 2 of the NHI drug reimbursement regulations restricts it — this is my rendering of the clause — to use as a single agent, in adults with recurrent disease who have had standard radiotherapy and failed chemotherapy containing temozolomide, subject to prior approval, with each application limited to 12 weeks, and a further application requiring objective evidence such as imaging showing there has been no progression[8]. Radiation necrosis is not among the indications grouped in the clause; whether an application can be made is a question for the medical affairs office.

lomustine is the standard, and the reason is that it keeps being everyone else's control arm

The guideline's own wording is that lomustine has never been shown superior to another agent in a randomised trial, but that it is increasingly regarded as the most appropriate standard of care, on the basis of its role as the control arm in several randomised trials, with a six-month progression-free rate of about 20%[1]. Temozolomide rechallenge probably has activity only in those with MGMT methylation, and there is no randomised trial supporting a dose-intensified schedule over the standard dose either[1].

Tumour-treating fields in the recurrent box is negative: in a randomised trial of 237 patients, median survival was 6.6 against 6.0 months (P=0.27), with fewer serious adverse events than chemotherapy (6% against 16%)[9]; the rest is in "Fields, immunotherapy, vaccines, protons, BNCT: where each one stands". Two more things can be checked on the Taiwanese side: clause 9.35 restricts carmustine implants to surgical adjunctive use in recurrent glioblastoma and prohibits combination with temozolomide; and lomustine currently has four NHI-listed products, but the reimbursement-provision column for them is blank — a blank field means this document has no clause of its own for it. It does not mean it is covered without applying, and it does not mean it is not covered[8]. Whether it can actually be used, and how it is claimed, is a question for your doctor and the medical affairs office.

The clinical trial box: look at its control arm first

I am not going to write the regorafenib story out again: a handsome phase II trial that a phase III trial later overturned, and the sequence is set out in "Fields, immunotherapy, vaccines, protons, BNCT: where each one stands". In the recurrence box all you need is the conclusion — it was not superior to lomustine, and it was more toxic[10]. The Taiwanese side matches up: the indications in clause 9.51 of the NHI drug reimbursement regulations and on the Taiwan FDA licence are metastatic colorectal cancer, gastrointestinal stromal tumour and hepatocellular carcinoma only — no glioblastoma[8][11].

That phase III trial used a Bayesian adaptive platform design, which teaches one thing in passing: a platform trial has a control arm, so joining a trial does not mean you are certain to get the new drug[12]; how to find one and how to read it is on this site in "Clinical trials: how to find one, how to read it".

Talking about palliative care earlier: where the reason actually lies

I am not going to use another cancer's "early palliative care extends survival" finding to persuade you, because the brain tumour phase III trial does not read that way. EPCOG randomised 217 patients across six German university medical centres to a 12-month intervention: the primary endpoint, quality of life at six months, did not reach statistical significance (mean difference 4.1, 95% confidence interval −4.4 to 12.6, P=0.34). The authors ran a further analysis adjusted for time of death to obtain a significant result, and the reason adjustment was needed is that survival differed significantly between the arms, and it was the control arm that was longer (P=0.018); palliative care problems and emotional function improved, and caregivers gained nothing. The authors' sentence is that what improved was how people live, not how long[13].

So why refer earlier anyway? The reason has nothing to do with survival: the life-limiting nature of a brain tumour, together with the symptoms that come from neurological decline, calls for an adequate and early palliative care approach[14]. A 2023 updated review says early palliative care intervention is essential for defining goals of care in good time and keeping symptom burden down, and says honestly that no drug has been shown in a randomised trial to improve fatigue or neurocognition significantly[15]. The systematic review supplies the joint: as the disease progresses, cognitive deficits worsen, and communication and decision-making become difficult[16]. How early the documents need discussing is in "Memory, concentration, and changes in who he is".

In a Korean glioblastoma dataset of 205 people, the median time from first palliative care consultation to death was only 44 days, and those who had a consultation received less aggressive end-of-life care[17]. What happens in the last few weeks is already written on this site, in "The last few weeks: what actually happens".

Every figure in months above is a comparison between arms of a trial, not a deadline handed to you; how to read survival numbers is in "That number, and how I am going to tell you about it". I am not putting these in rank order, because the rank order does not exist. What you can take into the multidisciplinary meeting is three sentences: what does the site and extent of this recurrence look like? How long is it since my last treatment? And which options have those two things left on the table?

From the physician

I am not putting these in rank order, because the rank order does not exist. Four things decide it: where the recurrence is and how far it extends, how long it has been since the last treatment, performance status, and the dose distribution of the last course of radiotherapy — taking those four into the multidisciplinary meeting is more use than asking whether there is another drug. During a recurrence, one leg swelling up or sudden breathlessness means coming back the same day.
