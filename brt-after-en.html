After it ends: recovery and the long run

Pigmentation, fibrosis and telangiectasia are three different long-term changes; there are randomised ten-year figures for appearance, and the words that matter in a mammogram report are stable and changed again.

Breast Radiotherapy Guide · Treatment and Self-Care · 8 references
#Follow-up#Late Effects#Survivorship#Imaging

Two questions in this article have no answer I could find, and I have left them blank on purpose: how many months until the colour fades, and how many years until the changes on the film settle. What can be found is more useful — the appearance figures at ten years, and the two words a radiologist is actually looking for on your mammogram.

On the day your course ends you get a slip with your next appointment on it. The slip has a date; what it does not have is everything after that — when the colour on that patch of skin will fade, whether it is normal for it to feel firmer, whether a mammogram will still read properly from now on. Those questions usually get squeezed into the last minute of a consultation, when in fact they are the part you will be living alongside for years. This article is about what comes after: the short-term tail end, the long-term changes, and what an irradiated breast looks like on a film.

My position first: every technique compared in this topic — ordinary intensity-modulated radiotherapy, TOMO, deep inspiration breath-hold — has a self-paid component among the more expensive options, and all of them are delivered in my own department; protons mean a referral elsewhere. So every "better" here comes with its primary source and the level of evidence behind it, and on cost I cite the official schedule where one exists and tell you to ask the medical affairs office where it does not. Concluding that the basic option is enough for you is a completely reasonable conclusion.

The two weeks after it ends are the high point of the acute reaction

First, join up the timeline. The peak of the skin reaction falls about 2 weeks after treatment ends, not on the day of the last fraction[1], and it then settles gradually over several weeks[2]; medically, anything within 90 days of irradiation counts as the acute phase[3]. That is why most hospitals put the first post-treatment appointment somewhere near this high point to look at the skin: not a formality, a sensible moment. The full set of care principles for during the course and just after is in "Skin reactions: when they come, how to care for them" and I am not repeating it here; but the dividing line needs saying once more — broken skin and weeping, or fever, odour from the wound, pus, or pain and swelling spreading beyond the treated area, are not things that wait for the next appointment[2].

The colour does fade, but it does not run on a calendar

Once the redness has gone, what usually stays is a patch darker than the skin around it. Pigment change is a known consequence of radiotherapy: both hyperpigmentation and loss of pigment are on the list of chronic skin changes[3]. As for how many months it takes to fade — that is a question I could find no citable answer to in the literature, so I am not giving you a number of months. What I would rather give you is the shape: colour changes usually move more slowly than the broken skin does, and most fade gradually with time. If it bothers you, raise it at an appointment rather than trying folk remedies or whitening products on your own.

One thing often overlooked at this stage: healed skin is more sensitive to sunlight, and the guideline recommends using a sunscreen of SPF 30 or above on the irradiated area once it has healed[2]. There is no expiry date on that one; it is every summer from now on.

Firmer to the touch: fibrosis, and a twenty-year account

The long-term changes are mainly three: fibrosis (tissue becoming firm and losing its give), telangiectasia (fine red vessels visible at the skin surface), and the pigment change just described[3]. Fibrosis is something you can feel and see, and it does not go back to how it was, so its probability has to be given honestly.

One decision shifts it noticeably: a boost to the original tumour bed. The EORTC randomised trial in Europe followed patients to 20 years, and severe fibrosis was 5.2% in the boost arm against 1.8% without it; in the same dataset, ipsilateral breast recurrence was 12.0% against 16.4%, and there was no difference in overall survival between the arms[4]. Those two sets of numbers have to be read together: what the boost buys is a few percentage points off the recurrence rate, and what it costs is severe fibrosis going from under 2% to just over 5%. That is a trade-off you are entitled to know about and entitled to take part in — if your doctor recommended for or against a boost, ask them face to face what the reason was.

Appearance at ten years: today's fractionation is gentler than the old standard

Hypofractionation (the same total dose delivered in fewer, larger fractions) is usually described as more convenient, but there is ten-year randomised evidence on the appearance end too. The UK START trial followed patients to ten years: in the arm given 40 Gy in 15 fractions, breast shrinkage, telangiectasia and breast oedema were all significantly less frequent than in the old-standard arm of 50 Gy in 25 fractions[5] (the absolute percentages are not in the abstract available to me, so I am only giving the direction). Shorter schedules have read out at ten years as well: in the FAST-Forward trial, moderate or marked change in the appearance of the breast or chest wall at ten years was 13.1% with 40 Gy in 15 fractions, 19.3% with 27 Gy in 5 fractions and 14.4% with 26 Gy in 5 fractions[6] — the one-week 26 Gy schedule is comparable to the three-week schedule on appearance, while the 27 Gy arm is clearly higher. To be labelled properly: these endpoints are clinician-assessed appearance changes, which do not track a patient's own perception exactly. How the various schedules compare on controlling the tumour, and why you were put on the number of fractions you were, is a separate decision, and that is in "Why am I not on the shorter course?".

An irradiated breast is supposed to look different on the film

This section is about the breast after conserving surgery; anyone who has had a mastectomy with reconstruction has no target for mammography and is followed differently, so check that individually with your team. After breast-conserving surgery and radiotherapy, a set of expected changes appears on the mammogram: breast oedema, skin thickening, post-surgical fluid collections (haematoma or seroma), architectural distortion, calcifications. The radiology literature on reading these emphasises that the changes have a characteristic sequence of evolution and move towards stabilisation, and that knowing this normal sequence reduces unnecessary recalls while preserving the ability to detect recurrence early[7]. So a report saying "post-surgical and post-radiotherapy change" is not bad news; it is the expected description. How many years until it is fully stable? The abstract gives no number of years, and I am not inventing one either.

The words that matter in the report are "stable" and "changed again"

What warrants concern is not the change itself but change that starts again after things had stabilised: increasing asymmetry, a mass getting larger, oedema or skin thickening increasing again, pleomorphic calcifications appearing near the surgical site — these call for further investigation to rule out recurrence, and where mammography and ultrasound cannot settle it, MRI is the problem-solving tool[8].

There is a very practical corollary here: deciding whether something "changed again" depends on comparing the new film with the old ones. So going back to the same hospital for follow-up, or at least making sure the old films are available alongside each new study, is itself part of the care. Changing hospitals is not a problem, but take the disc of your previous imaging with you.

For the years ahead, settle two things first

How often to come back, whether a mammogram is annual or less frequent, how long after radiotherapy the first one should be — I am not citing those scheduling numbers again here. The complete follow-up rhythm is in "Does follow-up need a yearly whole-body scan?", and that article plus what your own consultant has arranged are what to go by. What is worth flagging is that the Health Promotion Administration's two-yearly screening for women in general is about people who do not have the disease, not about you after treatment. The other reason for long-term follow-up — second cancers and their relationship with smoking — is in "Beyond the skin: fatigue, lungs, lymphoedema".

Before you leave the clinic, settle two things. First, when the next appointment and the next imaging are, and who is booking them — put it in your phone's calendar rather than relying on memory. Second, set your own trigger condition: if the treated side develops a new lump, if the skin thickens or reddens again, if there is nipple discharge, or if an appearance that had been stable starts changing again, you do not wait for the date in the calendar — you book an appointment. Both of those can be finished today, and neither needs anybody's permission.

From the physician

The colour fades gradually and the firmness may stay; I am not giving you a number of months for either, because the literature does not have one. What to remember are the words that decide the reading: stable, and changed again after being stable. Put the dates of appointments and imaging in your calendar, and take your old films with you if you change hospital.
