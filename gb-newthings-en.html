WHAT THE EVIDENCE SAYS

Fields, immunotherapy, vaccines, protons, BNCT: where each one stands

Five new treatments measured with one ruler: what design, which people were enrolled, was the primary endpoint met, and what does it cost. Negative results are written as negative.

Glioblastoma Guide · What the Evidence Says · 23 references
#Clinical Trials#Evidence#Proton Therapy#Costs & Self-Pay

Of the three phase III immunotherapy trials, one was not merely no better but statistically significantly worse. This article opens up the design gap in each item, and says exactly how far the Taiwanese NHI codes, the regulatory status and the availability could each be traced.

"Is there anything new, anything better?" I am asked this every week, and whoever asks has usually already collected three or four names. I measure them with the same ruler: what design, which people were enrolled, was the primary endpoint met, what does it cost. Some cells give an answer nobody likes, but writing a negative result up as positive is what harms people.

My position first: the radiotherapy part of glioblastoma treatment is the part I do myself; the other side of every comparison in this topic is my neurosurgical colleagues' operation and my neuro-oncology colleagues' drugs. Tumour-treating fields I do not use, and they are not a service of my department. So every comparison comes with its primary source, so that you can take it to the surgical and neuro-oncology clinics for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

Tumour-treating fields: one positive phase III, and who it enrolled

EF-14 is one of the few positive results here. JAMA, 2017: randomised, open-label (no sham device as a control), 695 people. What matters most is who it enrolled: people who had already completed concurrent chemoradiotherapy, a median of 3.8 months from diagnosis to randomisation[1] — that is, those who had come through the first six weeks without the tumour progressing immediately. Progression-free survival 6.7 against 4.0 months, overall survival 20.9 against 16.0, hazard ratio 0.63 for both (95% confidence interval on the overall survival one, 0.53 to 0.76)[1] — a hazard ratio compares how fast events happen, so 0.63 means events occurred more slowly in the group using the fields, not "the probability becomes 0.63 times as big"; the confidence interval in the bracket is the plausible range for that estimate, not your personal probability. The price is mild-to-moderate skin toxicity under the arrays, 52% against 0%[1]. How to read numbers like these is in "That number, and how I am going to tell you about it".

"You have to wear it 18 hours a day" was later made into a subgroup analysis: overall survival hazard ratio 0.78 for compliance above 75% against below, P=0.031[2]. But the word the authors put in their title is "prognostic", not predictive — this is a post hoc analysis, grouped by a variable the patient himself partly controls, and people who can manage the hours were in better shape to start with. Both sentences have to be said: people who wore it longer did live longer, but that is not all the machine's doing; and the reverse matters as much — people who could not make the hours on some days did not thereby harm themselves, and that should not become pressure at home. Whether to start and whether to continue are discussed with your doctor.

EANO 2021's exact words: its use in maintenance remains controversial, despite one positive phase III trial[3]. One pooled-analysis comment belongs alongside: the Cochrane network meta-analysis for older patients could not fit EF-14 into its network at all, because randomisation happened only after chemoradiotherapy; its verdict is neutral — in this selected population this treatment may improve overall survival, but quality-of-life and tolerability data in older patients are not yet sufficient[4]. TRIDENT/EF-32 (981 people), which moves the fields forward to run concurrently with radiotherapy, has completed and results are not yet posted[5] — a line you can raise in clinic. The physics is on this site in "Electric fields on the scalp: what they can do".

Immunotherapy: three phase III trials, one pointing the wrong way

CheckMate-498 enrolled newly diagnosed, MGMT-unmethylated patients, 560 of them, and swapped temozolomide for nivolumab (not added to it, in place of it): 13.4 against 14.9 months, hazard ratio 1.31, P=0.0037, the immunotherapy arm significantly worse[6]. CheckMate-548 enrolled methylated or indeterminate patients, 716 of them, this time adding the drug on, with a placebo control: 28.9 against 32.1 months, hazard ratio 1.1, no improvement; and grade 3 or 4 treatment-related adverse events rose from 33.6% to 52.4%[7]. In recurrent disease, CheckMate-143 (369 people) used bevacizumab as comparator: 9.8 against 10.0 months, hazard ratio 1.04; objective response rate 7.8% against 23.1%[8].

Be careful with that last one: the comparator was not a placebo, and bevacizumab itself has never been compared against placebo in recurrent glioblastoma[3] — that is called drawing level with a drug that has not been shown to extend survival. Three phase III trials, and of the three, not one met its primary endpoint.

Vaccines: the tightest design came out flat; the one reporting a gap changed its endpoint

ACT IV's design is close to faultless: randomised, double-blind, 165 hospitals in 22 countries, 745 people, enrolling only those centrally confirmed to express EGFRvIII. The result is flat: 20.1 against 20.0 months, hazard ratio 1.01, stopped early for futility[9].

DCVax-L is often held up as the hopeful example: 19.3 months in the newly diagnosed group against 16.5 in an external control, hazard ratio 0.80[10]. But its own subtitle says "externally controlled, non-randomised", and the peer-reviewed criticism lists four things: the primary endpoint was originally progression-free survival and was later changed to overall survival; the design allowed crossover after recurrence, so that about 90% of the two groups combined ended up receiving that vaccine; the control arm was assembled after the fact from other randomised trials' control arms; and the progression-free survival finally published ran the other way — 6.2 against 7.6 months, P=0.47[11]. Endpoint changed midstream, comparator swapped, 90% of both groups ending up on the drug: a survival gap reported out of that cannot be read as evidence from a randomised trial.

Protons: that randomised trial asked about escalating the dose

There is no phase III randomised trial of protons in glioblastoma. I have looked; there is not one. The closest, NRG-BN001, asked whether to escalate the dose, randomising separately within photon centres and within proton centres: in the photon half, escalated against standard gave a hazard ratio of 0.95, P=0.25, negative; in the proton half, 22.0 against 22.8 months, hazard ratio 0.81, P=0.11 — by this trial's pre-specified test, likewise not significant. As for protons against photons, the protocol states that comparison would only be made if both experimental arms had first beaten their own controls; the condition was not met, so that comparison was never performed[12].

The thing most needing to be seen: both of the proton centres' arms sat higher than both of the photon centres' arms, the proton centres' control arm included[12]. That is a difference between centres and the populations they enrol — a centre effect, not a beam effect. Another frequently quoted Japanese series pushed the dose to 96.6 Gy(RBE), median survival 31.0 months, but that is a single-institution, retrospective conference abstract in 29 patients, and 8 of the 29 developed radiation necrosis at a median of 18.2 months after radiotherapy[13] — precisely the kind of price you only live long enough to meet.

On the Taiwan side there is a sentence stronger than mine, printed by the regulator on an official consent form for the doctor to read out (my rendering): "At present, for treatment effect (tumour shrinkage), there is only indirect evidence supporting medical particle (proton/heavy particle) radiotherapy as superior to conventional radiotherapy; direct evidence (prospective comparative clinical trials) is ongoing." That template lists no indications and does not mention glioblastoma[14]. The physics of protons is on this site in "Protons: precise. Then what?".

BNCT: one-year survival 79.2%, median progression-free survival 0.9 months

Japan's JG002 is the main body of this cell: a multicentre, single-arm phase II study, 27 patients with recurrent malignant glioma (24 of them glioblastoma), against historical data, one-year survival 79.2%, median survival 18.9 months. The other number in the same paper reads with it: median progression-free survival read by RANO was 0.9 months. The authors explain that post-treatment brain oedema and imaging changes were scored as progression, and 21 of the 27 went on to bevacizumab after progression was declared[15]. So the survival figure has subsequent treatment mixed into it, and what the imaging measured may not have been progression — see "How the follow-up scans are scheduled, and why bigger is not always worse". The main adverse event was brain oedema[15]; the symptoms are in "When to come back the same day: three tiers".

Taiwan's own data are honest: a 2026 retrospective study of 171 patients with recurrent disease (116 on bevacizumab, 55 with BNCT added), 98 after matching — progression-free survival 5.34 against 3.70 months, hazard ratio 0.54, response rate 67.4% against 47.0%, but the survival hazard ratio was 0.86, P=0.524, no difference; the authors write that this supports its use as a salvage strategy but needs prospective validation[16]. Seeing a response and holding it are two different things. Equipment and official charges were checked on this site in "BNCT: the radiotherapy that does not aim"; I am not rewriting the amounts.

From REGOMA to the platform trial: good phase II news waits for a phase III

Italy's REGOMA in 2019 was a randomised phase II trial, 119 people, regorafenib against lomustine: 7.4 against 5.6 months, hazard ratio 0.50; the authors' word was "encouraging", and they stated it should be validated in an adequately powered phase III[17]. Cochrane rated the certainty of that evidence "low" at the time[18].

Then the phase III was done. GBM AGILE, JCO 2026: regorafenib stopped accrual for futility, no survival improvement in either the recurrent or the newly diagnosed unmethylated population, median hazard ratios 1.05 to 1.07, and more toxicity[19]. The handsome phase II numbers were later not confirmed. GBM AGILE is a platform trial bringing different drugs in by turns against a shared control arm; it is still recruiting, expected to close in 2030, three drugs have finished accrual, and regorafenib was the first to read out[20]. How to read a trial is on this site in "Clinical trials: how to find one, how to read it".

Having a code is not the same as being covered

I checked three things on the Taiwan side; the answers all came out different. Tumour-treating fields: the full NHI fee schedule table (5,995 items) returns zero entries for 「電場」 (electric field), and zero each for 「中子」 (neutron) and 「硼」 (boron)[21]; the specified medical equipment in Appendix 2 of the regulations governing specified medical technologies includes proton machines and heavy particle equipment, and no alternating electric field device of any kind[22]; and whether it holds a medical device licence I could not find official data on — could not find is not "there isn't one", and I am not going to turn it round into "there is" either. The shape of the cost matters too: there is no national uniform price, and self-pay charges are approved institution by institution by each local competent authority; and it is not paid once, it is a recurring monthly-type expense for as long as treatment continues — confirm that when you ask about price. Its regulatory status in Taiwan and how to ask about cost are already on this site in "Electric fields on the scalp: what they can do".

Protons are the opposite: the NHI table has eight codes (N21301 to N21308), but all carry zero points and the remarks column is marked "HTA項目" (HTA item)[21]; the specified-technologies regulations list proton machines as specified medical equipment and require institutions to follow central-authority rules on accepting patients who meet the indications, on charging, and on follow-up[22]; and a central announcement stating that glioblastoma may be treated with protons I could not find. As of 30 November 2024, ROC year 113, 13 institutions nationwide were listed as having proton equipment, 4 of them operating[23].

Lined up, that is the conclusion: having a code is not the same as being covered, having the machine is not the same as having the indication, and holding a licence is not the same as this disease being allowed to use it. The differences are on this site in "Approved, covered, effective: three different things". I am not writing amounts — the field on the official consent form is headed 「自費費用」, self-pay charge[14], there is no uniform national price, and ask the medical affairs office for a written itemisation. These are also not things every hospital has — to use one, first ask your doctor where you would be referred. One cost never appears on the itemisation: it crowds out standard treatment you have not finished, money you will need for later lines, eligibility for certain trials, and your physical reserve. So before you consent to any of these, I want one question on the table: if I use this, which road closes behind me?

From the physician

What is most often misread in this cell is not the numbers, it is the designs: one of the three immunotherapy phase III trials points the wrong way, and the vaccine study held up as hopeful changed its primary endpoint midstream. Two questions before using any of them: which design produced this result, and which road closes behind me if I use it? Ask the medical affairs office for a written itemisation of costs; for items where I could not find official data, I do not infer either way.
