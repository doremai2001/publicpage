BNCT: THE EVIDENCE

The scans showed no shrinkage, yet they lived longer

This trial's imaging response rate was 3.7%, and the same patients had a median survival of 18.9 months. Both are true, and neither had a control group.

Next-Generation Therapy Guide · BNCT: the evidence · 3 references

This is the longest and the most important article in the section. On the surface it is about brain tumours; underneath, it is about how to judge a trial that has no control group. Learn this once and the same move will not work on you again.

Warning 1 (what this displaces): Most of the treatments in this topic are paid out of pocket, and most sit outside standard treatment. Before you decide, you need to know that what they displace is usually not time but three things of real value: standard treatment you have not yet used up, money you will need for later lines, and your eligibility for clinical trials (many trials exclude people who have recently received other experimental treatments). And there is one more, harder to see — physical reserve. The strength burnt through in a course that does not work may no longer be there when a genuinely useful option appears. So the question is not whether this is worth trying — it is what you give up by doing it.

BNCT is almost always used somewhere that has already been irradiated once, so it displaces one more thing that other self-paid treatments do not touch: your re-irradiation allowance — any one site can take only so much radiation in total, and what is spent does not come back. In the brain that allowance is especially a one-off, and this article takes apart what it bought.

Warning 2 (finding the right doctor): Do not make this kind of decision alone, and do not make it on the word of the person offering the treatment. The doctor you are looking for is one willing to tell you "this is not right for you" — if they never once say who this is NOT for, that is not a consultation, it is a sale.

My position first: proton therapy and hyperthermia are both technologies within my own field, and the hospital I work at has the equipment for them. BNCT is not — my hospital does not offer it and I do not deliver it. I have nothing to sell you in this section. You should know that, because it changes how you read every sentence I write here.

The evidence level here has to be written in two layers: the trial itself is single-arm phase 2, and its survival comparison is made against a historical cohort. That second layer is what this article is really about.

The JG002 numbers

This was a Japanese multicentre, open-label, single-arm phase 2 trial. It enrolled 27 patients, of whom 24 had recurrent glioblastoma, recruiting between 2016 and 2018[1]. It used a cyclotron-based epithermal neutron source, with the boron drug given at 500 mg per kilogram. The primary endpoint was one-year survival in recurrent glioblastoma.

The result: one-year survival was 79.2% (95% confidence interval 57.0 to 90.8), and median overall survival was 18.9 months.

The same report carries two other numbers: the imaging response rate read by RANO was 3.7% across all 27 patients, which is one partial response; and median progression-free survival was 0.9 months.

Nought point nine months. Which is to say that on imaging almost every patient had "progressed" inside a month, and a year later nearly eight in ten of them were still alive. Those two statements sit in the same paper, about the same 27 people. Neither is a misprint, and the work of this article is to explain how both can be true at once.

First thing: imaging and survival can come apart

After the brain has been irradiated there is a phenomenon that turns up often. The tumour looks larger on the scan and takes up contrast more avidly, but what is actually happening is inflammation and a change in the blood-brain barrier caused by the treatment, not tumour growth. It is called pseudoprogression. The RANO criteria, which are the standard way of reading these scans, will score it as progression, because from the outside it looks like progression.

So in the first months after BNCT, imaging is not a reliable thing to read. The 0.9 months of progression-free survival mainly reflects a reading standard failing in this particular situation, rather than a treatment that stopped working within a month.

This has a practical use in clinic. If you or someone in your family is having this kind of treatment and the first scan report afterwards says progression, do not take it as settled. Ask the doctor whether this could be pseudoprogression, and whether it is worth repeating the scan before any decision is made on the strength of it.

Second thing: the 18.9 months, compared against whom

JG002 had no control group, so its authors took another trial as the comparator: JO22506, a single-arm phase 2 trial published in 2012, treating recurrent malignant glioma with bevacizumab alone, 31 patients, median overall survival 10.5 months[2]. Set 18.9 against 10.5 and it looks like a large gap.

The authors explained why they considered the comparison permissible: both trials enrolled bevacizumab-naive recurrent glioblastoma, so the populations were similar[1]. There is something in that argument. But three things have to be kept in mind alongside it.

First, the era. JO22506 was published in 2012; JG002 enrolled between 2016 and 2018. Several years sit between them, and supportive care, later-line drugs and the density of imaging follow-up all changed over that stretch. The same disease treated in a later era drifts upward in survival on its own, without anyone doing anything clever.

Second, the entry criteria are not identical. JG002's performance-status threshold was a little looser than JO22506's, but the median tumour volume it enrolled was only 7.3 millilitres, which is a fairly small lesion. People who make it into a BNCT trial have been through a screen: they are well enough to travel, well enough to lie still for the irradiation, and their disease is in a form the beam can reach. A selected group does better than an unselected one whatever you then give it.

Third, and this is the one that matters most: of JG002's 27 patients, 21 (78%) received bevacizumab after their disease progressed, and 5 of those also had temozolomide[1].

To say it in plain words: what this comparison actually contains is "BNCT followed by bevacizumab" set against "bevacizumab alone". Twenty-one of the twenty-seven treated patients went on to receive the very drug the comparison group had been given, so subtracting one survival figure from the other does not leave you with the effect of BNCT by itself. The original paper states this perfectly clearly, and it almost never survives into a second-hand account.

Third thing: the price

Treatment-related adverse events of grade 3 or higher occurred in 22 patients, which is 81.5%[1]. Brain oedema 48.1%, of which grade 3 was 11.1%; alopecia 66.7%; raised amylase 81.5%, of which grade 3 or higher was 66.7% and grade 4 alone was 48.1%. Serious adverse events 33.3%.

This is not a gentle treatment. Any description of BNCT as a precise therapy with few side effects can be held up against this set of figures and read a second time. What these figures are doing here is keeping the survival numbers company, because a survival figure quoted without its toxicity figure is only half of what happened to those 27 people.

The 2025 extended follow-up

The same patients were later reported with longer follow-up: one-year survival still 79.2%, with a narrower confidence interval of 63.3 to 88.7, and median overall survival 19.2 months[3].

Note that this is a conference abstract, not a full journal paper; I could not obtain a journal version when I did my checking. The extended follow-up also carries a different confidence interval from the original report, so anyone quoting it has to say which of the two they are drawing on. That kind of care looks fussy from outside, and it is precisely where having read the original separates from having copied something second-hand.

So does this treatment work

Only one statement here holds up, and this is it: in a selected group of patients with recurrent glioblastoma, small tumours, and enough reserve to go on to further treatment, BNCT followed by bevacizumab appears to give longer survival than a historical cohort given bevacizumab alone. That is the strongest statement this data will support, and not one word can be added to it.

It is not a randomised trial, so we do not know how much of the gap came from BNCT, how much from the selection, and how much from the era. Answering that requires randomisation, and this field has still not produced any. That is a statement about the evidence, not about the treatment: an unanswered question is not the same as a negative answer, and it is not the same as a positive one either.

Take the method in this article away with you. Next time any treatment claims that survival went from X to Y, you have three questions ready: which year is the control group from? Were the entry criteria the same? And what else did the treated group go on to receive afterwards?

From the physician

If the first scan report after treatment says progression, do not take it as settled. Ask whether this could be pseudoprogression and whether it should be repeated. That question costs you nothing.
