WHEN TREATMENT STALLS

Treat those two or three spots — and then?

Not something a test finds: the consensus says the diagnosis rests on imaging alone. Every trial here is labelled by phase and by size.

Situation Guides · When Treatment Stalls · 14 references
#Radiotherapy#Recurrence#Decisions#Clinical Trials

I have labelled the level of each trial for a reason: the randomised data supporting this is almost all phase 2, four trials stopped early after an interim look, and the one closest to how drugs are used now is negative and exists only as a conference abstract. Someone else's story may not be your cancer at all.

"There are only two or three of them — if we zap them, is that it?" Let me answer the dangerous half of that question first: in every one of these studies the local treatment was added on top of standard systemic treatment, and not one of them used it to replace chemotherapy, targeted therapy or immunotherapy. Any idea that "once they're treated I can stop the drugs" has no basis anywhere in this body of data. With that in front, the numbers behind it can be read.

Oligometastatic disease is not something a test finds. It is defined off the imaging

In 2020 the European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology and the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer convened 20 experts and built a full classification through systematic review and two Delphi rounds: first, whether widespread metastases had ever been present; then whether this is a first diagnosis and whether the metastases appeared with the primary or later; and finally, by whether they were found during a treatment-free interval or during systemic treatment, and whether the lesions are currently growing, splitting into oligorecurrence, oligoprogression and oligopersistence[1].

One sentence from that consensus has to be copied out: because no biomarker can yet identify genuinely oligometastatic disease in the clinic, the diagnosis rests entirely on imaging findings[1]. So "my scan shows three, therefore I am oligometastatic" does not follow — the count is one box on the classification table, and the history fills several of the others.

The randomised data supporting it is almost all phase 2, and every trial has a limit

SABR-COMET is a randomised phase 2 trial, 99 patients across ten hospitals in four countries, taking mixed cancers with 1 to 5 metastases. In the original report median overall survival was 41 months in the local treatment arm against 28 months in the control, hazard ratio 0.57 (95% confidence interval 0.30 to 1.10), p=0.090 — a hazard ratio compares the relative rate at which deaths occur in two groups over the same period, not a multiple of probability; 0.57 in plain words means "in this dataset deaths arrived more slowly in the local treatment arm", not "the chance of dying is down to 57%"; and the trial had set its statistical threshold in advance at p below 0.20, which is a screening design, stated as such rather than a flaw, but you need to know it while reading[2]. Five-year overall survival was 42.3% against 17.7% (p=0.006)[3], and eight-year 27.2% against 13.6%[4]. The word the authors used in the eight-year report is worth keeping: among patients randomised to local treatment, a minority (21.3%) achieved more than five years of recurrence-free survival — a minority, and recurrence-free survival, not cure[4].

The others need labelling just as carefully. The Gomez trial in lung cancer was randomised phase 2, 49 patients, closed early for efficacy, median progression-free survival 14.2 against 4.4 months (p=0.022), with a pre-set significance threshold of p below 0.10[5]; the Iyengar trial was a single-institution randomised phase 2, only 29 patients, also closed early, median progression-free survival 9.7 against 3.5 months (p=0.01)[6]. In prostate cancer, STOMP was randomised phase 2, 62 patients, with androgen-deprivation-therapy-free survival as the primary endpoint, median 21 against 13 months, hazard ratio 0.60 — but it used an 80% confidence interval, the log-rank p value was 0.11, and the authors themselves wrote that this should go to a phase 3 for confirmation[7]; ORIOLE was randomised phase 2, 54 patients, with progression at six months as the primary endpoint (19% against 61%, p=0.005), not survival[8]. The only positive phase 3 is SINDAS: 133 patients, median progression-free survival 20.2 against 12.5 months, median overall survival 25.5 against 17.4 months (both p<0.001) — but it used a first-generation targeted drug, excluded brain metastases, and stopped early after a planned interim analysis[9].

Four of these trials were closed early because the interim look showed benefit. That is not unqualified good news: stopping at the point where the data look best usually means the measured effect is somewhat larger than the real one.

The cost sits in the same set of numbers

In SABR-COMET, 3 patients (4.5%) in the local treatment arm died of treatment-related causes and none in the control arm; in the eight-year report, grade 2 or worse acute or late toxicity was 30.3% against 9.1% (p=0.019)[2][4]. This paragraph cannot be left out, because "treating a few more can't hurt" is the commonest misunderstanding on this topic.

The same approach, opposite answers in two cancers

The CURB trial enrolled patients with breast cancer and non-small-cell lung cancer who developed oligoprogression on systemic treatment, 106 patients, randomised phase 2. Overall median progression-free survival was 7.2 against 3.2 months (hazard ratio 0.53, 95% confidence interval 0.35 to 0.81, p=0.0035); but split apart, non-small-cell lung cancer was 10.0 against 2.2 months (hazard ratio 0.41, p=0.0039) and breast cancer 4.4 against 4.2 months (hazard ratio 0.78, p=0.43) — no difference; grade 2 or worse adverse events in the local treatment arm ran at 62% against 41%[10]. Same approach, same trial, two cancers, opposite answers. This is why the story of what worked for somebody else cannot be carried straight over to you. Cancer-specific detail belongs to each cancer's own guide — for example "Metastatic disease: is cure still possible?".

The trial closest to how drugs are used now is negative

NRG-LU002 is a randomised phase 2/3 trial of 215 patients with non-small-cell lung cancer and no more than three extracranial lesions, randomised, if not progressing after first-line treatment, to maintenance systemic treatment alone or with the addition of local consolidative therapy, in a population largely from the immunotherapy era. The result was a hazard ratio of 0.93 (0.66 to 1.31) for progression-free survival and 1.05 (0.70 to 1.56) for overall survival, neither showing a difference; and grade 3 or worse pneumonitis of 10% in the local treatment arm against 1% in the control[11].

Its status has to be labelled: it exists only as an abstract presented at a conference in 2024, with no full paper yet published, and I obtained these numbers through a 2026 review[11]. That review's own conclusion is direct: the randomised evidence that best defines practice to date does not support routinely adding local treatment for all non-progressing patients, it flags the pneumonitis risk, and it stresses rigorous staging and patient selection; the same passage adds that lesion count alone is an imperfect surrogate for tumour biology. Two further phase 3 trials, one taking 1 to 3 and one 4 to 10 metastases, have published protocols and no results yet[12][13].

So the honest version is neither "zap them and it's over" nor "zapping them is useless". It is: adding it may help some people, and who those people are has still not been defined.

The Taiwan section: oligometastasis is not in the wording; oligoprogression is, intracranially

For stereotactic body radiotherapy the National Health Insurance (健保) indication wording covers exactly two categories: early-stage primary lung malignancy, and a single primary hepatobiliary lesion. I found no matching item for oligometastatic disease in the wording, and the reimbursement rules state that prior review is required, with the whole course within two weeks and a limit of six fractions[14]. Intracranial treatment is another matter: the indications for X-knife and gamma knife cover metastatic brain tumours numbering no more than five, and one of them writes oligoprogression in explicitly — "no more than five progressing lesions in total and no more than three progressing organs" — with all cases requiring prior case-by-case approval from the insurer[14].

I checked the full table line by line and found no matching item for extracranial oligometastatic disease in the current indication wording of 37047B. Not finding it is not the same as not being able to claim it; whether a claim can be made is a question for your treating team and the hospital billing office (醫務課). The full table is the 114.03.13 version, verified 2026-08-30, and these three codes do not appear in the amendment schedule effective 115 年 9 月 1 日. In Taiwan this often ends up involving out-of-pocket payment, and how to ask about the money is in "How to ask about a self-pay item" and "The cost that isn't on the receipt"; technique and dose are in "Can somewhere already irradiated be treated again?".

Which kind of oligometastatic disease am I? Ask that first

One: which category am I in — oligometastases appearing for the first time, or local progression during systemic treatment? (Different category, different data.) Two: for my cancer, is there randomised data, what phase, and how many patients? Three: if local treatment is added, what happens to the systemic treatment? (The answer should be that it continues, not that it stops.) Four: what is the expected gain, what is the cost, and if this is not done, what is the alternative? Whether local treatment is added, and when, is a decision the team makes after assessment; until then, do not reduce so much as one dose of your current treatment yourself. Assessment takes time, and in that time severe new pain, leg weakness or a change in bladder or bowel control, sudden breathlessness or a violent headache do not wait for the next clinic — why is in "Back pain plus a weak leg: do not wait" and "A swollen calf: can it wait until tomorrow?".

From the physician

In every one of these studies the local treatment was added on top of standard systemic treatment, never instead of it, so "treat them and stop the drugs" has no basis in this data. Trials stopped at an interim look usually measure an effect larger than the real one. Whether to add it, and when, follows team assessment; until then, do not reduce so much as one dose of your current treatment yourself.
