AT HOME

How much should the formaldehyde in a new home worry you?

Formaldehyde is Group 1, but the case that it causes nasopharyngeal cancer rests on about a dozen occupational deaths worldwide, and no study has ever looked at residential formaldehyde and nasopharyngeal cancer at all.

Carcinogens at work and at home · At Home · 19 references
#At Home#Indoor Air#Head & Neck#Prevention

“How long does a new place need to air out?” gets answered online as anything from one month to three years. The only citable evidence is a decay model built from Chinese urban homes, and Taiwan has no official number of days; the place where the effort actually pays is before you order the boards.

The research tying formaldehyde to nasopharyngeal cancer followed over twenty thousand workers across nearly a million person-years and counted a little over ten deaths from it. That number is in my head whenever a patient asks whether a newly fitted-out flat causes nasopharyngeal cancer — and one thing matters more: there is still not one epidemiological study of residential formaldehyde and nasopharyngeal cancer. This piece takes the evidence apart, thinnest link included.

The whole case rests on about a dozen deaths worldwide

Monograph 100F of the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), 2012, puts formaldehyde in Group 1, carcinogenic to humans: occupational exposure causes nasopharyngeal cancer, and the working group considered confounding and bias unlikely to explain the association[1]. One misreading first: Group 1 describes how certain the evidence is, not how dangerous the agent is — the full account is in "Group 1 is not a danger ranking" and this series' entry page, "Carcinogens at work and at home".

The main support: the US National Cancer Institute cohort of ten formaldehyde plants — 25,619 workers, 865,708 person-years to 1994 — with 9 nasopharyngeal cancer deaths. Relative risk rose with peak exposure (p-trend < 0.001), not significantly with years exposed (p = 0.147)[2]. To 2004, person-years near a million, the count was still only 10; the highest peak-exposure group's relative risk was 7.66, 95% confidence interval 0.94 to 62.34 — the interval crosses 1, so "no difference between the groups" has not been statistically excluded; highest average intensity, 11.54 (1.38 to 96.81)[3]. Two orders of magnitude is what counting ten of anything gets you.

Worse: a reanalysis of the same data found 6 of 11 nasopharyngeal cancer deaths in one plant. Exposed workers at plant 1 died of it at 7.34 times the rate of the same-age general population (standardised mortality ratio 7.34, 2.69 to 15.97); at plants 2 to 10 they fell below expectation, 0.82 (0.17 to 2.41)[4]. An independent British cohort — 6 factories, 14,008 chemical workers, 1941 to 2012 — saw no rise in upper airway cancer or leukaemia in the highest exposure category and had one nasopharyngeal cancer case in the whole cohort; the authors concluded any excess risk is at most small even at relatively high exposure[5].

On leukaemia, IARC's own working group split

The same monograph called the leukaemia evidence sufficient, but split doing it: a small majority for sufficient, a minority for limited. Reasons included consistently raised risks in embalmers and pathology staff against contradictory industrial cohorts, and no clear account then of how formaldehyde reaches the bone marrow[1]. Later data widened the gap — that British cohort of 14,008 showed no rise in myeloid leukaemia either[5].

So the honest phrasing is not "the evidence is insufficient" but: the evidence was judged sufficient, the judges split into two camps, and later studies are still pulling against each other.

Taiwan's nasopharyngeal cancer has fallen for twenty-five years

If new fit-outs were a serious driver, Taiwan should show the opposite trend, since renovation has only grown over those decades. Instead the Taiwan Cancer Registry recorded 39,412 newly diagnosed nasopharyngeal cancers between 1995 and 2021, age-standardised incidence falling from 5.81 to 4.29 per 100,000, annual change −1.16% (p < 0.001), cumulative fall 26.2%, downward in both sexes[6].

The real background is Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) and constitution. Over 90% of adults worldwide have been infected with EBV[7]; in Taiwan, unaffected members of nasopharyngeal cancer multiplex families carry EBV antibody risk scores higher than general population controls, so susceptibility acts through how well the immune system holds EBV down[8].

Flatly: as of today, not a single study links formaldehyde exposure in homes or from renovation to nasopharyngeal cancer. Every number above comes from occupational populations — factory workers, embalmers, pathology staff. Your living room is not that setting.

Household formaldehyde still matters, just not for nasopharyngeal cancer

The 2010 WHO indoor air quality guidelines list the residential sources: formaldehyde-resin particleboard, plywood, medium-density fibreboard and furniture; urea-formaldehyde foam insulation; textiles, paints, wallpaper and adhesives; cleaning products; and combustion, smoking and cooking included[9].

IARC gives both magnitudes side by side: ordinary homes 0.02 to 0.06 mg/m³, occupational exposure in those cohorts 2 to 5 ppm[1] — nearly two orders of magnitude apart, which is why occupational conclusions cannot be carried into the living room. In the national measurements WHO collates, geometric means run 22 to 30 µg/m³; the mean in recently renovated Chinese homes is 238 µg/m³[9]. Taiwan's Indoor Air Quality Standards set formaldehyde at 0.08 ppm, averaged over one hour — often written up wrongly; the eight-hour values are carbon dioxide and carbon monoxide[10]. On IARC's conversion of 1 ppm = 1.23 mg/m³[1], 0.08 ppm is about 0.098 mg/m³, essentially WHO's 0.1 mg/m³ (30-minute) for preventing sensory irritation[9].

Worth saying out loud: the Indoor Air Quality Management Act, promulgated 2011[11], covers only premises the competent authority designates — ten categories in 2014[12], six more in 2017[13], all public places of the school, hospital, station and shopping centre kind. Private homes are not in there. Nobody inspects the formaldehyde in your flat. That one is yours.

How long to air a new place out? One model, and not a Taiwanese one

Taiwan has no official document on days of ventilation before moving in, and I found no formal guidance from the Ministry of Environment or the Ministry of the Interior. What exists is an exponential decay model from 38 cities, 42 studies and 66 sets of long-term monitoring: mean indoor formaldehyde as renovation finishes 0.376 mg/m³, decay constant 1.98 × 10⁻⁴ per hour; delaying move-in six months cuts the median lifetime inhalation cancer risk 40%, a year 60%[14].

Two things need labelling. First, 0.376 mg/m³ is about 0.31 ppm, 3.8 times Taiwan's standard of 0.08 ppm[1][14] — so the opposite misreading, "household levels are low anyway, ignore it", does not stand either. Second, that decay constant puts the half-life around 3,500 hours, nearly five months — my own arithmetic, not a number the paper states; and the model is a modelled estimate pooled from Chinese urban homes, not Taiwan, and not a trial. So no tidy number of months from me. The 40% and the 60% are relative reductions in that modelled risk, and the paper does not say how large the risk is in absolute terms; it supports "airing it out longer is worth it", not "under a year is dangerous". In clinic: push move-in back a few months if you can; if not, put the effort into ventilation and source control below.

One variable gets overlooked. WHO says it directly: emission falls over time but can continue for months, especially at high relative humidity and high indoor temperature[9]. A Taiwanese summer supplies both.

Ventilation works, and it pays diminishing returns

Field measurements in new US homes show raising the air exchange rate does lower indoor formaldehyde, with a counter-intuitive detail: at a reference rate of 0.35 h⁻¹ the reduction fell short of a fixed-emission-rate prediction by as much as 60%, because as concentration drops the materials emit faster again[15]. Which is why "just open more windows" is not the whole answer.

In the same study, homes built with low-VOC certified materials had formaldehyde on average 42% lower after adjustment for house age, temperature and relative humidity (33 versus 45 µg/m³)[15]. Emitting less at source beats ventilating hard afterwards — and source control is decided in the days you sign the contract and choose the boards:

  • Read the label. Plywood, wood flooring, medium-density fibreboard, particleboard, glued laminated timber and laminated veneer lumber are subject to mandatory inspection; under the Wood-based Panel Inspection Regulations they must be tested for formaldehyde emission and marked F1, F2 or F3[16], and the Bureau of Standards, Metrology and Inspection states that the smaller the numeral, the lower the emission[17]. The per-grade thresholds sit inside a paid-for national standard with no public page I could find, so I give no figures — pick the smaller number.

  • Green Building Material labelling can be specified. The Healthy Green Building Material criteria are a formaldehyde emission rate ≦ 0.05 mg/m²·hr and TVOC ≦ 0.19 mg/m²·hr, in three grades (Grade 1 ≦ 0.005, Grade 2 0.005 to 0.02, Grade 3 0.02 to 0.05 mg/m²·hr)[18]; a November 2025 release from the Architecture and Building Research Institute, Ministry of the Interior carries the same values[19].

  • Count the furniture with the boards. On WHO's source list they are the same class of thing[9] — a full set of fitted cabinetry enlarges the emitting surface.

For formaldehyde-removal sprays, photocatalysts and air purifiers I found no citable controlled studies, so I neither endorse them nor say they do nothing. Two more: air conditioning behind closed windows lets concentration build, so keep an outdoor-air intake or open windows on a schedule; and a smell does not mean the standard is exceeded, while no smell does not mean safe.

One thing before you order, one at handover, one before you move in

If the work has not started: choose boards with the smaller number before ordering, count the furniture into the emitting area at handover, and push move-in back — six months and a year correspond to modelled risk reductions of 40% and 60%[14] — further still with a pregnancy or a child under three.

Anyone already living there has nothing to regret. Ventilate at a fixed time daily, keep an outdoor-air intake when the air conditioning runs in summer, and add no new panel furniture this year; that covers everything the evidence supports. As for nasopharyngeal cancer, ask about your family history, not your wood flooring.

From the physician

I will not tell you the formaldehyde at home does not matter, and I will not let you read a new fit-out as nasopharyngeal cancer; both readings lead to bad decisions. The reason to manage it is irritation and low-dose accumulation over time. For nasopharyngeal cancer, ask about your family history, not your wood flooring.
