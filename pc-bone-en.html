FOLLOW-UP AND RECURRENCE

Back pain plus a weak leg: do not wait

Hormone therapy thins bone slowly; cord compression takes function away in days. One of these has to be dealt with the same day.

Prostate Cancer Guide · Follow-up and Recurrence · 20 references
#Prostate#Bone Health#Hormone Therapy#Side Effects#Urgent

Two different problems share one section of the body. The slow one is a T-score you should already know; the fast one is a set of symptoms that belongs in an emergency department tonight, not in next month's clinic.

Bone has two faces here. One slow — hormone therapy draws the mineral out a little at a time, and a few years on one fall breaks something. One fast — cancer presses on the spinal cord, and if you are late the function does not come back. The slow half you can read at leisure. For the fast half, please finish the last two sections today.

What hormone therapy does to bone, in numbers

Among 50,613 men with prostate cancer in the American SEER–Medicare data, of those surviving at least five years from diagnosis, 19.4% of those who had androgen deprivation therapy had a fracture, against 12.6% of those who had not (P<0.001), and the more GnRH injections in the first 12 months, the higher the risk[1]. This is claims data, and men put on hormone therapy have worse disease to begin with, so it is not proof of cause.

Sturdier is a meta-analysis by a Taiwanese author group of 16 cohort studies, 519,168 men: odds ratio for any fracture 1.39 (1.26–1.52), 1.55 for fracture requiring admission, GRADE certainty moderate and low respectively[2]. (An odds ratio compares the ratio of events in two groups; it does not mean the probability became 1.39 times larger.) On density, a meta-analysis of 5 prospective cohorts, 533 men, found the treated group differed in percentage change of lumbar spine bone mineral density by −3.60 percentage points (P=0.02), femoral neck by −3.11[3]. Only 5 studies, so take the direction and rough size, nothing finer.

That analysis ends by recommending regular bone density testing[3]. In practice: if you expect injections for more than a few months, have a baseline dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scan around the time you start. I found no document setting out the National Health Insurance (健保) conditions for a DXA scan itself, so ask your case manager (個管師) or the hospital billing office (醫務課); what is certain is that National Health Insurance uses the DXA T-score as the threshold for osteoporosis drugs[4], and you will need that number sooner or later.

The same drug, given two completely different ways

This is what gets confused most in clinic. Denosumab has two entirely different uses:

  • The one that protects against osteoporosis: the HALT trial enrolled men on hormone therapy without bone metastases at a low dose, once every 6 months: at 24 months lumbar spine bone density +5.6% against −1.0% on placebo (P<0.001), and at 36 months new vertebral fractures 1.5% against 3.9% (relative risk 0.38, 0.19–0.78), adverse event rates similar in the two groups[5].

  • The one for men who already have bone metastases: 1,904 men with castration-resistant disease and bone metastases were given a high dose, once every 4 weeks, against zoledronic acid: median time to a first skeletal-related event 20.7 against 17.1 months (hazard ratio 0.82; a hazard ratio looks at how fast events happen, not the same measure as the odds ratio above). The price is hypocalcaemia 13% against 6% (p<0.0001) and osteonecrosis of the jaw 2% against 1% (p=0.09)[6].

Same molecule, roughly a 12-fold difference in annual dose, and a different order of risk for jaw necrosis and low calcium. National Health Insurance draws the line clearly: Xgeva (section 5.5.4) is restricted to patients — prostate cancer among them — with lytic bone metastases[7], while osteoporosis prevention sits in another section. When you hear "a bone injection", ask first: which one, how often, and why that one.

Bone drugs will not make you live longer, and that has to be said first

In 1,432 men with non-metastatic castration-resistant disease given high-dose monthly denosumab to delay bone metastases, bone-metastasis-free survival was extended a median 4.2 months (hazard ratio 0.85), but overall survival was identical (43.9 against 44.8 months, hazard ratio 1.01, p=0.91), and 33 men (5%) developed osteonecrosis of the jaw against none on placebo[8]. A treatment that delays a scan finding, does not lengthen life, and gives one man in twenty a necrotic jaw needs talking through first.

Bisphosphonates are the same. A meta-analysis of randomised trials found no survival benefit from zoledronic acid in metastatic disease (hazard ratio 0.94, p=0.323), and none in non-metastatic disease either (0.98)[9]. What it buys is skeletal events: in a placebo-controlled trial of 643 men, skeletal-related events 33.2% against 44.2% (−11.0 percentage points, P=.021), with no difference in disease progression or quality of life[10]. CALGB 90202 tested whether starting earlier does better; it does not: median time to a first skeletal event 31.9 against 29.8 months (hazard ratio 0.97), no survival difference[11]. These drugs exist to reduce fractures and skeletal events, not to lengthen life, and I will not use the second to persuade anyone.

Osteonecrosis of the jaw, and the dental assessment that has to come first

The probability of jaw necrosis must be read together with the length of exposure. Long-term follow-up of prostate cancer patients gives cumulative incidence 4.6% at 1 year, 9.6% at 2 years, 20.3% at 3 years and 51.9% at 6 years; risk factors include extensive disease, anticoagulant or antiplatelet drugs, and dental treatment within the previous 6 months (hazard ratio 2.29)[12]. This is single-institution retrospective data over long exposure; it must not be read as "half of patients get jaw necrosis".

The MASCC/ISOO/ASCO guideline is explicit: before starting a bone-modifying agent, complete a comprehensive dental assessment and discuss modifiable risk factors; while on the drug, avoid non-essential extractions and dentoalveolar surgery; and on whether to stop the drug before dental surgery, the guideline says plainly that this remains uncertain[13]. So get the order right: the teeth that need extracting and the gum disease that needs treating are dealt with before the bone injections start. The head and neck series has a whole piece on dental assessment before radiotherapy, and the same method applies. What else hormone therapy costs is in "What hormone therapy costs you".

National Health Insurance does not pay before a fracture, and that is a concrete question to ask in clinic

One rule affects you directly: for bone loss caused by hormone therapy, National Health Insurance does not reimburse osteoporosis drugs until a fracture has occurred. Section 5.6.1 requires a T-score ≤ −2.5 together with a spine or hip fracture already sustained; or osteopenia (T-score between −2.5 and −1.0) with two or more fractures at those sites. The other route is a T-score ≤ −2.5 plus one high-risk factor (rheumatoid arthritis, insulin-treated diabetes, long-term high-dose oral corticosteroids) — and "currently receiving androgen deprivation therapy" is not on that list; the section also allows only one drug at a time[4]. A man on injections with a T-score of −2.6 and no fracture does not meet it.

I write this not to criticise the system but because it can be dealt with. Ask directly: "with my T-score and fracture risk, does the National Health Insurance route work for me? If not, what are the self-pay options and what do they cost a year?" Two other rules are worth keeping: zoledronic acid 4 mg (section 5.5.3.2.1) is restricted to patients including those with prostate cancer and lytic bone metastases, and to malignancy-associated hypercalcaemia[14]; and the radium-223 rule states in so many words 「不得合併使用 abiraterone、enzalutamide 及其他治療因惡性腫瘤伴隨骨骼事件之藥品，如 denosumab、bisphosphonates 等」 — it may not be combined with abiraterone, enzalutamide, or other drugs for cancer-related skeletal events such as denosumab and bisphosphonates[15] — which is why the bone injections stop while you are having radium-223.

If these symptoms appear in the middle of the night, go to the emergency department tonight

New back pain, together with any one of the following, is treated as spinal cord compression until it has been excluded: leg weakness, unsteadiness or inability to walk, numbness or loss of sensation, pain radiating along the course of a nerve, and a change in bladder or bowel function (unable to pass urine, incontinence, or a sudden loss of the sensation of needing to go). The UK NICE guideline on spinal metastases and metastatic spinal cord compression (NG234) lists these in Box 1, and recommendation 1.3.2 says a person with a history of cancer presenting with them should have the responsible team contacted immediately and the situation "treated as an oncological emergency"; 1.5.2 requires MRI as soon as possible and always within 24 hours; 1.10.4 requires urgent radiotherapy as soon as possible and within 24 hours where compression is unsuitable for surgery; 1.11.3 requires surgery as soon as possible after the neurological symptoms begin. The guideline also requires that patients at risk be told in advance what changes to watch for, and to make contact urgently if symptoms change or worsen[16]. Steroids are usually started at the moment of suspicion and stopped if it is excluded; the dose is the doctor's decision and is not written here.

Why "tonight" and "tomorrow morning" differ is answered by prostate cancer's own data. Among patients irradiated for metastatic spinal cord compression, 86% responded overall, and 33% of those who could not walk beforehand walked again; on multivariate analysis what determined functional outcome was the speed at which the motor deficit had developed — more than 14 days to appear better than 8 to 14 days, better than rapid deterioration within 1 to 7 days (p<0.001) — along with the number of vertebrae involved; the radiotherapy fractionation schedule made no difference (p=0.859)[17]. Read that sentence the right way round: the faster the symptoms come on, the worse the outlook. Rapid deterioration is the reason to be seen today, not a reason to "watch it one more night and see if it settles".

The other thing deciding the outcome is whether you can still walk when you arrive. In a clinical instrument from 283 patients treated with radiotherapy, tumour type, sensory deficit, sphincter function and whether the patient was still ambulatory separated three prognostic groups, whose rates of walking after radiotherapy were 10%, 65% and 97%[18]. The state you arrive in is largely the state you stay in — the whole reason not to wait.

For those suitable for surgery, decompression then radiotherapy beats radiotherapy alone: in a randomised trial of 101 patients (stopped early at interim analysis), the proportion able to walk after treatment was 84% against 57%, and walking was retained a median of 122 days against 13 days[19]; trials stopped early overestimate the effect, so trust the direction, not the precision. Nor is surgery a universal answer: among 54 prostate cancer patients operated on for cord compression, 19 of 54 had a complication within 30 days and one-year mortality was 59%[20].

Taiwan has no equivalent of the UK's spinal cord compression coordinator, which makes the instruction simpler: go to the emergency department the same day, and say the whole sentence — "I have prostate cancer, I now have new back pain with leg weakness (or numbness, or a change in bladder or bowel function), spinal cord compression needs to be excluded, I need an MRI." Say the words "spinal cord compression" and you are triaged entirely differently. Do not wait for tomorrow's clinic, do not take a painkiller and watch, and do not hold out until daylight because it feels awkward to turn up at night.

Men on injections ask the first two; men with bone metastases ask the last two

On hormone injections: have I had a bone density scan, and what is my T-score? Given my T-score and fracture history, does the National Health Insurance route work for me? With bone metastases already: which bone injection am I on, and how often? Have I had a dental assessment before starting it? And one last note for your phone: new back pain with leg weakness, numbness or a change in bladder or bowel function — emergency department, same day.

From the physician

Get a baseline DXA before long-term injections, and get the dental work done before the bone injections start. Put one line in your phone: new back pain with leg weakness, numbness or a change in bladder or bowel function — emergency department, same day.
