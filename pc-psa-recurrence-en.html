FOLLOW-UP AND RECURRENCE

What salvage treatment actually achieves

Salvage radiotherapy at the best possible starting point still fails in nearly three men in ten by five years. Here are all the cells of that table.

Prostate Cancer Guide · Follow-up and Recurrence · 23 references
#Prostate#Recurrence#Decisions#Hormone Therapy#Clinical Trials

Biochemical recurrence, metastasis and death are three separate events, often years apart. What decides how worried to be is your PSA doubling time — and what salvage buys depends heavily on the PSA at which it starts.

On the day someone hears "the PSA has gone up again", most men take in one word: recurrence. I take out paper and write three things separately — biochemical recurrence, metastasis, death. Three events, often years apart. The other side of the paper matters too: salvage treatment does not always bring it back, and this piece will not offer that comfort.

A rising PSA is not metastasis and not a death sentence, but it is not nothing either

The most quoted series: 1,997 Johns Hopkins men after radical prostatectomy, median follow-up 5.3 years. 315 (15%) had a PSA rise; of the 304 who did not start hormone therapy at once, 103 (34%) later developed metastases, and the median from the rise beginning to metastasis visible on a scan was 8 years[1]. Eight years is a median, not a promise, but the timescale here is years, not days: you have time to work it out.

Another 379 men with biochemical recurrence after surgery, mean follow-up 10.3 years: 16 years on from the recurrence, median survival had still not been reached; three variables separated them — PSA doubling time, pathological Gleason score, and the interval from surgery to recurrence[2]. If this piece leaves you one term, make it PSA doubling time: it sets how worried to be, and the strength of everything below. How low- and high-risk recurrence are separated is in "A PSA bounce is not a recurrence".

The real numbers for salvage radiotherapy, copied out as they stand

Ten academic centres, 2,460 men with a detectable PSA and negative nodes after surgery, given salvage radiotherapy, median follow-up 5 years. Five-year freedom from biochemical failure 56% overall, by the PSA at which treatment started:

  • PSA 0.01–0.20 ng/mL: 71% (441 men)

  • 0.21–0.50: 63% (822 men); 0.51–1.00: 54% (533 men)

  • 1.01–2.00: 43% (341 men); above 2.0: 37% (323 men)[3]

An older generation of the same message, 1,540 men, is blunter: six-year probability of freedom from progression 32%; for salvage radiotherapy alone at a PSA ≤0.50, 48%; with a PSA doubling time ≤10 months or Gleason 8 to 10 as well, 41%[4].

Let me be direct. First, earlier is better; that direction is reliable. Second, even at the most favourable starting point, close to three men in ten have a PSA that rises again within five years, and across everyone it is nearly half. So I will not say "salvage treatment usually works" — no cell in that table carries it. Third, "free of biochemical failure" is not cure; only that the PSA did not climb again in that period. Fourth, this is retrospective multicentre data, not a randomised comparison of PSA thresholds; a man caught at 0.2 and one who reaches 2.0 differ in more than timing. Trust the direction, treat the percentages as approximate.

Should radiotherapy follow surgery straight away? Three trials say you can wait

The old practice was to add radiotherapy at once when the surgical pathology looked bad — adjuvant treatment. Three randomised trials and a prospectively planned meta-analysis asked: can you wait until the PSA rises instead — early salvage?

RADICALS-RT, 1,396 men, median follow-up 4.9 years: five-year freedom from biochemical progression 85% adjuvant against 88% salvage (hazard ratio 1.10, 95% confidence interval 0.81–1.49, p=0.56 — no difference detected; a hazard ratio compares how fast events happen in two groups over one period). Adjuvant cost more: worse self-reported urinary incontinence at one year, grade 3 to 4 urethral stricture within two years 6% against 4% (p=0.020) — and in the salvage arm only 228/699 (33%) needed radiotherapy within eight years[5]. RAVES, 333 men, closed early for too few events: 86% against 87%, formally not demonstrating non-inferiority (p=0.15) — meaning "not enough men to prove the gap is small enough", not "salvage was worse"; grade 2 or higher urinary toxicity 54% salvage against 70% adjuvant, half the salvage arm never irradiated[6]. GETUG-AFU 17, 424 men (718 planned, stopped early): late grade 2 or higher urinary toxicity 59% adjuvant against 22% salvage[7]. ARTISTIC pooled all three, 2,153 men: event-free survival hazard ratio 0.95 (0.75–1.21, p=0.70), one percentage point at five years[8].

The honest wording is "no difference was detected", not "the two were proved equal" — RAVES never crossed its non-inferiority line. The practical meaning: wait until the PSA rises and between a third and a half of men never have that radiotherapy or its side effects.

Should hormone therapy be added? Here the caveat matters more than the conclusion

GETUG-AFU 16 gave salvage radiotherapy with or without 6 months of goserelin to 743 men whose PSA had risen from ≤0.1 to 0.2–2.0: five-year progression-free survival 80% against 62% (hazard ratio 0.50); at a median follow-up of 112 months, 120-month progression-free survival 64% against 49% (hazard ratio 0.54)[9][10]. RTOG 9601 added 24 months of high-dose bicalutamide in 760 men: at 13 years, 12-year overall survival 76.3% against 71.3%[11]; at 18.9 years, 18-year overall survival 53% against 43%, prostate cancer death 18% against 28%[12]. Different drugs, durations and eras — they cannot be merged into "add hormones and you're right".

What should govern the decision is RTOG 9601's subgroup analysis by pre-salvage PSA. In 118 men with a PSA above 1.5, the antiandrogen gave 25 percentage points of absolute 12-year overall survival (hazard ratio 0.45). But in the 389 men with a pre-salvage PSA ≤0.6 — the early salvage situation commonest today — there was no improvement in overall survival (hazard ratio 1.16, 0.79–1.70), the risk of dying of something else rose instead (subdistribution hazard ratio 1.94, 1.17–3.20; P=.01), and the odds ratio for late grade 3 to 5 cardiac and neurological toxicity was 3.57 (1.09–15.97; P=.05)[13]. (An odds ratio compares the ratio of events in two groups; it does not mean the probability became 3.57 times larger.)

So ask in clinic: at my PSA, what does adding hormone therapy buy, and what does it cost? For a man caught at a very low PSA the answer is nothing like it is above 1.5. What hormone therapy costs in its own right is in "What hormone therapy costs you".

Recurrence after radiotherapy: surgery, cryotherapy, HIFU — read the price first

A local recurrence after radiotherapy can still be salvaged surgically, but it is no mirror image of "radiotherapy after surgery". An international series of 404 salvage radical prostatectomies, median follow-up 4.4 years: 10-year freedom from biochemical recurrence 37%, metastasis-free survival 77%, cancer-specific survival 83%[14]. A meta-analysis of 34 studies gives the price: serious complications 14.56%; at one year, urinary incontinence of any degree (at least one pad a day) 49.57%, two or more pads a day 24.62%; erectile dysfunction 73.21% — no common measure was used for that last, so it cannot be read alongside the pad-based figures[15]. The authors conclude it "should be performed with caution and only under strict indications".

The non-surgical options are no synonym for gentle either. A meta-analysis of 64 case series, 5,585 men: biochemical control 69% brachytherapy, 69% external beam re-irradiation, 58% high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU); for incontinence the prevalence — the proportion still leaking at a given moment, not the cumulative "leaked at some point in the first year" measure above — 3% after brachytherapy, 28% after HIFU[16]. Subtract one measure from the other and salvage surgery sounds too heavy, HIFU too light. The option described in the lightest terms had the lowest control rate and the highest incontinence. A systematic review of focal (not whole-gland) salvage collected 15 studies, three-year biochemical disease-free survival four to seven in ten, the authors stating openly that patient selection, protocols and outcome reporting were all inconsistent and that no level 1 evidence exists[17]. Most series come from referral centres, so published complication rates are best-case, not average.

"Let's do a PSMA PET first and see where it is" — how far does that hold?

In 635 men prospectively studied, ⁶⁸Ga-PSMA-11 PET detected disease in only 38% at a PSA below 0.5 ng/mL, reaching 97% only at 5.0 and above[18]. The PSMA-SRT phase 3 randomised trial pushed it to decisions: 193 men, 37% of the PSMA PET arm localised, major management change 45% against 22% (23 percentage points, p=0.002). But at publication its primary endpoint — whether changing management on the scan improves outcomes — had not reported[19]. The only randomised trial so far showing an outcome difference is EMPIRE-1, single centre, 165 men — three-year event-free survival 75.5% against 63.0% (p=0.0028) — using a different tracer, ¹⁸F-fluciclovine[20]. Single centre, different tracer: not transferable to PSMA.

In Taiwan: the National Health Insurance Administration's (健保署) note on self-paid items lists PET's covered indications, and prostate cancer is not among them[21]; that document is about FDG, and for PSMA PET I found no official National Health Insurance document, so ask your case manager (個管師) or the hospital billing office (醫務課).

The Taiwanese rule: that tablet is not your turn yet

Men often arrive holding a news story and ask whether they can start a new hormonal agent now. Sections 9.49 (abiraterone) and 9.54 (enzalutamide) of the National Health Insurance Administration's Drug Reimbursement Regulations (全民健康保險藥品給付規定) are explicit: reimbursement begins at high-risk non-metastatic castration-resistant disease, or metastatic castration-sensitive or castration-resistant disease. Biochemical recurrence alone is not covered, and both need prior review and approval before use, with re-application every 3 months[22][23].

Two more things there. One, the National Health Insurance definition of PSA progression: 「PSA 下降達最低值(nadir)後，出現 PSA 值上升較 nadir≧25%，且 PSA≧2 ng/mL，並於至少3週後，再次抽血確認 PSA 值有上升趨勢」 — after the PSA has fallen to a nadir, a rise of at least 25% above it with a PSA of at least 2 ng/mL, confirmed at least three weeks later by a repeat test showing the rise continuing. Two, the PSA doubling time on the application must be calculated by linear regression from at least three consecutive values, each ≥0.2 ng/mL with the highest above 1.0, first to last at least 8 weeks apart and no more than 12 months[23]. That is why your doctor asks for another blood test — not stalling, the third point the rule needs. National Health Insurance funds one of these agents for life, with no swapping; that rule decides your treatment order from here on, and is set out in full in "After it spreads, the order of treatments".

The situations that do not wait for the next appointment

If you are on hormone therapy, or already have bone metastases, one set of symptoms cannot wait: new back pain, together with leg weakness, numbness, or a change in bladder or bowel function. Treat it as spinal cord compression: go to the emergency department the same day, not tomorrow's clinic. Its own section is "Back pain plus a weak leg: do not wait".

Otherwise, take three questions into your next appointment: what is my PSA doubling time? At my PSA, which cell of that table is my expected result from salvage radiotherapy? And if hormone therapy is added, what does someone in my range get, and pay? Ask those and you leave with something you can reason about, not one number and a sleepless night.

From the physician

Nothing here is a reason to rush or to despair; the timescale is years. Take your PSA doubling time, your pre-salvage PSA and the question “what does adding hormone therapy buy me at this level, and cost me” into the next appointment.
