VESTIBULAR SCHWANNOMA

After treatment: balance, hearing, the face

Some of what surfaces after treatment can wait for the next appointment and some has to be said the same day — and most people mistake the most urgent of them for a question about appearance.

Benign Brain Tumour Guide · Vestibular schwannoma · 18 references
#Hearing#Rehabilitation#Side Effects#Quality of Life

Of the four things I am asked about most after treatment, only one cannot wait: an eye that will not close — what it damages is the cornea, not your appearance. For the other three (balance, devices and subsidies for one-sided hearing, and the scan looking bigger), most of the data I can find reaches only "very low" certainty, so I have written the hardness of each into the sentence itself.

Once treatment is over the clinic questions change: will I wobble when I walk, is this ear any use any more, will my smile be crooked, and what if the next scan is bigger. One thing I would rather not hide goes first: for these particular questions after treatment for a vestibular schwannoma — also called an acoustic neuroma — the level of evidence mostly stops at "very low", so behind every number below I will tell you how hard it is.

Dizziness is the commonest of them, and the evidence for rehabilitation is graded "very low"

In a German cross-sectional study of 93 people after vestibular schwannoma surgery — cross-sectional meaning everything was measured at one point in time rather than followed forward — 80.6% had postoperative dizziness; the degree of handicap was associated with vestibular symptom burden, anxiety and psychological stress, but the authors say that with a cross-sectional design and no objective vestibular testing, no causal relationship can be established[1].

What about vestibular rehabilitation? A systematic review of 23 studies is honest about it: in the vestibular schwannoma population the effect of vestibular physiotherapy is "uncertain", and on GRADE assessment — a formal system for rating how much confidence the evidence deserves — all four of dizziness, static balance, dynamic balance and vestibular function come out at "very low" certainty; multimodal programmes combining gaze stabilisation, habituation, balance and gait training have the potential to improve things, while doing any one of them on its own mostly reached no statistical significance[2]. Another review of 8 studies, all using the Dizziness Handicap Inventory, is blunter: whether by surgery or by radiotherapy, the overall score was not changed by treatment[3]. In the 455-person prospective comparison, dizziness and balance changes did not differ significantly between the three groups either[4]. Worth doing, but do not expect it to be reliably effective.

Once the hearing has gone: where each of the three devices sits

The Brazilian Society of Otology guideline attaches a different strength to each of the three devices. Contralateral routing of signal (CROS) and bone-conduction devices: single-sided deafness caused by a sporadic vestibular schwannoma "may benefit" — weak recommendation, low evidence. The cochlear implant: restricted to single-sided deafness with intractable tinnitus where the cochlear nerve is preserved both anatomically and functionally, a moderate recommendation on moderate evidence, and the same recommendation states the price as well — follow-up imaging after the operation needs extra handling[5].

Results have to be read with their denominators. Of 41 vestibular schwannomas under 2 cm implanted at the time of resection, 48.8% were still using the device a year later; in another group of 17 followed for two years, mean word recognition was 40%, and the better the hearing in the opposite ear, the worse the implanted ear performed[5]. Among people implanted without surgery — during observation or after radiotherapy — a review of 12 studies and 50 cases put the proportion reaching open-set speech recognition at 64% in the radiotherapy group and 60% in the observation group[6].

The Taiwanese clauses say these words, and no more

This section I only copy out; I do not extrapolate from it. The adult indication for the NHI's cochlear implant procedure reads 「學語後失聰且兩耳聽力損失呈≧70dB HL（0.5、1、2、4KHz）」 — postlingual deafness with hearing loss of 70 dB HL or worse in both ears at 0.5, 1, 2 and 4 kHz — together with prior use of a hearing aid for six months or more and an aided open-set speech test score below 50%[7]. The assistive-device subsidy is a different route: item 240, 「人工電子耳」 (cochlear implant), in the schedule appended to the 《身心障礙者輔具費用補助辦法》, the Regulations on Subsidies for Assistive Device Costs for Persons with Disabilities, is capped at NT$600,000, once in a lifetime, and the eligible group reads 「18歲以上、未滿65歲……且個人未曾接受全民健康保險人工電子耳給付者之優耳聽力劣於90dB HL」 — aged 18 or over and under 65, who have never received an NHI-funded cochlear implant, with hearing in the better ear worse than 90 dB HL[8].

The category used for single-sided hearing is item 85, 「助聽器-雙對側傳聲型」, the contralateral-routing hearing aid (capped at NT$30,000, minimum service life four years). The specification clause reads 「1只符合助聽器-進階型規格或功能之助聽器配戴於優耳，及另1只為無線收音麥克風設備，配戴於劣耳耳部」 — one hearing aid meeting the advanced-type specification worn on the better ear, and one wireless receiving microphone worn at the poorer ear. Its eligible group is 「第二類：【b230】、【s260】或【02】」, which means a disability certificate has to be in place first; the subsidy rate is 50% for general households, 75% for lower-middle-income households and the full amount for low-income households, and anything bought before approval is not subsidised[8]. For hearing function (b230) at age six and over, the mildest grade, grade 1, is defined as 「雙耳整體障礙比率介於45.0%至70.0%，或一耳聽力閾值為90分貝以上，且另一耳聽力閾值為48分貝以上者」 — an overall bilateral impairment ratio between 45.0% and 70.0%, or a threshold of 90 decibels or worse in one ear together with 48 decibels or worse in the other (the clause's 「分貝」 is dB)[9].

Those words are what the clauses say. Whether your own audiogram lines up with them is a question to take, with the actual numbers, to your neuro-otologist, to the hospital's medical affairs office, and to the assistive-device centre or social affairs bureau for the district you are registered in — the three windows work to different criteria in the first place.

An eye that will not close is a corneal problem, not a cosmetic one

At House-Brackmann grade 3 the eye can still be closed completely with effort; from grade 4 it will not close[10] (the grading table is in "Watching, radiosurgery, surgery"). Once that line is crossed, this is no longer "the smile is a bit crooked": the complications the Brazilian guideline lists, following from incomplete lid closure, are exposure keratopathy, ulceration, and even corneal perforation, with management running from eye drops to facial nerve reconstruction or oculoplastic surgery[5]. So a dry eye, a red eye, a foreign-body sensation or blurred vision is something to go back and say out loud, not something to save for the next appointment.

A few other things. Steroids are often given for an early facial palsy, but the same guideline is clear that the practice lacks supporting evidence[5] — whether to start is not something I can advise you on, and a dose still less. The same holds for anyone already taking them: a corticosteroid must never be increased, reduced or stopped on your own initiative, because stopping suddenly buys you a different emergency (see "Pituitary apoplexy, and not enough hormone"); go back to the doctor who prescribed it for any adjustment. On hypoglossal-facial anastomosis there is a systematic review of 15 studies: the shorter the interval between the operation and the anastomosis the better the result, but the methodological differences between studies are too large[11]. The NHI fee schedule does contain a hypoglossal-facial anastomosis item, and its regulation column reads 「限神經外科專科醫師施行」, restricted to neurosurgical specialists[7]. As for disability certification, I could not find a clause in the schedule that takes facial palsy itself as an assessment dimension; the column that relates to the face is written in terms of area and structural defect[9].

Headache and a cerebrospinal fluid leak: one slow, one urgent

Long-term headache after surgery is commoner than most people expect: in a questionnaire cohort of 101 people after vestibular schwannoma surgery about 53% had it, and the strongest predictors were neither the tumour nor the approach but pre-existing psychiatric disorder, headache, chronic pain syndrome and a tendency to somatisation; the authors take it to be more like chronic post-surgical pain[12]. But in that 455-person prospective comparison, headache changes did not differ significantly between the three groups[4] — do not put every headache down to the treatment.

A cerebrospinal fluid leak is urgency of another kind. Incidence varies with study design and must not be pooled: 2.7% in one surgeon's series of 146 patients[13], and 3.0% in a single centre using the middle fossa approach (2/66, both successfully managed with lumbar drainage)[14]; the meta-analytic comparison of the three approaches showed no significant difference[15]. What you need to recognise is where it leaks from: one prospective record lists the routes in order as rhinorrhoea 80%, leakage from the retroauricular wound 33.3% and discharge from the ear canal 33.3%, with four in ten having more than one route and four in ten leaking again after it had first stopped[16]. Clear fluid running from the nose, fluid seeping from the wound, discharge from the ear canal, or any of these getting worse when you bend forward or lie down — these are for going back to a doctor, not for handling yourself.

Bigger on the scan: it only holds up in hindsight

Transient enlargement of the tumour on imaging after stereotactic radiosurgery is a known phenomenon; the guideline puts it at "up to 30% of patients" and says it is not a predictor of treatment failure[5]. But the denominators of the next two figures are different, and mixing them up gets you wildly wrong answers. The first: of 171 people whose tumour had already enlarged after radiosurgery, 142 (83%) were eventually judged to have had pseudoprogression — the denominator of that 83% is people whose tumour had already enlarged, not everyone treated; in the same dataset, late-onset enlargement occurring more than three years afterwards accounted for 21%, and 36% of people deteriorated clinically at the same time as the enlargement[17]. The second: among all 259 patients given single-session radiosurgery, early pseudoprogression was 35.5%, late 11.2%, stable 41.3% and true progression 12%; more than 86% of those with pseudoprogression or stable disease retained serviceable hearing, against only 61% of those with true progression[18].

What matters most is this: all of these data are retrospective classifications, and every one of those percentages could only be worked out after the follow-up had finished. The site's "The scan looks worse. The disease may not be." has already settled the same point: no imaging feature and no symptom can separate this from genuine deterioration at the time you are standing in it. So "getting bigger is usually nothing" is a sentence I will not say, and I would ask you not to say it either. There is exactly one correct move: keep the appointment as booked, and ask out loud how this change was measured; whether you are clinically stable is for a doctor to assess in person.

Three columns you can start keeping today

Take a notebook and keep three columns only: which day, and what movement makes you dizzy or unsteady; whether clear fluid has come from the ear or the nose and whether the wound is seeping; and whether your eye is dry in the morning and whether it closes completely. The dizziness column is for rehabilitation medicine and neuro-otology to read — a full-table search of the current fee schedule turned up no item called 「前庭復健」 or 「聽能復健」, vestibular or auditory rehabilitation; rehabilitation is claimed under a simple, moderate or complex therapy classification, and the balance-related content falls under exercise therapy and postural training[7]. Not finding a listed item does not mean it is not covered; ask rehabilitation medicine and the medical affairs office how it would be arranged. Open the notebook at the next appointment and ask three more things: how was this imaging change measured? Which balance exercises should I be doing, and how often is that reassessed? Does the eye need ophthalmology involved as well? How the follow-up intervals are set is in "How follow-up is scheduled, and when it stops", and what can be done if it comes back is in "If it comes back, what can be done".

From the physician

Getting bigger on a scan is the slow one; an eye that will not close is the urgent one, and the two are handled separately in clinic. The slow one can only be read backwards once the follow-up is over, so what you can do is go back at the time already booked and get the person doing the measuring to tell you how it was measured this time. The urgent one does not wait for the next slot: from the moment the eye stops closing, the cornea is exposed every day.
