WHAT COMES AFTER

Seizures, oedema and steroids

Whether to take a drug before you have ever had a seizure, whether the oedema settles once the operation is over, why a steroid must not be cut down on your own, and what the Taiwanese licensing text says word for word.

Benign Brain Tumour Guide · What comes after · 12 references
#Seizures#Steroids#Work & Travel#Evidence

Once the words "peritumoural oedema" turn up on a report, three things tend to follow in a row: somebody wants an anti-seizure tablet in advance, somebody feels better on a steroid and quietly takes less of it, somebody wants to know whether they can still drive. Each has its own answer, and two of the three have already been fixed in writing — one by a guideline, one by the law.

Two kinds of people ask me about this in clinic. One has already had a seizure and wants to know how long the drug has to go on. The other has never had one, but is holding a report with the words "peritumoural oedema" on it and would like a tablet in advance, just in case. For the second person there is now a definite answer, and the answer is don't — that is not my private opinion, it is the level A recommendation written into the 2021 guideline update from the Society for Neuro-Oncology and the European Association of Neuro-Oncology: "in patients with newly diagnosed brain tumours who have never had a seizure, clinicians should not prescribe anti-seizure medication in order to reduce the risk of seizures"[1].

Three in ten had already had a seizure before the operation

First the scope. Every seizure and oedema figure below comes from meningioma; vestibular schwannoma (also called acoustic neuroma) and pituitary adenoma grow outside the brain parenchyma or inside the sella, seizures are not their main problem, and in this article you only need the steroid section and the driving section. Why location decides the symptoms is written in "Where it sits matters more than how big it is".

Now put the denominator down. For supratentorial meningioma — the ones sitting over the cerebral hemispheres — the prevalence of seizures before surgery is 29.2%, from a systematic review and meta-analysis of 39 observational cohorts and 4,709 patients, which contains not a single controlled trial[2]. Among the associated factors that same analysis worked out, the strongest is peritumoural oedema: odds ratio 7.48 (95% confidence interval 6.13 to 9.47)[2]. An odds ratio is the ratio between two groups of "having it" against "not having it", and cannot be read as the probability multiplied several times over; the range inside the bracket is where that estimate plausibly sits. The other factors are much weaker: male sex 1.74, non-skull-base location 1.77.

The surgical end has numbers too. Of 703 people who had seizures before surgery, 69.3% became seizure-free after it; of 1,085 who had never had one, 12.3% developed new postoperative seizures[2]. These are pooled values, not your personal probability; and the 69.3% is not a reason to operate, because it comes out of cohort data with no control arm.

Never had one: the guideline's word is "should not"

Level A is the highest grade of strength in that guideline, and it is hanging on a negative sentence. That is not the same sentence as "there is at present no evidence supporting routine use": the latter means the data are not enough, the former means the data are enough and the direction is not to give it[1]. Meningioma's own meta-analysis points the same way: whether or not prophylactic anticonvulsants were given, the proportion with new postoperative seizures was no different[2].

The same guideline carries another line that gets quoted less often, and it blocks the commonest inference made in clinic: "there is insufficient evidence to support the use of tumour location, histology, grade, molecular or imaging features to decide whether to prescribe prophylactic anti-seizure medication (level U)"[1]. Level U means the evidence is not enough to come down on either side. So what about "my meningioma is on the convexity and the oedema is large — does that make me the exception"? The guideline says in as many words that there is nothing holding that sentence up. How the grade itself is decided is written in "How a meningioma gets called grade 1, 2 or 3", and it is equally not a basis for prescribing or withholding the drug.

Plenty of people are still citing the American Academy of Neurology's practice parameter from 2000. That document has been retired, the date is 4 June 2012, and the Academy's own page carries the words "This guideline is retired."[3]. Deciding whether to take a drug from a document that has been retired is a practice that ought to stop.

The few days either side of the operation are a separate box

The same guideline uses completely different words for the surgical box: "in patients with brain tumours undergoing surgery, there is insufficient evidence to recommend prescribing anti-seizure medication perioperatively or postoperatively to reduce the risk of seizures (level C)"[1]. That sentence has no answer yet, so I am putting the data pointing the other way up here as well.

A single centre collected 517 patients with supratentorial convexity or parasagittal meningioma who had no seizures before surgery; 30 (5.8%) had a seizure within the first week after the operation, and the only independent predictor that stood up in multivariable analysis was a surgical or medical complication (odds ratio 16.33, 95% confidence interval 7.07 to 37.7)[4]. The same paper pooled 10 publications from 2011 to 2021, and the direction there favours giving the drug: in supratentorial meningioma with no preoperative seizures, early postoperative seizures were 3.7% (47/1,282) in the treated group and 6.2% (95/1,525) in the untreated one, and among convexity and parasagittal tumours left untreated it was 9.0% (19/209)[4].

This is retrospective single-centre data plus a non-randomised pooling of the literature, a long way below the guideline above. I write both, because they are not about the same box: the long-term prophylaxis box has an answer, the few days around the operation do not. Whether it is used, and for how many days, is what your doctor decides from the extent of your surgery and your risk of complications.

Why a tumour growing outside the brain still waterlogs it

A meningioma grows on the outside of the brain, so by rights it should not leave the brain sitting in water — but it does. Immunohistochemistry on 79 grade 1 meningiomas, together with observation of the vessels during surgery, gave one concrete mechanism: vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) was expressed only on the tumour cells, and what actually correlated with peritumoural oedema was the combination of a pial blood supply and VEGF expression by the tumour (P<.002). Sex, age, tumour size, location and histological subtype, each taken on its own, explained nothing about where the oedema came from[5].

After the operation the oedema mostly does not go — this part runs against intuition

Once the tumour is out, does that sheet of oedema settle? Among 279 meningioma patients who had oedema before surgery, went to gross total resection and had an MRI at least a year afterwards, 96.8% (270/279) still had oedema change visible on the imaging; over a median five years of follow-up, only 35.8% (102/279) resolved by more than nine tenths[6]. The authors' reading is that what remains is mostly no longer leaking fluid but gliosis — the tissue itself has changed.

The next question is answered by 218 people from the same dataset. Those whose residual oedema volume fell in the highest tertile (median 12.8 cm³), compared with those in the lowest (median 0.5 cm³), had 4.7 times the odds of new-onset seizures (P=0.03); those whose postoperative oedema volume actually increased had 6.7 times the odds of new-onset seizures and 4.4 times the odds of persisting seizures[7]. Of the 23 who had an electroencephalogram, in 10 the epileptogenic focus sat exactly where the residual oedema was[7].

So "the swelling settles once the operation is done, and then the tablets can stop" does not hold in meningioma. Whether the drug stops is something your doctor decides from your seizure history, your electroencephalogram and your imaging together — not from whether the report still says oedema.

Steroids: the drug that makes you feel better, and the bill that comes with it

The side effects one by one, and why the taper has to be slow, are already written on this site in "Steroids: the drug that saves you, and has to come down", and I am not rewriting them. I am leaving three anchors here, and every one of them has to carry its population label first, because not one of them was produced in a meningioma population.

The first is dose. Two double-blind randomised trials in patients with brain metastases, 89 evaluable people between them, found that dexamethasone 4 mg a day and 16 mg a day gave comparable improvement in Karnofsky score at day seven (6.7±11.3 against 9.1±12.4 points), while toxicity was clearly commoner in the 16 mg arm (P<0.03)[8]. The second is time. Among 216 patients with primary brain tumours (mixed diagnoses, not a pure meningioma population) who took dexamethasone continuously for more than two weeks, 23 (10.6%) developed clinically significant steroid myopathy, two-thirds of them between weeks 9 and 12 of continuous treatment[9]. These mg figures are how the studies sorted their patients; they are not a regimen to copy.

The third is why you must not stop it yourself. A meta-analysis of 74 studies and 3,753 people cut the rate of adrenal insufficiency after stopping by route, dose and length of course, and got everything from 4.2% to 52.2%; the authors' concluding sentence is that no form of administration, dose, treatment duration or underlying disease can be said with certainty to exclude adrenal insufficiency[10]. That is whole-population data, not brain tumour data, but the direction is plain: the price of quietly taking one tablet fewer is not "a slightly weaker effect", it is a different emergency. Adding, reducing, stopping — all of it goes back to whoever prescribed it.

On the Taiwan side I have pulled up the current dexamethasone and prednisolone entries and looked at them: the drug reimbursement regulations column is empty for the great majority of them, with no special provision aimed at brain tumour oedema[11]. That only proves what the search returns; it cannot be turned round into an inference about how reimbursement is determined. How to file a claim is a question for the medical affairs office.

The last question is usually: can I still drive?

I am quoting the driving section word for word, because it gets recited from memory as six months or a year far too often. The Road Traffic Safety Rules (as amended on 26 June 2026, ROC year 115), Article 64, paragraph 1, subparagraph 1, item 6-1, setting out the standards for passing the physical examination, reads — this is my rendering of the clause — "Epilepsy. This shall not apply where a diagnostic certificate is produced, issued by a physician at a medical institution, attesting that no seizure has occurred for the last two years or more." The same item carries two further open-ended provisions, left to a specialist physician's determination. Article 52-3 gives the road back: a person who satisfies that proviso may apply to sit the driving test for a motorcycle or an ordinary small passenger car licence; such a licence is renewed once every two years from the date of issue, and the renewal has to be done within one month before or after the expiry date[12]. The opposite direction is written down too: if a seizure occurs, or if the licence is not renewed periodically as required, the licence must be handed back promptly and the person may not drive a motor vehicle until then (Article 76, paragraph 1, subparagraphs 5 and 8, together with paragraph 2); and after handing it back, a person who has had no seizure for the last two years or more and produces a physician's diagnostic certificate may be issued a new licence directly, without sitting the test (Article 65)[12]. How a professional licence is assessed under the age of 60 is not fixed in the text; I could not find an express provision I could cite, so ask the motor vehicles office. What the family should do while a seizure is actually happening is written more completely on this site, in "Seizures, anti-seizure drugs, and driving".

Three things to get ready before your next appointment. Copy down the line on the report that says "peritumoural oedema", and ask where that volume sits in relation to seizure risk. If you are on a steroid, write down the date you started — weeks 9 to 12 are where the myopathy clusters, and the date is easier to forget than the dose. If you drive, take Article 64, Article 52-3 and Article 76 into the consulting room and ask "can I drive at the moment, and when do we reassess".

From the physician

What worries me is two moves in opposite directions: cutting your own steroid down as you go along, or asking for an anti-seizure tablet because the report has the word "oedema" in it. I have met both in clinic. The first may buy you a different emergency; against the second, the current guideline writes "should not" at its highest grade of evidence — so neither move is one you can make on your own.
