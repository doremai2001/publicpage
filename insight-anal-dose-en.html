ANAL CANCER

Nine gray less, and the result held

PLATO-ACT4: cutting the dose from 50.4 Gy to 41.4 Gy in early anal cancer gave comparable response at six months, less acute toxicity and better sexual function.

Insights · Urologic & GI · 6 references
#Anal#Chemotherapy#Side Effects#Quality of Life

A good cure rate is not the same as living well afterwards. Anal cancer has long done well on organ preservation; the cost lands in the pelvis — diarrhoea, faecal urgency, perineal skin breakdown, sexual dysfunction. This trial addresses exactly that gap: can we give less?

Anal cancer is genuinely rare. Taiwan's national cancer registry logs only around a hundred-odd new anal and anal canal cancers a year in its long-term trend series[1], a fraction of the colorectal caseload, yet its treatment has been settled for decades. Since the Nigro protocol emerged in the 1970s, concurrent chemoradiotherapy has displaced permanent colostomy as first-line care, and most patients keep a working anus. More than nine in ten anal squamous cell carcinomas trace back to persistent human papillomavirus infection, with higher risk among people who are immunosuppressed, living with HIV, who smoke, or who have a history of receptive anal intercourse. The cost of cure lands on the pelvis: diarrhoea, faecal urgency, perineal skin breakdown, sexual dysfunction. An Italian survivorship cohort followed for a median of over four years made the gap plain. Clinician-graded severe gastrointestinal toxicity affected only 6.7% of patients, yet roughly a third of survivors reported lasting bowel dysfunction and toileting dependence, and women under 70 scored markedly worse on sexual function[2]. A high cure rate is not the same thing as living well afterwards.

The UK's PLATO platform was built to close that gap. PLATO-ACT4, published in The Lancet Oncology in June 2025, randomised 160 patients with T1–T2 anal squamous cell carcinoma up to 4 cm and no nodal involvement. Both arms received the same concurrent mitomycin and capecitabine; only the radiotherapy dose differed: standard 50.4 Gy in 28 fractions, or reduced 41.4 Gy in 23 fractions[3]. Complete clinical response at six months was 92% with the reduced dose and 87% with the standard dose, while grade 3 or worse acute toxicity ran at 35% versus 46% — both pointing the same way[4]. Sexual function recovered over time in both arms and looked better in the reduced-dose group at six months. Tracking with the EORTC QLQ-ANL27, the anal cancer-specific quality-of-life module, most treatment-related symptoms had returned to baseline by six months, while patients with more advanced disease carried persistent bowel and functional impairment. Worth noting from the other end of the platform: ACT5, which tested dose escalation, bought no gain in either efficacy or quality of life. More is not the answer; less, in the right patients, might be.

What this means for a patient needs stating precisely. ACT4 is a phase 2 trial and its primary endpoint, three-year locoregional failure, is not yet mature, so dose reduction currently holds only for small, node-negative, early-stage tumours. Locally advanced or node-positive disease still gets the standard dose. Dose is set by the radiation oncologist from stage and tumour site; nobody should ask to cut, delay, or stop treatment on their own initiative. Is any of this usable in Taiwan? Yes. ASTRO's first clinical guideline on anal cancer radiotherapy, issued in February 2025, recommends intensity-modulated radiotherapy with daily image guidance over 3D conformal technique specifically to reduce toxicity[5], which is standard equipment in Taiwanese radiation oncology departments. On reimbursement: the computerised treatment planning and linear accelerator delivery that IMRT requires are National Health Insurance benefit items, whereas image guidance, arc therapy, tomotherapy and proton therapy are usually self-paid, and prices vary widely. Ask which add-ons are being proposed, and why, before the course starts. Chemotherapy coverage is decided indication by indication, so have your team confirm it. Further upstream, Taiwan's Health Promotion Administration extended publicly funded HPV vaccination to junior high school boys from September 2025 and names anal cancer among the cancers it prevents[6].
