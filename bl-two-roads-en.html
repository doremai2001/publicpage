MUSCLE-INVASIVE: TWO ROADS

Remove the bladder, or keep it

There is no completed randomised trial to compare the two roads in muscle-invasive bladder cancer. This article lays out the matched cohorts, the registry data and the three directions selection bias runs in, and gives you the questions to put to your doctor.

One number shows how hard this comparison is: in the most-quoted matched study, of every ten people who had a bladder removed at those centres over the same period, only about three had a tumour that would have allowed either road. The other seven were never in the comparison at all.

"Take it out, or keep it?" By the time that question reaches me, the patient usually already holds an answer, and only one. Muscle-invasive bladder cancer has two roads — radical cystectomy, or endoscopic resection followed by concurrent chemoradiotherapy, which is trimodality therapy — and the shape of the evidence decides how hard every number below can be stated. This article will not tell you which road to take. It gives you two things instead: who these studies actually compared, and the sentences to get out of your mouth at the next clinic visit.

My position first: the radiotherapy inside trimodality therapy is the part I deliver myself, and this topic argues that Taiwan under-uses bladder preservation — an argument that suits me, and you have every reason to discount it accordingly. So every comparison here comes with its primary source, and I have written down the things bladder preservation cannot do as well. Take it to the urology clinic for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

Nobody ever finished the trial that would settle this

The European Association of Urology's 2026 guideline puts it in its own words: "No successfully completed RCTs are available comparing the outcomes of TMT with RC." — no successfully completed randomised trial compares trimodality therapy with radical cystectomy[1]. The one trial that closed for want of patients was called SPARE: it took its first patient on 20 July 2007 and closed accrual on 12 February 2010 on the advice of the independent data monitoring committee and the trial steering committee, with 45 patients in total[2]. And there is one randomised trial from 1991 that did finish — DAVECA 8201 in Denmark, 183 patients, T2 to T4a — but what it compared was preoperative radiotherapy of 40 Gy plus planned cystectomy against full-dose radiotherapy of 60 Gy with cystectomy only for residual tumour; it accrued between 1983 and 1986 and gave no concurrent chemotherapy, so it is not the trimodality therapy we are talking about today[3].

Why SPARE could not recruit, the authors set out plainly: of 796 screened patients with T2–T3 N0M0 disease, 490 were ineligible (most could not have withstood all three treatments), 165 were approached and 45 consented (27%); among the 120 who declined, 51 preferred radiotherapy and 25 preferred surgery. Their own concluding sentence is that patients and clinicians alike held strong preferences and were unwilling to let a randomisation decide which road to take, and that clinicians lacked equipoise between the two in the first place[2].

The strongest comparison we have compared a group that had already been selected

Zlotta 2023 is a retrospective analysis from three North American university centres using propensity-score matching together with inverse probability weighting — propensity-score matching means assembling two groups whose recorded characteristics (age, stage, hydronephrosis and so on) look alike before comparing them; it holds down the differences you can see and cannot touch the ones nobody wrote down, and it is not a randomised trial[4]. The population was clinical T2–T4 N0M0 urothelial carcinoma treated between 2005 and 2017, 722 patients (440 cystectomy, 282 trimodality therapy), with median follow-up of 4.4 and 4.9 years.

The entry criteria are themselves the point, and belong with every quotation of the results: a solitary tumour under 7 cm, no hydronephrosis or unilateral only, no extensive or multifocal carcinoma in situ — in other words, people in whom the tumour allowed either road. After propensity-score matching, five-year metastasis-free survival was 74% against 74%, five-year cancer-specific survival 83% against 85% (p=0.057), and five-year disease-free survival 76% against 76%, with no difference detectable in any of them; salvage cystectomy was needed in 38 patients (13%)[4]. The sentence this licenses is "in this selected group of patients, these endpoints showed no detectable difference". It does not license "just as good".

One more number. Those 440 cystectomy patients were only 29% of all the radical cystectomies performed at the same centres over the same period[4] — of every ten people who had a bladder removed, roughly three had a tumour that would have allowed either road to begin with.

Selection bias runs in three directions, not one

The first direction favours preservation. In single-centre and matched cohorts, the trimodality patients are people who were picked, who were motivated, and whose tumours were favourable. A meta-analysis restricted to matched cohort studies (87 studies, 28,218 patients) found an overall survival hazard ratio of 1.05 (95% confidence interval 0.78–1.40) and a cancer-specific survival hazard ratio of 1.05 (0.69–1.58), with no difference detectable; the authors still close by writing that evidence from high-quality controlled trials is needed[5]. A hazard ratio divides the rate at which events occur in one group by the rate in the other, and a confidence interval means that if you ran the same study many times over, the answer would land in that range.

The second direction favours cystectomy. The National Cancer Database, 2004 to 2013, AJCC stage II to III, 32,300 patients: after propensity-score matching, the hazard ratio for death with concurrent chemoradiotherapy against cystectomy was 1.406 (1.235–1.601)[6]. SEER, 2010 to 2022, T2N0M0, 10,495 patients: after matching, five-year overall survival was 62.6% with cystectomy against 36.7% with trimodality therapy[7]. The reason sits in the columns a registry cannot see: the international multicentre RISC cohort measured it, and the people who took the preservation road were older, in worse performance status and carrying more comorbidity, all three with p values below 0.01[8].

The third direction also favours cystectomy, and it comes out of the statistical method itself. Robesti 2025 reconstructed the survival curves of 6 studies and 8,594 patients and found that 10 of the 12 reported endpoints carried an imbalance in loss to follow-up (informative censoring — the people who drop out early are not the same as the people who stay), overall tilting towards cystectomy; and after correction, cystectomy was still the better arm — five-year overall survival 42% against 30%[9]. I am putting that in because it counts against my position.

There is one more asymmetry. The people who had surgery come out with a pathological stage (of those 440, 44% were pT3–4 and 26% node-positive); the people who kept their bladder have only a clinical stage, and nobody knows what their true stage was[4].

"Not having surgery is the easier road" has to come apart

The radiotherapy inside trimodality therapy is 64 Gy in 32 fractions over six and a half weeks, or 55 Gy in 20 fractions over four weeks, with radiosensitising chemotherapy running alongside it[1]. In RTOG 0712, a randomised phase II trial, treatment-related grade 3–4 toxicity was 21/33 (64%) in one arm and 18/33 (55%) in the other, mostly haematological[10]; the matched-cohort meta-analysis gives a pooled estimate of 11.4% for acute grade 3 or higher toxicity[5]. And the end of the course is not the end of anything: none of the three guidelines gives a stopping point for cystoscopy afterwards[1].

Then comes the number that is easiest to skip. Pooling 73 studies, 9,110 patients, series followed for more than five years, the salvage cystectomy rate after trimodality therapy is about 19.2% — roughly one person in five ends up losing the bladder anyway; the range EAU gives for modern series is 10% to 30%, and it is that wide because the series differ in whom they selected, how closely they followed people, and whether concurrent chemotherapy was genuinely delivered[11][1]. The complication rate of that operation runs between 67% and 72%[11]. And when EAU quotes the patient-reported data from BC2001, what it writes is that about a third of patients still score below their pre-treatment level on bladder symptoms five years on[1]. The detail is in "The bladder you kept, and what it needs"; who is eligible and how it is delivered is in "Trimodality therapy: how it is done, and who it suits".

On the surgical side, the price is real and so is the reason

EAU's recommendation for T2–T4a N0M0 is a strong one: radical cystectomy[1], and the reason is not tradition. With this operation alone, about half of patients live beyond five years[1] — which is exactly why neoadjuvant chemotherapy exists ("Why chemotherapy comes before the operation"). In the column of costs, the decisive thing is not which percentage you pick but which cohort it came out of: 90-day mortality is 2.5% in the selected university-centre population[4], 4.7% pooled across 66 retrospective studies[12], 5.6% in the Dutch national registry of 9,287 patients[13], and in the randomised SWOG S1011 trial 7% (19/292) in the extended-lymphadenectomy arm against 2% (7/300) in the standard arm[14]. The pooled 90-day complication rate is 58.5%, ranging from 36.1% to 80.5% across studies[12]. Those figures, and what the days look like with each of the three urinary diversions, are in "Cystectomy and urinary diversion".

On quality of life, all we have is retrospective

A cross-sectional retrospective study at two hospitals: 226 people with cT2–cT4 non-metastatic disease who had been suitable for either road and were disease-free for at least two years, a 77% response rate, 173 respondents (64 trimodality therapy, 109 cystectomy), median follow-up 5.6 years. After propensity-score adjustment, the trimodality group scored 9.7 points higher on overall quality of life (out of 100, p=0.001), 8.7 to 32.1 points higher on sexual function and 14.8 points higher on body image, while urinary symptom scores were similar between the groups[15]. The authors' own concluding sentence is that long-term quality of life is good on both roads and that whether trimodality therapy is actually better needs prospective study to confirm[15]; EAU's comment on it is equally cautious[1]. There was also a two-year gap between the groups from diagnosis to filling in the questionnaire[15]. This can only be taken as a clue.

What the guidelines say, what the NHI pays for, and what I see in clinic

Start with the guidelines in their own words. EAU: "For all suitable candidates, both bladder-preserving techniques and RC should be discussed", and trimodality therapy "should be offered to all suitable candidates … and not only to patients with significant comorbidities"[1][4]. NICE NG2, recommendation 1.5.5: "Offer a choice of radical cystectomy or radiotherapy with a radiosensitiser", and 1.5.6 requires that the discussion include a urologist who performs radical cystectomy, a clinical oncologist and a clinical nurse specialist[16]. The guidelines have written "both roads get discussed" into themselves.

Then the Taiwanese side. In the NHI fee schedule, every component of both roads has its own listed item: 78011B radical cystectomy, 78013B with pelvic lymphadenectomy, 77032B ureteroileal cutaneous diversion; on the radiotherapy side 36018B simulation imaging and 36015B computerised treatment planning – complex, together with the field charges; on the resection side 78008C endoscopic resection of a bladder tumour[17]. The difference between the two roads is not whether they are paid for. But a full-text search of that same file for 「膀胱癌」 returns zero hits[17]: there is no listed item whose indication is bladder cancer, so how your own claim and any prior review will actually run is a question for the medical affairs office or your case manager — do not infer from "there is an item" that "it will go through".

Last, my own words, and please take them as one radiation oncologist's clinic observation rather than as a statistic: most of the people who reach my room to talk about trimodality therapy are people whose general condition has already ruled out an operation, and the people whose tumour would allow either road and who want to hear the second one out before deciding are, in what I see, fewer than I expected. How many people in Taiwan actually have trimodality therapy, I cannot find a denominator I could cite, so I am not going to give you a Taiwanese percentage, and I am not going to substitute another country's figure for it. And one honest addendum: internationally this proportion is rising — the SEER utilisation rate for T2N0M0 went from 11.9% in 2010 to 26.2% in 2022[7], and EAU itself writes that in modern series "approximately 25-30% of cystectomy candidates may be appropriate for bladder preservation"[1]. The two figures have different denominators and cannot be subtracted from one another, but the 2026 American registry is already close to the range the guideline names.

At the next clinic visit, get these sentences out

  • Given my tumour — how many, how many centimetres, extensive carcinoma in situ or not, hydronephrosis or not, whether it can be resected completely — do I count as someone for whom either road is open?

  • That survival figure you just quoted: which study is it from? Randomised, matched, or registry? How many people, followed for how long? Was that a selected group?

  • If I take the preservation road, how do you estimate my chance of eventually needing a salvage cystectomy? How does that operation differ from having surgery first?

  • If I take the surgical road, which urinary diversion will mine be, and why that one?

  • Has my case been through a multidisciplinary team discussion? (See also "Has my case been discussed by a team?" and "Asking for a second opinion, out loud".)

This decision will not resolve itself outside the clinic room; it needs people from both specialties to sit down and talk it through with you. Which sentences you need to get out, though, you can write down today.

From the physician

This article will not choose a road for you, and it should not be yours to choose alone. Before the next appointment, settle one thing: exactly where your tumour falls — how many, how many centimetres, extensive carcinoma in situ or not, hydronephrosis or not, whether it can be resected completely — and ask your doctor to say which kind of study the figures he quotes came from. If the answer is vague, take the references at the end of this article to urology and to radiation oncology and hear each of them out.
