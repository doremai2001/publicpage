DURING TREATMENT

Five treatments, fifteen, or twenty-five

The randomised trials of hypofractionation and of partial breast irradiation have now been read out to ten years. This one covers who fits how many treatments, what fewer treatments costs, and where the NHI provisions fix the number.

Breast Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 16 references
#Short Course#Decisions#Treatment Pathway

Five treatments in one week is not one thing: 26 Gy and 27 Gy give different ten-year cosmetic results. Two things called partial breast irradiation are not one thing either: once a day and twice a day differ a great deal in late toxicity. What decides the outcome is the detail of dose and technique, not the words "how many times".

At the first radiotherapy appointment, the question that comes out first is usually not about how well it works. It is how many times. Let me set out the shape of the answer straight away: five weeks and twenty-five treatments is no longer the default, but not everyone can be brought down to five in one week either. What decides it is not how many times you would like to come. It is whether the treated volume has to include the nodes, whether you have had reconstruction, whether the tumour is on the left or the right, and the shape and size of the breast.

Taking twenty-five out of the default slot

The two UK START trials enrolled women with completely excised invasive breast cancer (pathological stage pT1-3a, node-negative or one to three positive nodes, no distant metastases), after breast-conserving surgery and after mastectomy alike. START-B randomised 2,215 women to fifteen treatments over three weeks (40 Gy) or twenty-five over five weeks (50 Gy). At a median follow-up of 9.9 years, ten-year locoregional relapse was 4.3% against 5.5%, hazard ratio 0.77 (95% confidence interval 0.51–1.16, p=0.21) — a hazard ratio is the ratio of the rate at which events happen in the two groups, and 0.77 looks lower, but this range crosses 1, which means no difference was measured. Breast shrinkage, telangiectasia and breast oedema were all significantly less frequent in the fifteen-treatment arm[1]. The Canadian trial enrolled 1,234 women with clear margins and negative axillary nodes who had had breast-conserving surgery: ten-year local recurrence was 6.7% with twenty-five treatments and 6.2% with sixteen, an absolute difference of 0.5 percentage points (confidence interval −2.5 to 3.5); cosmetic outcome rated good or excellent at ten years was 71.3% against 69.8%[2]. ASTRO's 2018 whole-breast irradiation guideline puts 40 Gy in 15 fractions or 42.5 Gy in 16 fractions as the preferred schedules[3].

One week: there are ten-year data now

FAST-Forward enrolled 4,096 patients at 97 UK hospitals (pT1-3, node-negative or one to three positive nodes, after either breast-conserving surgery or mastectomy) and compared 40 Gy in 15 fractions with two one-week five-fraction schedules. At five years both five-fraction arms met non-inferiority — and non-inferiority does not mean "just as good", it means "the amount by which it came out worse did not exceed a margin agreed in advance"[4]. The ten-year results published in 2026, at a median follow-up of 10.1 years, give the ten-year cumulative incidence of ipsilateral breast relapse as 3.6% for 40 Gy, 2.9% for 27 Gy in 5 fractions and 2.1% for 26 Gy in 5 fractions; moderate or marked change in the breast or chest wall assessed by a clinician at ten years was 13.1%, 19.3% and 14.4% respectively[5].

Five fractions is not one thing: 26 Gy and 27 Gy do not produce the same normal-tissue result. Against 40 Gy, the overall odds ratio for normal tissue effects from one to five years was 1.55 (1.32–1.83) for 27 Gy and 1.12 (0.94–1.34, p=0.20) for 26 Gy[4][5]. The earlier FAST trial stretched five fractions to one a week: among 915 low-risk patients aged 50 or over with pT1-2 node-negative disease, the odds ratio for normal tissue effects was 2.12 for 30 Gy and 1.22 for 28.5 Gy[6]. So the real variable is not "how many times", it is the dose per treatment and the interval between them. Fewer treatments also does not mean a smaller dose or an easier ride — the dose per session is higher, and the peak of the skin reaction often lands one to two weeks after the course finishes, by which time you are no longer coming to the hospital every day.

One limitation belongs here as well: the FAST-Forward axillary substudy enrolled only 466 patients with a median follow-up of 7 years, and the authors write themselves that the sample size limited the precision of that subgroup estimate[5]. For anyone who needs the axilla or the supraclavicular fossa treated, the five-fraction evidence is not as thick as it is for the whole breast — who needs those areas treated is in "Should the axilla and supraclavicular area be treated too?".

The entry conditions for partial breast irradiation are finer than you would expect

Treating only the part of the breast where the tumour was, rather than the whole breast, comes with an explicit list of who qualifies. IMPORT LOW enrolled women aged 50 or over, after breast-conserving surgery, with unifocal invasive ductal carcinoma, a tumour of 3 cm or less, 0 to 3 positive nodes and margins of at least 2 mm, and excluded anyone with a previous other malignancy, a mastectomy, or neoadjuvant or concurrent chemotherapy[7]. The ten-year results published in 2025 covered 2,016 analysable women, median age 63, only 3% node-positive, median follow-up ten years: ipsilateral breast relapse was 2.8% with whole-breast and 3.0% with partial-breast treatment, an absolute difference of 0.16 percentage points (−1.28 to 2.89)[7]; the five-year results were published earlier[8]. The GEC-ESTRO selection recommendations updated in 2026 list several things outright as contraindications: being a BRCA1/2 carrier or under 40, a positive invasive margin, multicentric disease or disease larger than 30 mm, triple-negative disease, an extensive intraductal component or lymphovascular invasion, and axillary macrometastasis or an unknown axilla[9]. Everything in this section concerns a population that is mostly hormone-receptor-positive, early and largely node-negative; triple-negative and HER2-positive disease is not in it.

Two things called partial breast irradiation, two different results

RAPID enrolled 2,135 women and used 38.5 Gy in 10 fractions given twice a day: eight-year ipsilateral breast relapse was 3.0% against 2.8% for whole-breast treatment, meeting non-inferiority; the price was grade 2 or worse late toxicity in 32% against 13%, and an absolute difference of 17.7 percentage points in cosmesis rated fair or poor at year seven[10]. The Italian Florence trial used once-daily intensity-modulated radiotherapy, 30 Gy in 5 fractions, in 520 women with a median follow-up of 10.7 years: ten-year ipsilateral breast relapse 3.7% against 2.5% for whole breast (hazard ratio 1.56, 0.55–4.37, p=0.40), ten-year overall survival 91.9% in both arms, and acute toxicity, late toxicity and cosmesis all better than in the whole-breast arm[11]. NSABP B-39 enrolled 4,216 women: ten-year ipsilateral breast relapse was 4.6% with partial-breast and 3.9% with whole-breast treatment, and it did not meet the equivalence criterion set in advance; the authors' own conclusion supports whole-breast irradiation overall, but the ten-year absolute difference is less than 1 percentage point[12]. ASTRO's 2024 partial breast irradiation guideline accordingly recommends daily or every-other-day external beam over twice-daily treatment, on the grounds of late toxicity; the same guideline states that single-fraction intraoperative radiotherapy used on its own carries a higher ipsilateral breast recurrence rate than whole-breast irradiation[13]. So when you ask "can I have partial breast irradiation", ask in the same breath "with what technique, and how many treatments a day".

The boost: two twenty-year numbers that have to be read together

The EORTC boost trial randomised 5,318 patients with stage I or II disease, median follow-up 17.2 years. Twenty-year ipsilateral breast relapse was 12.0% with a boost and 16.4% without, a difference of 4.4 percentage points; but twenty-year overall survival was 59.7% against 61.1% (hazard ratio 1.05, p=0.323), no difference in survival. Over the same period, severe fibrosis rose from 1.8% to 5.2%[14]. Fibrosis is a long-term change in the breast you can see and feel, not a temporary one. The authors' conclusion is that the largest absolute benefit is in younger patients, and that for women over sixty at lower risk this part can usually be left out — a trade-off to be talked through, not a rule. Whether some people can leave out the radiotherapy altogether is a different article: "Omitting radiotherapy: every condition has to hold". The point of that one is not "it can be left out", it is that if one condition is missing the case does not hold.

Taiwan's payment codes write the number of treatments into the rule

The NHI has breast-specific bundled hypofractionation codes: 36022B is "with a local boost", a course of 20 treatments, and 36023B is "without a local boost", a course of 16; the stated indication is after breast-conserving surgery with the treated volume including the whole breast, and it says in so many words that the supraclavicular, axillary and internal mammary nodes are not included[15]. The NHI's own question-and-answer document, discussing the proportion paid, writes that a claim "must not exceed the number of fractions set in the fee schedule (20/16/5)", from which you can see that a five-fraction schedule exists inside the claiming framework[16]; but I have not obtained the official text giving that five-fraction item's full name and indication, so I am not going to write that five fractions for breast cancer is covered. The provisions have also been under revision for several years, and the contraindications originally listed under those two codes are being amended, so what actually applies to you is a question for your attending doctor and the hospital's medical affairs office. Whether partial breast irradiation has a reimbursement provision of its own in Taiwan, I likewise could not find a formal document I could cite — confirm that one with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office; I am not going to tell you it is covered or that it isn't.

Before the number of treatments is settled, copy down these three lines

First, copy these lines off your pathology report: the largest tumour diameter in centimetres, the margin in millimetres, how many nodes were taken and how many were positive, and the receptor and HER2 results. The entry criteria of every trial above are decided by those lines. Second, write down whether the tumour is on the left or the right, and whether you have had reconstruction and of what kind. Third, write one question out in full: "In my situation, will the treated area include the supraclavicular fossa or the axilla? If not, is fifteen treatments or five the better fit for me, and on what grounds?" The number of treatments is not yours to specify, but the reasoning is something you are entitled to ask to hear.

From the physician

Almost all of these trials enrolled operable early breast cancer, and a different population cannot be read across. At your next appointment, ask one thing: in my case, does the treated area include the supraclavicular fossa or the axilla? If not, is fifteen treatments or five the fit for me, and on what grounds?
