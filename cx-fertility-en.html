HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

Young patients: fertility and the ovaries

The threshold for uterus-sparing surgery is 2 cm and negative nodes; after radiotherapy the ovaries can be rescued but the uterus cannot. Every option's deadline is the same: before the first treatment begins.

Cervical Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 9 references
#Fertility#Decisions#Surgery

This article holds three honest sentences: only a minority qualify for the uterus-sparing operation; keeping the uterus does not mean a safe full-term pregnancy; ovarian transposition protects hormones, not the ability to carry a child. None of them is pleasant — and all of them are only useful if heard before treatment starts.

My position first: definitive chemoradiation and brachytherapy are the treatments I deliver every day. So every sentence about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it — not more warmly.

The first thing this article has to say is not medicine — it is time: every fertility-related option — uterus-sparing surgery, ovarian transposition, freezing eggs or embryos — must be settled before the first treatment begins. Once treatment starts, some doors close for good. So if having a child is still on your mind, even with only a flicker of uncertainty, say this sentence out loud at your first clinic visit after diagnosis: "I still want to have children." The earlier it is said, the more roads you have.

Uterus-sparing surgery exists — the threshold is the tumour, not your age

The fertility-sparing operation is called trachelectomy: removing the cervix and surrounding tissue while keeping the body of the uterus. Its threshold, in the 2023 guideline of the three European societies (ESGO/ESTRO/ESP), is written strictly: tumour under 2 cm, squamous or HPV-associated adenocarcinoma only, negative lymph nodes — and it must first go through counselling at an oncofertility centre and multidisciplinary team discussion[1]. Fertility-sparing treatment above 2 cm carries a significantly higher recurrence risk, and the guideline says outright it is not standard treatment[1]. The lymph nodes are the first gate: if nodal spread is found during surgery, the fertility-sparing plan has to be abandoned and the road turns to definitive chemoradiation — that is not anyone being heartless, it is the safety floor the guideline has written down[1].

The least welcome part first: most patients do not qualify

This road is a narrow one kept for the few. The literature reviews agree: fertility-sparing surgery applies only to a small group with good prognosis — small tumour, negative nodes, histology outside the high-risk types[2]; the threshold alone excludes the majority. And even those who qualify and reach the operating table face a variable: a systematic review of 47 studies and 2,566 patients undergoing radical trachelectomy found that in about 9%, the operation was converted to hysterectomy on the spot because margins or nodes failed — a possibility to know about, and to have talked through, before surgery[3]. For those who do qualify, the cancer outcomes are not poor: in the same review, the median recurrence rate reported across studies was 3.3% and the median 5-year overall survival 97.4% — those are the middle values across 47 studies laid side by side, not one cohort's number[3]. The strict conditions are precisely the premise of that report card. I put this section up front not to put out hope, but so you walk into that discussion with the right expectations.

Keeping the uterus is not the same as a safe, full-term pregnancy

This is the second honest thing I always say. A systematic review compiling 2,777 patients after fertility-sparing treatment and 944 pregnancies: a live birth rate of 70%, but a preterm birth rate of 38%, mostly linked to early rupture of membranes — and the larger the excision, the more preterm births[4]. The guideline's rules match: every pregnancy after trachelectomy is managed as high-risk, and anyone with a cervical cerclage delivers by caesarean only[1]. What fertility-sparing surgery buys is "you may try to become pregnant" — not "the pregnancy will go smoothly". Those are two different conversations, and in clinic they need having separately.

On the radiotherapy side: the ovaries can be saved, the uterus cannot

If your stage puts you on the road of definitive chemoradiation, you face a different and sharply drawn line. Pelvic external beam alone runs at 45 to 50 Gy, with brachytherapy adding more on top[1] — the uterus sits entirely inside the field. The literature's advice is that once the adult uterus has received more than 45 Gy, patients should be counselled against attempting pregnancy on their own[5]; and a uterus that has been through abdomino-pelvic radiotherapy, even when pregnancy occurs, shows increased risks of miscarriage, preterm birth, low birth weight and placental abnormalities[6]. This is not a question of whether the radiotherapy was delivered precisely enough — the uterus's tolerance of radiation is simply far below the dose needed to cure this disease. So keep this sentence: ovarian transposition protects hormones, not the ability to carry a pregnancy — moving the ovaries out of the field can prevent early menopause, but after pelvic radiotherapy it is the uterus that is the gate that will not open, and the damage to it is irreversible. Whether transposed ovaries keep their function, and how low the dose has to be kept to mean anything, I have written with the full numbers in "Moving the ovaries out of the field — 8 Gy is the line".

Freezing preserves a chance — and the reality of who pays

For those who want the chance of a biological child when the uterus cannot be kept, the road is freezing eggs or embryos before treatment — an established method in the 2025 ASCO guideline, listed alongside ovarian transposition and conservative gynaecological surgery[7]. How freezing works, what probability it actually buys, and how many days the process takes are the whole of the site's "Some options close when treatment starts" — including that article's thesis sentence: freezing preserves the chance, not the child. One piece of the Taiwanese reality first: the Ministry of Health and Welfare's medical fertility-preservation subsidy that took effect in September 2025 is limited to breast cancer and haematological cancers — cervical cancer is currently not on the list[8]; individual counties and cities have their own subsidy schemes with differing conditions — check with the public health bureau or your case manager. How much of the cost lands on you, ask before freezing is set in motion — not on egg-retrieval day.

Every road forks before the first treatment

The ASCO 2025 guideline's words: fertility preservation "should be discussed before cancer treatment begins"[7]; ESGO writes that ovarian transposition "should be discussed with the patient from the start"[1]. And one line runs the other way and needs drawing just as clearly: no fertility-preservation arrangement should delay or alter the cancer treatment itself — the guidelines' design is two conversations in parallel, not a choice between them[7][1]. You do not need to choose between saving your life and saving your fertility; what you need is for both teams to see you at the same time, before treatment starts.

Three documents to bring to the very first visit

First, the pathology or biopsy report: tumour size, histological type, lymphovascular invasion — the entire threshold for fertility-sparing surgery lives in those lines[1]. Second, the MRI report: tumour size and nodal status. Third, a referral or booking record for reproductive medicine — if you do not have one yet, ask the team to write it there and then; that step does not need to wait. One Taiwanese addition: radical trachelectomy (80211C) is listed in the item file of the NHI fee schedule[9]; confirm the coverage conditions and self-paid details with the medical affairs office. Then come back to the sentence this article opened with — "I still want to have children." In this whole course of treatment, it is the one sentence that least tolerates being said late.

From the physician

Say "I still want to have children" at the very first visit after diagnosis, and ask the team for a reproductive-medicine referral in the same breath. The national subsidy currently does not include cervical cancer; check county and city schemes and the costs with your case manager — not one of these questions is too early.
