WHAT COMES AFTER

Can radiotherapy grow a second tumour

What to do if your head was irradiated in childhood, and how small the absolute second-tumour risk is for an adult having radiosurgery — the numbers in the two directions cannot be used to cancel each other out.

Benign Brain Tumour Guide · What comes after · 14 references
#Late Effects#SBRT / SRS#Evidence#Decisions

One and the same radiation carries risks two orders of magnitude apart in two different groups of people. Three labels keep those groups apart — how many Gy, at what age, and across how long a follow-up. Take one label away and the figure ends up attached to the wrong reader.

This question comes out of two different mouths. One belongs to someone whose head was irradiated in childhood, for ringworm of the scalp or for a childhood cancer, who is now in their forties and has seen a meningioma on a health-check report. The other belongs to someone who has to decide tomorrow whether to have stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), and is afraid of growing a second tumour by having it. Same question; the answers those two people should walk away with are almost opposite. What tells you which one you are is the dose, the age at which it was given, and how long the follow-up ran.

How anybody decides a tumour was caused by radiation

The field uses the Cahan criteria, which come from an 11-case report of post-irradiation bone sarcoma published in 1948[1]. A 2026 systematic review sets out the current modified version as four clauses: the tumour arises within a previously irradiated field; there is a sufficiently long latency between the irradiation and the tumour appearing; the second tumour is histologically different from the original lesion; and the patient has no predisposing condition such as NF2, Li–Fraumeni or Gorlin syndrome[2].

The same paper admits the second clause is loose: there is no agreed lower bound on "a sufficiently long latency", and each study sets its own[2]. These four clauses exist to do epidemiological classification. So how is "was mine caused by the radiation?" decided? By the pathology and the complete treatment history, and that is the doctor's job.

The childhood-irradiation side: how big the numbers are

The two most quoted populations are both childhood exposures, but the doses are far apart.

The first is the Israeli tinea capitis cohort: 10,834 people who had X-ray treatment for ringworm of the scalp in the 1950s, with matched population and sibling control groups, a mean estimated brain dose of 1.5 Gy and a median follow-up of forty years. For benign meningioma the excess relative risk is 4.63 per gray (95% confidence interval 2.43 to 9.12) — an excess relative risk is the proportion by which the risk exceeds that of unexposed people, not your personal probability; the range in the bracket is where that estimate plausibly sits. The risk was still present more than thirty years after exposure[3].

The second is the childhood cancer survivor cohort, at much higher doses. Among 4,221 survivors who had received cranial radiotherapy, 169 developed 199 meningiomas between them; the median interval from primary cancer to meningioma was twenty-two years, and the cumulative incidence by age 40 was 5.6% (95% confidence interval 4.7 to 6.7%). The dose response is clear: taking 1.5 to 19.9 Gy as the reference, the hazard ratio for 30 Gy and above was 2.6 (1.6 to 4.2) — a hazard ratio compares the speed at which events happen in two groups, and above 1 means they happened faster[4].

Dose and age decide more than how high the risk is

A nested case-control study was done in the same database. Among 14,361 five-year survivors there were 116 subsequent central nervous system tumours, 66 of them meningiomas, occurring at a median of seventeen years after the original diagnosis. The odds ratio for radiation exposure and a subsequent meningioma was 9.94 (95% confidence interval 2.17 to 45.6), and the dose response was linear (excess relative risk slope 1.06 per gray); after adjusting for dose, which primary cancer it was and whether chemotherapy had been given were both unrelated to the risk[5]. An odds ratio is the ratio between two groups of "having it" against "not having it", and cannot be read as the probability multiplied several times over.

Pooling 237 studies and 1,809 cases gives this: median latency 22.7 years; the lower the dose, the longer the latency, in fact (median 36.3 years below 20 Gy, about 21 years at 20 Gy and above); median age at irradiation 8.3 years; grade 1 accounting for 69%; multiple tumours 17.3%; and of the 440 cases with recurrence data, about a quarter recurred, independently of dose[2]. This is a pooling of case reports and case series, not a meta-analysis, and the denominator is different for every variable.

This kind of meningioma does not behave like the usual kind

The 2024 international consensus says it plainly: radiation-induced meningiomas make up only 1 to 2% of all meningiomas, but they differ from the sporadic kind both biologically and clinically — more aggressive, more often multiple, and more resistant to standard treatment[6]. Be careful with that denominator: it says "1 to 2% of all meningiomas are radiation-induced", not "1 to 2% of irradiated people will grow one".

On management, the same consensus says standard treatment does not differ from the sporadic kind, with surgical resection first line for symptomatic lesions; when there are several, deal with the largest or the symptomatic one first, and active monitoring of the rest remains a safe initial strategy. But it writes the limits down in the same breath: even grade 1 ones can behave aggressively, a fair number are resistant to radiotherapy, and tumour control rates after radiosurgery are overall lower than in the sporadic kind[6].

The symptom side has denominators too. In the same cohort, 20% (30/149) developed at least one new neurological symptom within six months either side of the meningioma diagnosis, most commonly seizures (8.3%) and focal neurological deficit (7.1%); over a median 72 months of follow-up after diagnosis, 22 (13%) died, 6 of them from a cause attributed to the meningioma[4].

If you are the person whose head was irradiated as a child

The most useful thing first: dig out the treatment summary from back then — where it was aimed, how many Gy, at what age — and take it to your consultant. Then something that is often misread: the current international consensus states in as many words that the evidence is insufficient to support a standardised screening protocol, germline genetic testing and routine neuroimaging included, even in high-risk groups. So this is not "go and demand a whole-body screen"; it is taking the dose and the site to a doctor and asking them to set an individualised follow-up for you. The nearest thing to a recommendation the consensus offers is this: since these meningiomas may be diagnosed forty years after the original treatment, regular interval imaging, at closer intervals for people who had intensive treatment, may be warranted[6]. The verb is "may be warranted", not "should".

More practical still is the section of NG99 on monitoring for late effects of treatment. It lists the effects that appear months to years after treatment, with second tumours among them, alongside cataract, cognitive decline, seizures, hearing loss, hypopituitarism and stroke. The actions it gives the clinical side are concrete: assess the risk of late effects at the end of treatment, write it into a written treatment summary and explain it to the person; check blood pressure, lipids and glycated haemoglobin regularly in those at risk of stroke; check endocrine function regularly where the dose may have affected the pituitary; and refer those at risk of visual or hearing problems for ophthalmology and audiology[7].

The sentence to take into clinic is: "given this dose and this site, what should I be followed for regularly?" And one thing that does not have to wait for a doctor to raise it: a new or changing neurological symptom is itself a reason to come back, without waiting for the scheduled date. Whether Taiwan has official second-tumour follow-up recommendations, I could not find a document I could cite; how to arrange it is a question for your own consultant.

The adult radiosurgery side: absolute risk and benefit in the same frame

So what about the person currently weighing up radiosurgery? The numbers in this section are not in the same order of magnitude as the ones above, so please read it all the way through. An international cohort from five centres collected 4,905 people treated with radiosurgery between 1987 and 2011, followed for at least five years and with no previous irradiation (meningioma 1,490, vestibular schwannoma — also called acoustic neuroma — 1,011, pituitary adenoma 641), median follow-up 8.1 years; the overall incidence was 6.80 per 100,000 person-years (95% confidence interval 1.73 to 18.50), with a ten-year cumulative incidence of 0.00045%. The authors conclude that under long-term follow-up the risk remains very low, comparable to the general population's risk of developing a primary central nervous system tumour[8]. In a UK cohort of about 5,000 people and 30,000 person-years, just one new astrocytoma appeared, against a national-incidence expectation of 2.47 cases[9]. Another single-centre cohort is much smaller but followed for longer: 273 people who had radiosurgery for benign central nervous system lesions, median follow-up eleven years, 3,216 person-years in total, 0 radiation-associated malignancies and 2 malignant transformations, with a risk of 0.4% at five, ten and fifteen years (0.05 to 2.6%)[10]. There is a line at guideline level that can be quoted directly as well: the new level III recommendation in the 2026 stereotactic radiosurgery guideline update says that adults with sporadic vestibular schwannoma undergoing radiosurgery should be counselled that radiosurgery does not increase the number of second malignancies above the rate expected in the general population[11]. Why people go down this road at all is written more clearly in the NHI clause than in any efficacy figure: the conditions for 37028B and 37029B list, word for word, "has previously undergone craniotomy but has residual tumour or tumour recurrence", "where craniotomy might cause neurological injury or carries a high risk", and "has severe cardiopulmonary or other medical disease making the patient unsuitable for invasive surgery or general anaesthesia"[12]. What goes on the scales opposite this risk is what each of those three situations costs.

Vestibular schwannoma has a closer-fitting denominator still. In one institution's data with a median follow-up of twelve years, malignant transformation occurred in 2 of 1,061 vestibular schwannomas treated with radiosurgery (0.19%); converted to 12,940 lesion-years, that is an annual risk of 0.016%, and there were 0 cases in the first five years[13]. The baseline has to sit beside it: people who never had radiation carry a risk anyway — in SEER, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumours of the eighth cranial nerve in people with no radiation work out at roughly 1 for every 1,041 acoustic neuromas[14]. These two numbers belong on the same scales as those three situations in the paragraph above: someone with residual tumour after an operation, or for whom craniotomy is too risky, is weighing up what the tumour in front of them is going to do, not this two-in-a-thousand box.

Two things must not be dropped. One, this is not zero: that cohort of more than four thousand nine hundred people did contain 2 malignant transformations and 1 malignancy inside the treated field[8]. Two, the same 2026 guideline carries a recommendation pointing the other way: in adults with intracanalicular or sub-2 cm sporadic vestibular schwannoma, radiosurgery should not be recommended as superior to observation alone for hearing preservation[11]. A very small second-tumour risk does not mean radiosurgery is worth it for everyone — that question belongs to "Watching, radiosurgery, surgery".

The two sets of numbers cannot cancel each other out

What has been described here are two different groups of people: on one side children, wide treatment fields, decades-old technique, followed for forty years; on the other adults, small volumes, a single high dose, and a median follow-up of only about eight years. Using that 0.00045% to reassure someone whose head was irradiated in childhood is wrong; using that 5.6% to talk someone out of radiosurgery is equally wrong. The cohort of 4,905 people deliberately excluded anyone previously irradiated, so it cannot answer "what happens if somewhere already irradiated is irradiated again" — that one is on this site, in "Can somewhere already irradiated be treated again?".

The next step differs for the two. If your head was irradiated as a child, take the dose and the site and ask "what should I be followed for regularly". If you are weighing up radiosurgery, change the question to the one that actually needs comparing: against the other options, what does this road buy and what does it cost in tumour control, in hearing and in the facial nerve — the second tumour is a very small box on that table. Both kinds of reader usually get stuck on the same thing, which is not sleeping either side of a scan; "Treatment ended. Why am I more afraid?" on this site was written for that.

From the physician

Both kinds of reader will land on this article, and they should not take the same thing away from it. For the one whose head was irradiated as a child, the decades-old record dug out of a drawer is worth more than any statistic. For the one deciding tomorrow, the boxes worth spending time on are not this one — they are hearing and neurological function.
