Watching is a form of treatment

Watching names the imaging, the interval, what gets measured alongside and the conditions that stop it — and that table is different for each of the three tumours; in the vestibular schwannoma box it is also paid for in hearing.

#Active Surveillance#Imaging#Follow-up#Decisions

Ask how your "growth" was measured. Volume, longest diameter, events per 100 person-years: three tumours, three different rulers. The numbers cannot be set against each other, and someone else's result cannot be laid over yours.

"Let's watch it for now" is very easily heard in clinic as "the doctor thinks it's nothing". That is not what it means. Watching is a prescription: it names the imaging, the interval, the things to be measured alongside, and the conditions that stop it. The prescription looks different for each of the three tumours.

Three guidelines, three different timetables

Put three sentences down untouched, side by side, and you can see they are not interchangeable. For meningioma, the European Association of Neuro-Oncology writes that by current consensus, for a suspected or grade 1 meningioma, "an annual MRI is recommended for five years, after which the interval may be doubled", and notes that this is a good clinical practice point[1]. For vestibular schwannoma (also called acoustic neuroma), the American Congress of Neurological Surgeons' 2018 imaging guideline writes that "MRI should be performed annually for five years, after which the interval may be lengthened provided the tumour is stable", as a level 3 recommendation[2]; there is a 2026 update of that guideline with different wording and different intervals, and the two versions are set out side by side in "How follow-up is scheduled, and when it stops". For pituitary adenoma, the Endocrine Society writes this for an incidentaloma that does not meet the criteria for surgery: "MRI at 6 months for a macroadenoma and at one year for a microadenoma, thereafter progressively less frequently if the size is unchanged; visual field testing at 6 months and annually for those whose lesion abuts or compresses the optic nerves and chiasm; endocrine testing at 6 months and annually for macroadenomas"[3].

The strength of the evidence has to be said plainly. The meningioma line is expert consensus[1]; the relevant recommendations in that vestibular schwannoma guideline set are all level 3, the lowest, and the authors wrote in their own executive summary that "several well-constructed questions and subsequent searches did not yield information sufficient to make meaningful and supportable recommendations"[4]. There is no randomised trial holding these intervals up, and your doctor will adjust them to your situation.

Meningioma: change the ruler and the answer turns round

Measured by volume: a prospective study followed 64 people with an incidental meningioma for five years and defined growth as a volume 15% above baseline — 48 of them (75%) grew in volume. Switch to a composite endpoint and it looks completely different: a cohort across 33 centres in 15 countries, 1,248 people and 1,010 untreated meningiomas with a median follow-up of 61 months, using "growth plus symptoms plus meningioma-related death plus loss of the window for curative treatment" as its endpoint, found 88.1% progression-free at five years and 85.7% at ten[5][6].

Two more things come out of the volume study. First, there were five growth patterns, of which the S-shaped one accounted for 35%, and more than six in ten were self-limiting[5] — so projecting this year's rate ten years forward is wrong as a method, never mind as an answer. Second, growth rate goes with age: under 55, 0.10 cubic centimetres a year; 55 to 75, 0.24; over 75, 0.85[5]. The older you are the faster it grows — and the older you are the more likely you are to leave for some other reason first. In that same cohort, 383 people (40.5%) died of something else while the meningioma was quiet and untreated[6]. The two sentences are only complete together.

Does watching let things get out of hand, then? A meta-analysis — the pooling of several studies into one estimate — of 20 studies and 2,130 people, together with its 2019 correction, gives this: 8.4% developed symptoms during active monitoring (95% confidence interval 2.8 to 16.7% — run the same study many times and the answer would be expected to land in that range), and 24.8% went on to an intervention; "most patients who progressed did so within five years of diagnosis"[7][8].

Vestibular schwannoma: "growth" means more than 2 mm on the longest diameter

Here the ruler changes again. Of 3,637 sporadic vestibular schwannomas in the Danish national series from 1976 to 2015, 2,312 people were observed, with a mean follow-up of 7.33 years; growth was defined as "an increase in linear measurement of more than 2 mm", following the Tokyo 2001 consensus[9].

Split by location, the proportion that had grown by five years after diagnosis was 21% for intracanalicular tumours and 37% for those extending into the cerebellopontine angle; at ten years, 25% and 42%; growth occurred "primarily in the first five years". Four hundred and thirty-four people (19%) crossed over to active treatment because of growth, and where they went differed as well: intracanalicular tumours were mostly kept under observation, and those extending into the cerebellopontine angle mostly went to surgery[9].

"It grew" does not mean "it will keep growing". A four-centre study of 592 patients in whom growth had already been measured but who continued to be observed found that, counting from the first detection of growth, the proportion with no further growth within five years was 32% for intracanalicular tumours and 22% for cerebellopontine angle ones; the authors' conclusion is that "growth detected during observation does not necessarily portend future growth … tolerating some growth is defensible in appropriately selected patients"[10]. That qualifier cannot be dropped: who is appropriately selected is chosen by the doctor.

Watching a vestibular schwannoma has a price, and the price is hearing

A study of 156 intracanalicular vestibular schwannomas managed conservatively, mean follow-up 9.5 years: the pure-tone average deteriorated from 51 dB to 72 dB, and the word recognition score fell from 60% to 34%; the proportion with AAO-HNS class A hearing fell from 19% to 3%. In the same group, 37% of tumours grew and conservative management failed in 15%[11].

The same 156 people, the same follow-up, a different scoring system, and the answer moves by 24 percentage points: counted as AAO-HNS class A or B, serviceable hearing preservation was 34%; counted as class I or II on the word recognition score, it was 58%[11]. So whenever you see a "preservation rate", ask one question first: which ruler was that measured with? The American Congress of Neurological Surgeons' guideline puts the probability of retaining serviceable hearing on observation at greater than 75% to 100% at two years, greater than 50% to 75% at five years, and only greater than 25% to 50% at ten, and notes that the most consistent predictors are the hearing before treatment and the tumour not growing[12]. How the other two roads — stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) and surgery — compare is in "Watching, radiosurgery, surgery".

Watching a pituitary tumour means measuring three things

This box changes the ruler once more: the unit is events per 100 person-years. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 studies with follow-up of three to fifteen years put the rate of lesion progression at 12.5 per 100 person-years for macroadenomas (95% confidence interval 7.9 to 17.2), 5.7 for solid lesions, 3.3 for microadenomas and 0.05 for cystic lesions; new endocrine dysfunction overall was 2.4; pituitary apoplexy and worsening visual field deficits were both rare. The authors rated the quality of this body of evidence as "very low" and noted substantial heterogeneity[13].

Watching a pituitary tumour is not imaging alone; that guideline requires visual field testing and endocrine testing alongside it[3]. The two emergencies are in "Pituitary apoplexy, and not enough hormone" — read it once before you need it.

What stops the watching, and what loosens the follow-up

The conditions for stopping observation and moving to treatment have a different source for each tumour: for meningioma it is "symptomatic or growing"[1]; for vestibular schwannoma it is measured growth, after which the route divides by location[9]; for a pituitary tumour it is the Endocrine Society's surgical list (visual field deficit, visual abnormality or neurological compromise from compression, a lesion abutting the optic pathway, pituitary apoplexy with visual disturbance, and a hypersecreting tumour other than a prolactinoma)[3].

When things can be loosened is a question the meningioma box has research on. A prognostic model built on 441 people showed that after five years of follow-up the risk of progression plateaued in every risk group; and that people with an age-adjusted Charlson comorbidity index of 6 or more (an 80-year-old with chronic kidney disease, for instance) were, within five years of diagnosis, "15 times more likely to die of another cause than to undergo intervention, irrespective of risk group". The authors' conclusion is that "the benefit of intensive monitoring is small for low-risk and older patients with comorbidity", and that risk-stratified follow-up can also reduce anxiety[14]. The external validation in those 1,248 people points the same way, describing the tool's purpose as sorting people into early intervention, continued monitoring, or safe discharge from the clinic[6].

Let me put this one bluntly: that is a decision the doctor makes after stratifying you with a tool, not something you can decide for yourself — so the thing worth saying out loud in clinic is, for my tumour, which tool did you use and which items did you look at to put me in this layer? As for how many unchanging years are enough to stop scanning altogether, none of those three guidelines says[1][2][3]. When it stops is in "How follow-up is scheduled, and when it stops".

In Taiwan, stereotactic radiosurgery is not the default first road

This one changes your situation in Taiwan directly, so I am quoting the provision; the English is my rendering of it. The indications for items 37028B and 37029B in the National Health Insurance fee schedule do name "acoustic neuroma, meningioma, pituitary tumour, craniopharyngioma", but the same clause goes on to say "and one of the following conditions must be met": "A. Has previously undergone craniotomy but has residual tumour or tumour recurrence. B. Craniotomy would risk neurological injury or carry high risk. C. Has severe cardiopulmonary disease or other medical illness making invasive surgery or general anaesthesia unsuitable. … F. Unilateral intracranial cerebellopontine angle acoustic neuroma less than 2.5 cm in width (excluding the internal auditory canal)." And "every case requires prior case-by-case application to the insurer"[15].

In plain words: of the six conditions the clause lists (A to F), most of them describe a situation in which "craniotomy is not workable or is too risky" — but F does not. A unilateral intracranial cerebellopontine angle acoustic neuroma under 2.5 cm in width falls inside the provision on size alone, with no previous operation. So you cannot say in one sentence that the provision places radiosurgery after surgery; it lists six situations, and every case still requires prior case-by-case application to the insurer. How radiosurgery itself is delivered is in "One session, or fractionated". As for how often follow-up MRI may be done, I could not find a listed item for these three tumours anywhere in the schedule[16] — and not finding a listed item is not the same as there being no cover. How to claim it is a question for the medical affairs office.

Turn "we'll watch it" into a timetable you can actually read

Before you leave the consulting room, get these four boxes filled in:

  1. Which month the next scan is, and which sequence.

  2. What gets measured alongside it: hearing, visual fields, hormone bloods — write down the one that applies to you.

  3. What should bring you back early: ask your doctor to name the specific items for your tumour, not "come back if you feel unwell".

  4. This visit's measurement: how many millimetres of longest diameter or how many cubic centimetres of volume, and which method was used. Without it, next time there is nothing to compare against.

Watching is not leaving the problem alone. It is putting the problem in the diary. What I worry about is not the thing growing; it is nobody looking at it.

From the physician

Before you leave the consulting room, write down four things: which month the next scan is, what gets measured alongside it, what should bring you back early, and this visit's measurement together with the method used. Whether the watching continues is the doctor's decision after stratifying you with a tool — but writing the timetable down clearly is something you can do yourself.
