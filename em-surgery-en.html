DIAGNOSIS AND STAGING

What the operation removes, and what happens to the lymph nodes

Total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy is the standard extent; the nodes are the hard part — taking them all out has not made anyone live longer, and the sentinel procedure misses roughly 3% to 4% of the patients who do have nodal metastases.

Endometrial Cancer Guide · Diagnosis and staging · 16 references
#Hysterectomy#Surgery#Sentinel Node#Staging

Minimally invasive surgery had the brakes put on it by a randomised trial in cervical cancer, while in this cancer two randomised trials came out level — different cancer, different operation, not a contradiction. There is a still more counter-intuitive conclusion inside: clearing out the pelvic nodes has not made anyone live longer.

Endometrial cancer runs in the opposite order from a lot of other cancers: most people have the operation first and then decide, with the pathology report in hand, whether anything else is needed. So what the operation did and did not do goes on to shape every decision that follows. The surgery is not mine — I am the department that picks things up afterwards with radiotherapy — so I have attached the primary source to every section, for you to take back to the gynaecological oncology clinic and ask about.

What the standard operation takes out

The FIGO 2023 staging document describes it this way: the standard surgery for early endometrial cancer is total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy by a minimally invasive laparoscopic approach; for serous and undifferentiated carcinoma and carcinosarcoma, infracolic omentectomy is added as well, because the risk of microscopic omental metastases is high[1]. FIGO's 2025 report on cancer of the uterine corpus goes into more detail: the recommended procedure is extra-fascial total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and removal of the adnexa is recommended even if the tubes and ovaries look normal; the same document states that removal of the vaginal cuff is not advised, and that in most cases there is no benefit from excising parametrial tissue[2].

Peritoneal washings are still taken — FIGO's 2025 report still lists peritoneal evaluation and washings when it describes the current procedure[2] — it is only that a positive result does not change the stage, which is dealt with in "The staging changed; your treatment does not change with it".

Minimally invasive against open surgery: in this cancer the answer runs the other way from cervical cancer

Two randomised trials. The GOG's LAP2 enrolled 2,616 patients with clinical stage I to IIA cancer of the uterine corpus — that sub-stage IIA exists only in the FIGO 1988 version — 1,696 to laparoscopy against 920 to laparotomy, with both arms having complete pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy. That the staging version is older than today's is covered in "The staging changed; your treatment does not change with it". The short-term gains are real: moderate to severe post-operative adverse events 14% against 21%, and hospital stay longer than two days in 52% against 94%[3]. So is the cost: 25.8% of the laparoscopy patients were converted to laparotomy during the operation, most often because of poor visibility, and the proportion in whom nodes could not be obtained was twice that of laparotomy (8% against 4%)[3].

The survival result takes two sentences, not one. LAP2's pre-set threshold was that the recurrence risk should not rise by more than 40% for non-inferiority to be declared — what a non-inferiority design sets out to prove is that the shortfall does not exceed a line agreed in advance, not that the two are equally good. The result was a hazard ratio of 1.14 (90% lower bound 0.92, 95% upper bound 1.46), which did not meet the line the trial had set itself[4]. A hazard ratio, in plain language, divides how fast events occur in one group by how fast they occur in the other. But the absolute numbers in the same report are very small: 3-year recurrence 11.4% against 10.2%, an absolute difference the trial reports as under 1.2 percentage points; 5-year overall survival was 89.8% in both arms[4]. Giving only the first half is misleading; so is giving only the second. The LACE trial, run in Australia, New Zealand and Hong Kong, used an equivalence design: 760 patients with stage I endometrioid cancer (again, a version predating FIGO 2023) had 4.5-year disease-free survival of 81.6% against 81.3%, a difference of 0.3 percentage points (95% confidence interval −5.5% to 6.1%), meeting the pre-set definition of equivalence[5].

Look carefully at the comparison. Also minimally invasive, also in the pelvis, and cervical cancer's LACC trial reached the opposite conclusion: among 631 patients with early cervical cancer, 4.5-year disease-free survival after minimally invasive radical hysterectomy was 86.0% against 96.5% after open surgery, a difference of 10.6 percentage points[6]. Different population, different tumour, different operation — that was radical hysterectomy, not ordinary total hysterectomy. So "minimally invasive surgery hit the brakes in cervical cancer" cannot be carried over to endometrial cancer, and that question has an article of its own, "Where minimally invasive surgery hit the brakes". As for the robot, FIGO's 2025 report says in its own words that safety is now well documented, but any survival benefit "remains to be proven"[2].

Sentinel lymph nodes: what you gain, and what gets missed

A sentinel node procedure finds the few nodes cancer cells would reach first and sends only those to pathology. The American FIRES study, across 10 centres, enrolled 385 patients, of whom 340 went on to complete lymphadenectomy as the comparator: at least one sentinel node was mapped in 86%, sensitivity was 97.2% (85.0 to 100), and negative predictive value 99.6%. The authors themselves wrote the line worth remembering — sentinel lymph node biopsy "will not identify metastases in 3% of patients with node-positive disease"[7].

There are data in high-grade disease too. Canada's SENTOR enrolled 156 patients, 126 of them with high-grade endometrial cancer: the per-patient mapping rate was 97.4% and bilateral mapping 77.6%, with sensitivity 96%, false-negative rate 4% and negative predictive value 99%; and among the 27 node-positive patients, 7 (26%) had metastases lying outside the boundaries of conventional pelvic lymphadenectomy or detectable only with immunohistochemistry[8]. The less flattering result belongs here as well: France's SENTI-ENDO had a per-patient sensitivity of only 84%, and all 3 false negatives were non-endometrioid endometrial cancers[9]. Histological type affects how much this test can be relied on.

The choice of dye has a randomised trial behind it. FILM injected both dyes into the same patient; among the 163 evaluable patients and 485 nodes, the green dye, indocyanine green (ICG), found 471 (97%) and the blue dye 226 (47%), a difference of 50 percentage points; and the proportion of what each found that pathology confirmed to be lymphatic tissue did not differ[10].

Taking all the nodes out has not made anyone live longer

Two randomised trials, and they agree. The British-led ASTEC enrolled 1,408 patients, randomised to standard surgery or standard surgery plus pelvic lymphadenectomy: the hazard ratio for overall survival was 1.16 (0.87 to 1.54), an absolute difference at 5 years of 1 percentage point; the hazard ratio for recurrence-free survival was 1.35 (1.06 to 1.73), pointing if anything towards not doing the lymphadenectomy, and after adjustment for baseline and pathology neither was significant. The conclusion, in the authors' words, is that pelvic lymphadenectomy cannot be recommended as routine procedure for therapeutic purposes[11]. The Italian trial of 514 patients found the same: a median of 30 nodes removed against 0, the proportion in whom nodal metastases were found did rise from 3.2% to 13.3%, and complications occurred in 81 patients against 34 — but 5-year disease-free survival was 81.0% against 81.7% and 5-year overall survival 85.9% against 90.0%, so there was no benefit[12].

There is only one correct reading: what these two trials proved is that the act of clearing the nodes has no therapeutic effect, not that nodal status is unimportant — ASTEC's post-operative radiotherapy was randomised separately and independently of the nodal findings, so it never answered the question of whether knowing the nodal status is useful[11]. The sentinel node procedure is the compromise sought between "getting the staging information" and "cutting out less". One sentence from FIGO's 2025 report sums up where things stand: since the development of sentinel lymph node biopsy, the indications for full surgical staging lymphadenectomy are scarce[2]. As for swelling in the legs: it is less common after switching to sentinel nodes, and how much less depends on who did the measuring and how, which is a whole article — "Lower-limb lymphoedema: prevention and treatment" — and I am giving no figures here.

Should the ovaries stay

This is a different question from keeping the uterus in order to have a child (that one is in "Keeping the uterus to have a child: what it takes"). The available evidence is all retrospective. In the SEER database from 1988 to 2004, among 3,269 patients aged 45 or under with FIGO 1988 stage I disease, 402 (12%) kept their ovaries: multivariable analysis showed no effect of ovarian preservation on cancer-specific survival (hazard ratio 0.58, 0.14 to 2.44) or overall survival (0.68, 0.34 to 1.35), and the authors' wording is "may be safe"[13]. There is a figure in a higher-risk population as well: among 7,230 patients aged under 50 with FIGO 2009 stage I to II grade 2 endometrioid or high-risk histological types, 295 (4.1%) kept their ovaries, and after weighting and adjustment 5-year survival was 94.2% against 94.4%, hazard ratio 1.05 (0.57 to 1.94)[14].

The guideline's wording is guarded: in premenopausal women with low-grade, early-stage disease, ovarian preservation "could be considered"[2]. Could be considered — not something you can insist on, and not something that must be removed.

The Taiwan side: what I found in the provisions, and what I did not

What I found first. The National Health Insurance fee schedule has item 63017B, "sentinel lymph node excision surgery — unilateral", 12,656 points, and its indication reads verbatim (my translation of the clause) "malignant breast cancer, melanoma and squamous cell carcinoma"[15]. ICG appears in the same fee schedule under only two items: 09093B, ICG dye test of liver function, and 23813B, ICG fundus angiography[15]. In the Taiwan FDA's western medicine licence records, there is currently only one valid licence for injectable ICG — 衛署藥輸字第018254號, "Diagnogreen Injection", whose indication reads verbatim (my translation of the clause) "diagnostic agent for hepatic and circulatory function"[16]. Lymphatic mapping is not among the indications registered on that licence. This is "I looked and the content is not there", not "I did not look".

Now what I did not find: searching the full text of the fee schedule, I could not find a listed item for sentinel lymph nodes in endometrial cancer, and there is no dedicated listed item for peritoneal washing cytology either[15]. Not finding a listed item means not finding one — it does not mean there is no cover, and it does not mean there is; ask the medical affairs office or your case manager. Internationally the sentinel node procedure is already the mainstream approach; practice differs between hospitals in Taiwan, so put this question directly to your own gynaecological oncologist.

Get the answers to these before you sign the consent form

  • "What is this operation taking out? Does the omentum come out too?" The histological type decides the answer[1].

  • "Minimally invasive or open? If it has to be converted during the operation, what is the commonest reason?" In LAP2 a quarter of patients were converted[3].

  • "What is being done about the nodes — sentinel, full lymphadenectomy, or nothing? With my histological type, how reliable do you think the sentinel procedure is?"

  • "I have not been through the menopause — is there any chance of keeping my ovaries? If not, what is the reason?"

One more thing: ask the nursing station for a copy of the operation note and the pathology report when you are discharged. Every later discussion about radiotherapy or chemotherapy turns on those two pieces of paper.
