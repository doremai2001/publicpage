WHEN YOU ARE THE FAMILY

When he cannot decide, who decides

Taiwan's only ranked list of relatives sits in Hospice Palliative Care Act Article 7, and governs terminal life-sustaining treatment alone.

Situation Guides · When You Are the Family · 8 references
#Law & rights#Decisions#Family#How to ask

Some things only he can write down, and only while he still can. There are numbers behind that: the person deciding on the patient's behalf predicted his treatment preferences correctly 68% of the time, and prior discussion of preferences did not improve it. That is not saying they cannot be trusted; it is saying guesswork cannot carry this.

"He's unconscious — who signs now?" I have heard that in the emergency department and outside the ward many times, and it is not one question but four: the health care agent, the advance decision, the ranked family consent in statute, and decision-making capacity. The four have different legal sources, conditions and reach, and mixing them up causes harm.

First: the health care agent conveys, he does not decide

Article 3, subparagraph 5 of the Patient Right to Autonomy Act (病人自主權利法) defines it: "health care agent (醫療委任代理人): a person appointed in writing by the declarant to express the declarant's wishes on his behalf when the declarant is comatose or unable to express his wishes clearly." Article 10 I requires him to be an adult with legal capacity and to consent in writing; Article 10 II excludes anyone who would gain from the declarant's death, heirs excepted[1].

His powers are three, written tightly: "1. To receive the information under Article 5. 2. To sign the consent form under Article 6. 3. To express the patient's medical wishes on his behalf in accordance with the content of the patient's advance decision." Where there are two or more agents each may act alone, and an agent handling the appointed business is to produce identification[1].

Note the limit in item 3: on the patient's behalf, in accordance with the content of the advance decision. He expresses, he does not decide; where the patient never signed an advance decision, item 3 has no content to convey — "I am the health care agent, so I can decide for him whether he is treated" is a misunderstanding. Separately, Article 5 II of the Hospice Palliative Care Act (安寧緩和醫療條例) also has a 醫療委任代理人, and that one exists to sign the hospice palliative care declaration (意願書) on the patient's behalf; the powers are different[2].

Second: the advance decision is the patient's document, and the family's place is the consultation

Article 8 I: a person with full legal capacity may make an advance decision (預立醫療決定), and may withdraw or vary it in writing at any time. The three requirements in Article 9 I: an advance care planning consultation (預立醫療照護諮商) provided by a medical institution and sealed by it in evidence; notarisation, or witnessing by two or more people with full legal capacity; and registration on the National Health Insurance card. Paragraph II places the family: the declarant, at least one relative within the second degree of kinship, and the health care agent shall take part in the consultation (that relative may be excused on death, disappearance or special cause)[1].

It does not start itself once signed. Article 14 I lists five clinical conditions — terminal illness, irreversible coma, a permanent vegetative state, severe dementia, and other circumstances announced by the central competent authority. Paragraph II is the sentence families most need to know: "Each of the conditions in the preceding paragraph shall be diagnosed by two physicians holding the relevant specialist qualification, and confirmed by at least two consultations with the palliative care team." Article 15 adds one more: for patients under Article 14 I subparagraph 1 (terminal illness) and subparagraph 5, before an advance decision is put into effect, its content and scope shall be confirmed with the declarant while he still has the capacity to form an intention[1] — cancer patients usually fall under subparagraph 1, so that check does apply to you.

Third: the ranked family consent exists in one place, and governs one thing

Taiwanese law does contain a ranked list of relatives. It sits in Article 7, paragraphs III to VI of the Hospice Palliative Care Act, and the order is: 1. spouse; 2. adult children and grandchildren; 3. parents; 4. siblings; 5. grandparents; 6. great-grandparents, great-grandchildren or collateral blood relatives within the third degree; 7. direct relatives by marriage of the first degree. And it is used for one thing only: the withholding, terminating or withdrawing of cardiopulmonary resuscitation or life-sustaining treatment in a terminally ill patient. Three conditions must hold at once — diagnosis as terminally ill by two physicians holding the relevant specialist qualification, no 意願書 signed, and the patient comatose or unable to express his wishes clearly. The consent form may be given by one person; where the closest relatives disagree, the order above decides; where a later-ranked relative has already produced a consent form and an earlier-ranked one disagrees, that must be put in writing before execution. Where no closest relative can be found: after a hospice palliative care consultation, a physician's order made in the terminal patient's best interests takes the place of consent. And neither the consent form nor that order may run contrary to what the terminally ill patient expressly stated before falling into a coma or becoming unable to express his wishes clearly[2].

Surgery, invasive examination or treatment, and every other medical decision go down a different road, and on that road there is no ranking. Article 63 II of the Medical Care Act (醫療法) reads: "Where the patient is a minor or is unable to sign in person, the consent form under the preceding paragraph may be signed by his statutory agent, spouse, relatives or a person with a relevant connection." Article 64 II is structured the same way[3]; and Article 6 of the Patient Right to Autonomy Act says only "with the consent of the patient or a person with a relevant connection"[1]. These categories stand side by side; the statute does not provide who comes first — but note that the precondition in Article 63 II is "unable to sign in person". While the patient can still express himself and has clearly said no, the signature of a person with a relevant connection cannot replace his decision (Patient Right to Autonomy Act Article 4 II: a person with a relevant connection shall not obstruct the institution or physician acting on the patient's decision about the medical options). So that list of seven is not "Taiwan's family decision order" — it has nothing to say about whether to operate, whether to have chemotherapy or radiotherapy, whether to place a nasogastric tube. Applying it to those decisions is the misuse with the largest consequences I have seen.

The Ministry of Health and Welfare's surgical consent form template puts it more plainly still: the form is in principle signed by the patient personally, and where he is for some reason unable to give consent it may be signed by a statutory agent, spouse, relative or person with a relevant connection — that last category expressly including a partner regardless of gender, a cohabitant and a close friend[4] (verified 30 August 2026).

Fourth: capacity — clinically the physician, legally the court

Clinically the judgement rests with the physician, and at the critical points the law asks for more than one — the "two physicians holding the relevant specialist qualification" and the "at least two consultations with the palliative care team" above are that[1]. Removing legal capacity is for the court: an order of guardianship under Article 14 of the Civil Code (民法) is applied for by the person himself, his spouse, relatives within a defined range, a prosecutor or another interested party, and a person under such an order has no capacity to perform juridical acts; Article 15-1 provides for an order of assistance. Article 167 of the Family Act (家事事件法) further requires that the expert examination involve a psychiatrist or a physician with psychiatric experience, who shall issue a written report[5].

Two things stay apart. An order of guardianship is a legal effect, and does not mean the person lacks capacity for every clinical decision; and someone with no such order may still, at a given moment, lack capacity for one decision. That judgement is made each time, for the individual decision. Internationally, a review of 43 prospective studies found incapacity in 26% of medical inpatients (95% confidence interval 18 to 35%) against 2.8% in healthy older controls, and clinicians' judgement was reasonably accurate but identified only 42% of the affected patients[6]. Not Taiwanese data, not cancer-specific; but it shows "can he still decide this" is a question to ask out loud, not to feel out at home.

Two numbers, for the person who is now the agent

A review of 16 studies, 2,595 patient–agent pairs and 19,526 paired responses found that the person deciding on the patient's behalf predicted his treatment preferences correctly 68% of the time, getting roughly a third of scenarios wrong; and neither the patient choosing that person himself nor prior discussion of preferences improved accuracy[7]. That measured hypothetical scenarios, not real clinical decisions, and it is not saying the person acting for the patient cannot be trusted.

Another analysis, of 3,746 US adults aged 60 and over, found that 42.5% needed a medical decision at the end of life, and of those, 70.3% had by then lost decision-making capacity[8] (US system, reported retrospectively by the person who acted for the patient, observational). Side by side, the two numbers do not point at "guess harder". They point at that thing being written down by him, while he still can.

Signing these does not remove the duty to resuscitate

Article 7 of the Patient Right to Autonomy Act still stands: where a patient in critical condition is encountered, then except where Article 14 I and II and the Hospice Palliative Care Act's relevant provisions apply, appropriate emergency care or necessary measures shall be given first, without unwarranted delay[1]. Somebody brought in after a road accident is not left unresuscitated because he once signed an advance decision.

Ask these at the next visit: whether his situation is at the stage where these procedures come into play; if something is to be signed, how the consultation is arranged and who must be there; and if a signature is needed at short notice, who will be on the spot and what identification to bring. Those answers sit with the case manager (個管師), the medical social work office (社工室) or the hospital billing office (醫務課). Telling him the diagnosis is in "Whether to tell him, and what the law says"; his own right to refuse is in "He refuses treatment, or wants to try something else".

From the physician

Who may sign, and when it falls to family at all, is not settled by an agreement at home. Ask three things at the next visit: whether his situation is at the stage where these procedures apply; how the consultation is arranged and who must attend; and what identification to bring if a signature is needed at short notice. The case manager, social work office or billing office will know.
