HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

How much of the axilla has to go?

The box with a negative sentinel node holds the hardest evidence; in the box with a sentinel macrometastasis, the 2026 numbers point the unfavourable way instead.

Breast Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 11 references
#Surgery#Staging#Decisions#Lymphoedema

Z0011's conclusion only counts when four preconditions hold at once: the operation was a lumpectomy, the radiotherapy was whole-breast with standard tangential fields, systemic adjuvant therapy was given, and no third field was added. Those four lines are written into the trial design, not into your notes — so the first thing to ask is not "can I have less done", it is "do I meet those four lines".

How much of the axilla has to come out has moved steadily in one direction over the past twenty years — towards doing less. By 2026, the numbers in one of the boxes have turned back the other way. There is no single answer here that fits everyone; there is only which box you are in — and the strength of the evidence differs a great deal from box to box.

A negative sentinel node: this box has the hardest evidence in the whole question

NSABP B-32 enrolled 5,611 patients with invasive breast cancer whose axillary nodes could not be felt clinically; the 3,989 who were sentinel-node negative were then randomised to sentinel biopsy plus axillary lymph node dissection, or sentinel biopsy alone. At a mean follow-up of 95.6 months, the hazard ratio for overall survival was 1.20 (95% confidence interval 0.96 to 1.50, p=0.12) — a hazard ratio divides how fast deaths occur in one group by how fast they occur in the other, and 1.20 looks unfavourable, but the confidence interval crosses 1, so that difference does not stand up; 8-year overall survival was 91.8% against 90.3%, and 8-year disease-free survival 82.4% against 81.5% (hazard ratio 1.05, 0.90 to 1.22, p=0.54)[1].

What gets saved is written in the same trial's morbidity report: at 36 months, an arm volume difference of 10% or more between the treated and the other side was present in 14% after axillary dissection and 8% after sentinel biopsy alone; at 6 months, numbness 49% against 15%, tingling 23% against 10%[2]. The British ALMANAC trial supplies a different set of denominators: any lymphoedema at 12 months occurred in 5% of the sentinel group (515 patients) against 13% of the standard axillary treatment group (516), relative risk 0.37 (95% confidence interval 0.23 to 0.60); loss of sensation 11% against 31%[3].

One or two positive sentinel nodes: Z0011's conclusion cannot leave its preconditions behind

ACOSOG Z0011 enrolled 891 women with clinical T1/T2 disease, impalpable axillary nodes and 1 to 2 positive sentinel nodes, median age 55. At a median follow-up of 9.3 years, 10-year overall survival was 86.3% (sentinel biopsy alone) against 83.6% (with axillary dissection), hazard ratio 0.85 (one-sided 95% confidence limit 1.16), non-inferiority P=0.02; 10-year disease-free survival 80.2% against 78.2%[4]. What a non-inferiority test sets out to show is not that the new approach is better, but that the amount by which it falls behind does not exceed a margin agreed in advance.

That conclusion hangs on four preconditions, and losing one of them means it cannot be applied: everyone had a lumpectomy, everyone had whole-breast radiotherapy with standard tangential fields, everyone had systemic adjuvant therapy, and the trial forbade the addition of a third field[4]. If you are having a mastectomy, or the radiotherapy plan is different, Z0011 is not your data.

Axillary radiotherapy in place of dissection: AMAROS has a cost on each side

The EORTC's AMAROS trial put 4,806 people through sentinel biopsy; the 1,425 with a positive sentinel node were randomised to axillary lymph node dissection (744) or axillary radiotherapy (681). The 10-year cumulative incidence of axillary recurrence was 0.93% (7 cases, dissection) against 1.82% (11 cases, axillary radiotherapy), hazard ratio 1.71, 95% confidence interval 0.67 to 4.39 — seven cases against eleven; when the event count is that small, an interval stretching from 0.67 to 4.39 is inevitable, and that 1.71 pins nothing down. Overall survival and disease-free survival showed no difference either (hazard ratio 1.17, 0.89 to 1.52; and 1.19, 0.97 to 1.46)[5].

What does separate cleanly is the cost, and it grows on both sides. Lymphoedema at 5 years was 24.5% after dissection against 11.9% after axillary radiotherapy (P<0.001); the 10-year cumulative incidence of a second primary cancer was 12.1% after axillary radiotherapy (95% confidence interval 9.6 to 14.9) against 8.3% after dissection (6.3 to 10.7)[5]. I read those two lines out one after the other in clinic, because when a patient hears "we could switch to radiotherapy instead", what is usually in her head is only the first line. This is not "radiotherapy is the gentler one". They are two different costs, and they land at different points in time: one is your arm over the next few years, the other is a different illness ten years later.

No axillary surgery at all: the populations in those two trials are as narrow as a slit

SOUND enrolled women with tumours no larger than 2 cm and a negative preoperative axillary ultrasound; 1,405 were in the intention-to-treat analysis, median age 60, and 87.8% were ER-positive/HER2-negative. Among the 708 who had a sentinel biopsy, 97 (13.7%) turned out to have positive nodes after all. At a median follow-up of 5.7 years, 5-year distant disease-free survival was 97.7% (sentinel biopsy) against 98.0% (no axillary surgery at all), hazard ratio 0.84 (90% confidence interval 0.45 to 1.54), non-inferiority P=0.02[6].

INSEMA's primary-result population is just as narrow: clinically node-negative, T1/T2 and no larger than 5 cm, having breast-conserving surgery, with 90% clinically T1. 5,502 were randomised; at a median follow-up of 73.6 months, 5-year invasive disease-free survival was 91.9% (no axillary surgery, 962 patients) against 91.7% (sentinel biopsy, 3,896 patients), hazard ratio 0.91 (95% confidence interval 0.73 to 1.14), below the pre-set non-inferiority limit of 1.271; axillary recurrence was 1.0% against 0.3%[7]. The 2025 St Gallen consensus also listed "avoiding sentinel node surgery in many low-risk, ER-positive patients" as one of that year's updates[8]. Carrying those two trials' conclusions across to someone with a larger tumour, a suspicious node on ultrasound, or a planned mastectomy means a different group of people is doing the talking.

This 2026 set of numbers points back the other way

INSEMA also had a second randomisation, in patients who were clinically node-negative, had surgery first, had breast-conserving surgery, and had 1 to 3 sentinel nodes with macrometastases: 485 were enrolled and 386 entered the per-protocol analysis. At a median follow-up of 74.2 months, 5-year invasive disease-free survival was 86.6% (95% confidence interval 81.0 to 90.7, sentinel biopsy alone, 217 patients) against 93.8% (88.7 to 96.6, with axillary dissection, 169 patients), hazard ratio 1.69 (0.98 to 2.94), P=0.058; 5-year overall survival was 94.9% against 96.2% (P=0.663)[9].

The authors conclude that no significant difference was observed, and that sentence is not wrong — but read in full it says this: the two arms differ by 7.2 percentage points, the point estimate points towards the side that omits dissection doing worse, and the sample is nowhere near large enough to support any conclusion. Italy's SINODAR-ONE, in a similar population (889 patients, T1-2 with 1 to 2 sentinel macrometastases), saw 5-year overall survival of 98.9% against 98.8% (p=0.936), but with a median follow-up of only 34 months[10]. So I say it straight in clinic: when the sentinel node carries a macrometastasis, whether to go on and clear the axilla has no reassuring set of numbers behind it at present, and this box is not one a patient can ask to have omitted.

And there is one box that appears in none of the trials above: people who have preoperative chemotherapy first and still have residual nodal disease afterwards. The 2021 axillary management guideline from Ontario Health and ASCO handles axillary strategy and timing in the preoperative setting separately[11]; the question of that sequence itself — surgery first or chemotherapy first — is in "Surgery first, or chemotherapy first?".

Taking fewer nodes out is not the same as not swelling

This operation decides the arm as well. Some people still swell after a sentinel biopsy — in B-32's sentinel-only arm at 36 months, 8% still had a treated arm more than 10% larger by volume than the other side[2]. That number is not zero, and it does not turn into zero because the report says the nodes were not involved. How the real probability breaks down according to what was done to the axilla, which risk factors have evidence behind them, and which of the traditional warnings are in fact unsupported by data, is what "A swollen arm: the real odds of lymphoedema" spends a whole article on, so I will not repeat it here.

Which papers this axillary question needs spread out in clinic

Whether you can do less depends on which box you are in, and the box is decided by these papers:

  • The preoperative axillary ultrasound report, and whether a fine-needle or core biopsy of a node was done and what it showed.

  • Whether your surgical plan is conserving surgery or mastectomy, and how the postoperative radiotherapy fields have already been planned. Z0011's and INSEMA's preconditions are written on those two lines.

  • Surgery first or systemic treatment first. This item alone rules out half the trials above.

  • Once the sentinel result is back, whether the report says micrometastasis or macrometastasis, and how many. The answer in the macrometastasis box is not the answer in the micrometastasis box.

  • Your dominant hand, what your work involves, and your weight — these affect the risk of arm complications, and they are worth putting into the discussion.

The sentence to get out of your mouth is: "In my situation, how far are you planning to go? And if the sentinel node turns out positive on the table, how will you decide?" On the Taiwan side, for sentinel lymph node biopsy and axillary dissection as operations, and for whether the dye, isotope or magnetic tracer used for localisation has to be paid for out of pocket, I obtained no citable formal provision for any of them — confirm these with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.

From the physician

Before surgery, get the axillary ultrasound report, whether a node has been sampled, and the planned radiotherapy fields clear. If the sentinel node carries a macrometastasis, whether to clear the axilla is for the team to assess; it is not something to ask to have omitted. Whether the tracer used for localisation is self-pay is a question for your case manager.
