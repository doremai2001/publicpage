One session, or fractionated

The line between single-session and fractionated is drawn by how much dose the optic pathway can take, not by which machine; the long-term figures on both sides, when the complications arrive, and what Taiwan's NHI clause actually says.

The dividing line in this question is a physical one: how much the optic nerve and the chiasm can absorb.

Two people with the same meningioma: one is booked in for a single session, the other for more than twenty. The question I hear most often in clinic is "isn't one session better?", and that question has gone wrong before it finishes. Single-session and fractionated are not a ranking of how well they work. They are a division of labour set by the volume of the tumour and by how close it sits to the optic nerve.

My position first: stereotactic radiosurgery and fractionated radiotherapy are the part I deliver myself; the other side of every comparison in this topic is my neurosurgical colleagues' operations, my neuro-otology colleagues' hearing assessments, and my endocrinology colleagues' drugs. That is why every comparison here comes with its primary source, so you can take it to neurosurgery, to neuro-otology and to endocrinology for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

What really draws this line is how much the optic nerve can take

In the collected normal-tissue dose-constraint literature (QUANTEC), the paper on the optic nerves and chiasm puts it bluntly: with fractionated treatment, toxicity rises appreciably above a total dose of 60 Gy (at about 1.8 Gy per fraction); with single-session radiosurgery, it rises appreciably above 12 Gy. And what it says the evidence is strong for is something else again — bring the dose per fraction down and the total the optic pathway will tolerate goes up[1]. A more recent pooled analysis (34 studies, 1,578 people, a population that includes cavernous sinus meningioma, pituitary adenoma, craniopharyngioma and malignant skull base tumours, not meningioma alone) puts finer numbers on it. In people not previously irradiated, the maximum point dose to the optic apparatus corresponding to under a 1% risk of radiation-induced optic neuropathy is 12 Gy in 1 fraction, 20 Gy in 3 and 25 Gy in 5, and the authors themselves recommend being more conservative still at 10 Gy for a single session. In people who have been irradiated before, the risk is roughly ten times that of people who have not[2].

Translated into one sentence: when a tumour is up against the optic nerve or the chiasm, the reason it becomes a fractionated course lies here, not in which machine is newer.

What the guideline writes, and the half it does not write

The wording of the European Association of Neuro-Oncology (EANO) 2021 guideline is that in well-selected cases with tumours that are not large, stereotactic radiosurgery is established as an alternative to surgery. It also relays that for intracranial meningiomas under 3 centimetres in diameter, local control after radiosurgery is comparable to a Simpson grade I resection. For larger volumes it mentions fractionated stereotactic treatment in 2 to 5 sessions[3]. What it does not give is a hard threshold of the form "within so many millimetres of the optic nerve it must be fractionated". If somebody tells you "over 3 centimetres always has to be fractionated", that is a line drawn by clinical habit, not a rule written into the guideline; when the same tumour gets booked for different numbers of sessions at two different hospitals, this is usually where the difference lives.

The long-term single-session figures, and the ruler they were measured with

The largest series covers 15 centres, 4,565 patients and 5,300 benign meningiomas, all treated at least five years earlier: median tumour volume 4.8 cubic centimetres, median marginal dose 14 Gy; after excluding those with under 24 months of imaging follow-up, 3,768 tumours were analysed, with a median imaging follow-up of 63 months[4]. The judgement was made on volume change on imaging: 58% shrank, 34.5% were unchanged, 7.5% grew, giving a control rate of 92.5%, with only 2.2% needing further treatment. Five-year progression-free survival, meaning the proportion still free of tumour growth on imaging, was 95.2% and ten-year 88.6%; permanent complications 6.6%[4].

Two things from that same dataset have to be said alongside it. First, control was better at the skull base than at the convexity (p<.001) — the site by itself changes what you should expect. Second, control was significantly higher in tumours diagnosed on imaging alone without any pathology than in histologically confirmed grade 1 tumours (p<.001)[4] — which is direct evidence of selection bias, meaning the groups differed before treatment started: the ones irradiated without an operation were already the slower-growing ones. Before you take 92.5% as your own expectation, work out which box you are in.

Fractionated treatment is dealing with a different size of tumour

The Heidelberg long-term experience in 317 patients (48.3% grade 1, 8.2% grade 2, 43.5% grade unknown): median target volume 33.6 cubic centimetres, seven times the volume of the series above; median total dose 57.6 Gy at 1.8 Gy per fraction; median follow-up 5.7 years. Overall local control was 93.1%. Of these, 70.4% stayed stable and 6.9% progressed on imaging at a median of 4.5 years[5]. The recurrence rate was 15.5% for volumes above 60 cubic centimetres against 4.3% at or below 60 (p<0.001). Local failure was significantly more common in grade 2 (p<0.002). On function: 42.9% had improvement of a pre-existing neurological deficit, 8.2% had a pre-existing symptom worsen, and 8 people developed new symptoms (reduced vision, trigeminal neuralgia, intermittent tinnitus)[5].

For meningiomas of the optic pathway treated with conventional fractionation, the pooled data (this passage is relayed by a review rather than taken from the primary data, and the population is meningioma only) give pooled local control of 99.8% with roughly 90% of people keeping or improving their vision, tumour size being associated with visual deterioration; estimated radiation-induced dry eye 10.1% and retinopathy 7.2%[6]. These two sets of figures cannot be set against each other directly — the populations differ in volume by several times over.

Protons: lovely physics, and no clinical winner yet

A meta-analysis restricted to grade 1 meningioma comparing four techniques (24 studies, 4,673 people): five-year local control was 95.9% for conventionally fractionated protons and 93.0% for conventionally fractionated photons, with the two stereotactic radiosurgery groups at 95.3% and 89.8%; and meta-regression showed neither treatment modality nor tumour volume to be significantly associated with local control[7]. A separate analysis of protons alone (19 studies, 1,431 people; 70.6% grade 1, 25.2% grade 2, 4.2% grade 3) found a pooled complication rate of 16%, five-year overall survival 91% and mean local control on imaging 71%, with high heterogeneity throughout, and the authors concluded that more prospective work is needed[8].

There is exactly one randomised comparison, and it has only just started. COG-PROTON-01 randomises benign cavernous sinus meningioma 1:1 to fractionated proton or photon radiotherapy, with an expected 160 people, and its primary endpoint is cognitive and functional deterioration (visual, auditory, neurological, endocrine) at five years after the end of radiotherapy, not tumour control; recruitment opened in February 2024 and primary completion is February 2032[9][10]. So "protons are better in meningioma" is a sentence that has no clinical data holding it up today. How to think about protons generally is on the site in "Protons: precise. Then what?" and "Is proton therapy actually better?".

Complications: how often, and in which window

The commonest thing after a single session is radiation-induced brain oedema. In a systematic review of 26 studies (weighted towards non-skull-base meningioma), 13 either recruited only non-skull-base tumours or reported them separately: symptomatic oedema 5% to 43%, any oedema 28% to 50%. Most studies report a mean time of onset three to nine months after treatment[11]. Factors associated with higher risk include a higher marginal or maximum dose, a larger tumour, a non-skull-base site (parasagittal especially), no previous operation, and oedema already present before treatment — but the authors stress that the studies do not agree with one another[11].

For the other magnitudes: permanent complications were 6.6% in that 4,565-patient cohort[4]; on the fractionated side, 8.2% had a pre-existing symptom worsen and 8 people developed new symptoms[5]; where doses are escalated above 66 Gy, symptomatic radiation necrosis was 5.9% overall with no difference between the groups (relayed by a review)[6]. Cranial neuropathy in these cohorts shows up as trigeminal neuralgia, reduced vision and tinnitus[5]. On hypopituitarism I have to be honest: for the rate after radiotherapy specifically for meningioma, I could not find a primary study I could cite; the pituitary group has its own data, in "Where endonasal surgery and radiotherapy fit". How oedema is managed and how steroids are adjusted is in "Seizures, oedema and steroids", and do not adjust the dose yourself.

Taiwan: what the clause says, and what it does not

The National Health Insurance (NHI) fee schedule contains two intracranial stereotactic radiosurgery items, both effective 1 April 2026: 37028B, three-dimensional X-knife stereotactic radiosurgery, at 82,000 points, and 37029B, image-guided intensity-modulated X-ray stereotactic radiosurgery, at 153,229 points[12]. Both clauses carry a size threshold: 37028B reads, in translation, "a diameter of less than three centimetres or a volume of fifteen cubic centimetres or under, with the number of lesions three or fewer", and 37029B "three-dimensional diameters not greater than 3.5 by 3.5 by 3.5 centimetres, or a volume of twenty cubic centimetres, with the number of lesions three or fewer". The indications name, word for word, acoustic neuroma, meningioma, pituitary tumour and craniopharyngioma (the clause's "acoustic neuroma" is the vestibular schwannoma, also called acoustic neuroma) — but one of six conditions A to F must also be met: A is "has undergone a craniotomy but has residual or recurrent tumour", B is "a craniotomy would carry a risk of neurological injury or be highly dangerous", C is "has severe cardiopulmonary or other medical disease making invasive surgery or general anaesthesia unsuitable", through to F, "a unilateral intracranial cerebellopontine angle acoustic neuroma under 2.5 centimetres in width (internal auditory canal excluded)". And the clause is unyielding on one point: "every case requires prior case-by-case application to the insurer"[12]. Note 3 to 37029B reads, in translation, "the item for stereotactic radiosurgery with γ-knife is claimed by analogy to this item" — I am quoting the clause's own wording, and passing no judgement on any machine[12].

One thing to be clear about: there is no separate item for fractionated stereotactic treatment in the current version. So the codes cannot be used to infer how the NHI distinguishes single-session from fractionated; how your own course gets claimed is a question for the medical affairs office or your case manager. As for where it can be done, not every hospital has it.

The question to ask is not which one is better

Spending those ten minutes in clinic on your own tumour is far more use than spending them on which machine. Ask two numbers about the tumour first: what is its volume, and how far is it from the optic nerve and the chiasm? Then two numbers about the plan: if this is a single session, where will the maximum point dose to the optic apparatus land[2], and if it is fractionated, how many sessions in total and why that number? Save the last question for the clock: over that three-to-nine-month window, how is the oedema to be watched, and what would mean coming straight back rather than waiting[11]?

And one sentence for anyone still hesitating: this article only handles "if it is to be irradiated, how". Whether to watch first is in "Watching is a form of treatment"; whether to add radiotherapy after a grade 2 resection is a different question again, in "After the operation, do you add radiotherapy".

From the physician

If someone will only talk to you about machines, change the question: how far away is the optic nerve, and what point dose will it see? Write those two lines on a piece of paper and take it in with you. And in the three to nine months afterwards, a worsening headache or a new neurological symptom means coming back — not waiting for the next scheduled scan.
