The side effects that actually make people stop

Joint pain, hot flushes, vaginal dryness, bone loss and the risk to the womb: how much evidence each one has, what can be done, and where Taiwan's reimbursement threshold gets in the way.

Breast Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 16 references
#Endocrine Therapy#Side Effects#Quality of Life#Bone Health#Daily Life

Only three of the ways of handling this have randomised evidence — exercise, duloxetine and acupuncture — and the size of the effect is small in all three, with acupuncture explicitly listed as not recommended for hot flushes. For incidence in Asian populations there is one retrospective series of 299 patients.

"Doctor, I don't think I can keep taking this." Most people who say that sentence are not people who are insufficiently afraid of recurrence. They are people whose fingers are too stiff in the morning to open a bottle cap, who are woken three times a night by heat, who have started to feel their heels hurt when they walk. This article is about those side effects, and about which of the ways of handling them actually have a randomised trial behind them. Whether the tablets run for five years or ten is a different decision, in "Five years of endocrine therapy, or ten?".

Joint pain is not a minority problem, and it really does make people stop

The ELPh prospective randomised trial enrolled 503 women with early breast cancer starting an aromatase inhibitor: within two years, 32.4% stopped the first drug because of adverse effects, and of those 24.3% were because of musculoskeletal symptoms; the median time to discontinuation for any symptom was 6.1 months. Younger age (hazard ratio 1.4, 95% confidence interval 1.02–1.9) and previous taxane chemotherapy (1.9, 1.00–3.6) were both associated with a higher likelihood of stopping — a hazard ratio compares the speed at which stopping happens, not the proportion of people it happens to, and the lower bound of both intervals is right up against 1, so these can only be read as signals[1]. The HOPE trial states the background more directly: joint pain occurs in as many as 50% of survivors taking this class of drug, and it is the commonest reason for poor adherence[2].

The Asian data are thin. What can be cited is a retrospective series from one Korean hospital: among 299 postmenopausal patients with invasive breast cancer taking this class of drug, 69 (23%) developed musculoskeletal symptoms attributed to the drug, and 29 of them (39.7%) stopped because of it; in multivariable analysis, previous tamoxifen use was associated with stopping (odds ratio 4.27, 1.74–10.50; an odds ratio is not 4.27 times the probability, it is one ratio of happened-to-did-not-happen divided by another, and this interval is wide enough that only the direction can be read)[3]. That is a single-centre retrospective study and cannot be treated as an incidence figure for Taiwan. A separate study of pharmacy dispensing records in 8,769 women found that by 4.5 years 32% had already stopped and only 49% overall had taken the full course in the optimal way, with the highest risk of stopping in women under 40 (hazard ratio 1.51, 1.23–1.85); Asian patients were more likely to complete the course[4].

Two opposite things have to be said clearly here at once: please say the symptoms out loud, because switching the drug, adding treatment for the symptom and adjusting how it is taken are all things a clinic can do; but whether you stop is not something you decide at home. Those five or ten years are not a length of time, they are the effect itself — take a stretch out of the middle yourself and you have already borne the side effects while losing a piece of the benefit. This whole article is about hormone-receptor-positive breast cancer: aromatase inhibitors are used after the menopause (or before it in combination with ovarian function suppression), and tamoxifen can be used either side of the menopause; HER2-positive receptor-negative and triple-negative breast cancer do not involve these drugs at all.

Three things do have randomised evidence, and none of the effects is large

The most valuable one first. HOPE enrolled 121 survivors who had been on the drug for at least six months, with a worst pain score of 3 or above, and who had been doing little exercise; they were randomised to an exercise programme (150 minutes of aerobic exercise a week plus twice-weekly supervised resistance training) or usual care, for one year. At twelve months the worst pain score had fallen by 1.6 points (29%) in the exercise group and had risen by 0.2 points in the usual-care group (p<0.001)[2]. The largest effect and the smallest cost — but it takes a whole year of doing it.

On the drug side there is SWOG S1202: a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 299 people, enrolling postmenopausal patients with early breast cancer on the drug with an average joint pain score of 4 or above. At twelve weeks the duloxetine group's average joint pain was 0.82 points lower than placebo (95% confidence interval −1.24 to −0.40, p=0.0002); the price was adverse events of any grade in 78% against 50% on placebo[5] — the two numbers have to be read together.

Acupuncture has SWOG S1200: 226 postmenopausal women with early breast cancer, on the drug, with a worst pain score of 3 or above, randomised to true acupuncture, sham acupuncture or a waitlist control. At six weeks the worst pain score fell by 2.05 points with true acupuncture and 1.07 with sham, an adjusted difference between true and sham of 0.92 points (0.20–1.65, p=0.01) — but the clinically meaningful difference set in advance was 2 points, and the authors write themselves that the clinical importance of this improvement is uncertain[6]. For acupuncture for this kind of joint pain in Taiwan, I did not obtain an indication provision I could cite — that one is to confirm with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office, and do not reason from somebody else's experience to your own.

Switching to another drug is a workable strategy, but not "switch and the pain goes"

Among the 83 women in ELPh who switched to a different aromatase inhibitor, 38.6% were able to keep taking the second one, for a median of 13.7 months[1]. ATOLL, a six-month prospective non-randomised study, enrolled 179 people who had stopped anastrozole because of musculoskeletal symptoms and switched them to letrozole: at six months 128 (71.5%) were still taking it; but at the same time point 116 (73.9%) still had joint pain[7]. Switching lets a good proportion of people carry on. It is not the same as the symptoms going away.

Hot flushes: some approaches are recommended, some are explicitly not

Oxybutynin has randomised evidence. ACCRU SC-1603 enrolled 150 women with at least 28 hot flushes a week for whom oestrogen was unsuitable (two-thirds of them taking tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor); at six weeks the within-group change in the weekly hot flush score was −16.9 on the higher dose, −10.6 on the lower dose and −5.7 on placebo, with side effects mainly dry mouth, difficulty passing urine and abdominal pain[8]. The earliest randomised evidence in this field is the venlafaxine study from 2000[9].

The 2023 nonhormone therapy position statement of The North American Menopause Society splits into two halves: recommended are cognitive behavioural therapy, clinical hypnosis, SSRIs and SNRIs, gabapentin, fezolinetant and oxybutynin; not recommended are supplements and herbal products, cooling techniques, avoiding triggers, exercise, yoga, mindfulness and acupuncture[10]. Acupuncture has randomised evidence in joint pain and is listed as not recommended for hot flushes; when you ask about it, be clear which symptom you are asking about.

Vaginal dryness and local oestrogen: on this one there are only observational data

This is a question almost nobody asks as soon as they sit down. The largest data at present come from two population-based cohorts in Scotland and Wales, covering 49,237 women newly diagnosed with breast cancer and 5,795 breast cancer deaths, of whom 5% used vaginal oestrogen after diagnosis. Compared with women who used no hormone replacement therapy, users had no increase in breast-cancer-specific mortality (fully adjusted hazard ratio 0.77, 0.63–0.94); the authors' own wording is that this "may provide some reassurance"[11].

But that is an observational study, not a randomised trial, and confounding by indication cannot be excluded — doctors are already less likely to prescribe it to higher-risk patients. The direction is reassuring, but the level of evidence is not the top tier, and this still has to be discussed individually with your own doctor, particularly if you are taking an aromatase inhibitor. I did not obtain the reimbursement provisions for topical vaginal oestrogen preparations in Taiwan; that one is to confirm with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.

Bone and womb: two risks that need absolute numbers to discuss

On the bone side, the ten-year safety data from ATAC set the timing out clearly: fractures during treatment numbered 451 in the anastrozole group and 351 in the tamoxifen group (odds ratio 1.33, 1.15–1.55, p<0.0001), but during the post-treatment follow-up period they were 110 against 112 (odds ratio 0.98, p=0.9) — the excess fracture risk is concentrated in the years you are taking the drug, and it disappears once it stops[12]. There is something that can be done: ABCSG-18 randomised 3,420 postmenopausal patients taking an aromatase inhibitor, and in the denosumab group the time to first clinical fracture was significantly delayed (hazard ratio 0.50, 0.39–0.65), including in women whose baseline bone mineral density T-score was above −1 (0.44, 0.31–0.64)[13]. How bone density is monitored and how bone-protective drugs are chosen is set out in full in "Bone density, bone-targeted drugs and exercise".

Section 5 of the NHI drug reimbursement regulations, on drugs for osteoporosis, has two routes: one is a postmenopausal woman with a T-score of −2.5 or below on dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry who has already had a vertebral or hip fracture, or with osteopenia (a T-score between −2.5 and −1.0) causing two or more vertebral or hip fractures; the other is a T-score of −2.5 or below together with rheumatoid arthritis, insulin-treated diabetes or long-term glucocorticoid use — "taking an aromatase inhibitor" is not on the list[14]. I did not obtain the provisions covering bone density testing either. So "my bones have thinned because of this drug" will mostly not automatically qualify for drug reimbursement in Taiwan, and the actual position is to confirm with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.

On the womb side it is tamoxifen. The label quotes data from NSABP P-1 in high-risk women, as events per 1000 women-years: endometrial adenocarcinoma 2.20 with tamoxifen against 0.71 with placebo; pulmonary embolism 0.75 against 0.25. The same label also states that for most patients with diagnosed breast cancer the benefits of the drug outweigh the risks[15]. The 12,894-patient ATLAS dataset gives absolute figures: the cumulative risk of endometrial cancer during years 5 to 14 was 3.1% in those continuing to ten years and 1.6% in those stopping at five, with corresponding mortality of 0.4% against 0.2%[16]. The symptoms to watch for are specific: vaginal bleeding after the menopause, abnormal bleeding between periods, swelling and pain in one leg, or sudden chest pain. Endometrial cancer at ten years in ATAC was 6 cases with anastrozole against 24 with tamoxifen[12] — the two drugs carry different combinations of risk, and that is part of any conversation about switching.

Turn "I feel awful" into four numbers

"I feel awful" cannot be acted on in clinic, but four numbers can. First, pain: on a 0 to 10 scale, how bad has it been at its worst this week. Second, function: which movement you cannot do — opening a bottle cap, fastening a button, how many minutes your fingers are stiff in the morning, whether you can walk for fifteen minutes without stopping. Third, hot flushes: how many a day, whether they wake you, how many nights a week. Fourth, the tablets: how many days you have missed this month, which days, and why.

Write those four lines in the notes app on your phone and read them out at your next appointment. With numbers, your doctor has grounds to switch your drug, add treatment for the symptom or arrange an exercise prescription; without numbers, that conversation usually stops at "give it a bit longer". And once more: say it when you cannot go on taking it, but whether you stop is the treatment team's decision.

From the physician

Say the side effects out loud, but whether you stop is the treatment team's decision — the length of the course is part of the effect itself. From today, keep four numbers: worst pain score, which movement you cannot do, how many hot flushes a day, how many days of tablets you have missed this month. Numbers are what make the next step negotiable.
