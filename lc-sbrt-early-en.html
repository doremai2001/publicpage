HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

Inoperable does not mean incurable

The randomised evidence comparing stereotactic radiotherapy with surgery amounts to 58 patients. Here is what it can and cannot support.

Lung Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 18 references
#Lung#SBRT / SRS#Surgery#Decisions#Clinical Trials

If the answer to “can this be operated on” was no, the next question is what the evidence behind the alternative actually shows — the sample sizes, the confidence intervals, and the three trials that closed before they could answer anything.

In early lung cancer, "can this be operated on" is not one option among several; it is the fork itself. For people who can have an operation the guidelines still put surgery first, and I am not going to touch that. The hard part comes afterwards. What is left to someone judged unable to have one is exactly the thing I do every day, and what it can and cannot give you should be something you can see the evidence for. So this piece opens not with what stereotactic body radiotherapy is good at, but with something that counts against my own specialty.

Eight in ten radiation oncologists say "about the same". Outside my specialty it is 28%

A 2018 cross-specialty web survey collected 959 completed responses, 64% from radiation oncologists. It asked what current evidence shows about radiotherapy against surgery for stage I non-small cell lung cancer in someone who could have an operation. 80% of radiation oncologists said the two were equivalent or that radiotherapy was better; among everyone else it was 28% (p<0.01)[1].

I am in that 80%. So discount this article: wherever I say the thing I do is also an option, check whether I put the figures on the other side next to it. I have, in every section below, including the report card where five-year survival is 40%.

The randomised evidence that can answer this question comes to 58 people

One published dataset randomised stereotactic radiotherapy against lobectomy: a pooled analysis of two trials, 58 patients randomised, 31 to radiotherapy and 27 to surgery, tumours under 4 cm, all fit for a lobectomy. Three-year survival was 95% in the radiotherapy group (95% confidence interval 85–100) and 79% in the surgical group (64–97), hazard ratio 0.14, confidence interval 0.017 to 1.190 — a hazard ratio compares how fast deaths accrue over the same stretch of time, not a multiple of probability; 0.14 means deaths came more slowly in the radiotherapy group here, not that the chance of dying was cut to a fourteenth. Six of the seven deaths were in the surgical arm. Taken apart, one trial reached significance on its own (p=0.0067) and the other showed no difference at all (p=0.78). The authors' conclusion: too few patients, follow-up too short, more randomised studies still needed[2].

A confidence interval crossing 1 means this dataset has not excluded "the two are the same". Why only 58? Three randomised trials were opened to answer the question and all three closed early for failure to accrue; the registry entries record actual enrolment of 36[3], 22[4] and 13[5] — 71 patients against a combined planned target of 2,410. A 2025 review by one thoracic surgeon and two radiation oncologists compresses it into one line of its own arithmetic: a target of 2,410, and 2.8% enrolled[6]. The numerators differ by a handful of patients; the conclusion is identical. This question has never been filled.

The same review takes that surgical arm apart: operative mortality 4%, against 1.4% in the American thoracic surgery database; 80% of the operations were open thoracotomies rather than minimally invasive; 11% never received a lobectomy; one of the trials did not even require a tissue diagnosis first[6]. A surgical arm like that cannot hold up "radiotherapy did better".

So this is the sentence I have to write plainly: there is not enough randomised evidence to support stereotactic body radiotherapy being equivalent to surgery. 58 patients, a confidence interval crossing 1, one of the two trials showing no difference whatever, and a surgical arm that was plainly not what it should have been — all this data supports is that the question is unanswered.

The strongest thing that can be written down today is the 2025 American College of Chest Physicians guideline's own sentence: for stage I patients at average or low operative risk, surgical resection remains the best treatment; stereotactic body radiotherapy is the preferred approach for those who are not considered appropriate candidates for surgical resection[7].

Two trials of adequate size are still recruiting: one expects primary results in 2028[8], the other not until 2032[9].

That "revised STARS" is not a trial that compared itself with surgery

The most-cited recent study is the 2021 revised STARS, and its design has to be stated: this is a single-arm trial at one hospital, 80 patients with tumours 3 cm or smaller and biopsy-confirmed N0M0, five-year survival 87%. The group it was set against had video-assisted lobectomy in that same hospital's database, matched 1:1 after the fact by propensity score on age, tumour size, histology and performance status; their five-year survival was 84%, hazard ratio 0.86 (0.45–1.65), p=0.65[10].

A single arm plus an after-the-fact match inside one institution is a different order of thing from a randomised allocation. The authors' closing sentence is the one worth copying out: the approach remains promising, and they strongly recommend the decision be made jointly by a multidisciplinary team[10].

What the report card looks like for people who could not be operated on

What made this the standard for medically inoperable stage I lung cancer is a single-arm phase 2 trial, 55 evaluable patients, peripheral tumours only[11]. The five-year figures go down as they stand: recurrence in the treated tumour itself 7.3%, but local recurrence counting the rest of the same lobe 20.0%, distant spread 23.6%, five-year disease-free survival 25.5%, five-year overall survival 40.0%; with longer follow-up, grade 3 toxicity accumulated to 27.3% and grade 4 to 3.6%[12].

Read those two together. "Local control above 90%" is the narrowest definition; change it and five-year local recurrence is one in five. And 40% five-year survival is not the treatment failing; it is what this population is — people whose lungs or hearts had already ruled an operation out. A Japanese trial running operable and inoperable patients in one study says it cleanly: three-year survival 59.9% in the inoperable group, 76.5% in the operable group[13]. Same machine. The difference is in the people. When you see a survival figure, ask first whose survival it is.

The one randomised trial this treatment has won was against older radiotherapy

It has won a randomised trial, but not against surgery. Fourteen hospitals across Australia and New Zealand randomised 101 stage I patients unfit for surgery or declining it, 2:1: local progression 14% against 31%, hazard ratio for local treatment failure 0.32 (0.13–0.77, p=0.0077); the price was one grade 4 and seven grade 3 treatment-related events in the stereotactic arm[14]. Which is why "no wound, hardly any side effects" is a sentence I do not write. Recognising radiation pneumonitis is in "Breathless after treatment — which pneumonitis is it"; what an irradiated lung leaves on follow-up scans is in "One kind of change that is not a recurrence"; whether proton therapy is worth it is in "Is proton therapy actually better?".

The large non-randomised datasets mostly favour surgery, and they cannot be taken at face value either

The American National Cancer Database identified 30,658 patients with clinical stage I disease and no comorbidity recorded in the chart at all. After propensity matching the survival curves crossed inside the first year, then went the way of surgery: five-year survival 71.0% against 41.8%. Restricted to patients whose charts record that they declined a recommended operation, it was still 71.4% against 55.9%[15]. A separate analysis of patients declining surgery found median survival of 74 against 47 months after matching, adjusted hazard ratio 1.69[16] — this one points the opposite way from the 0.14 above: 1.69 means deaths in the radiotherapy group came sooner, not that 69% more people die, and it comes without a confidence interval.

I post these figures, and must say in the same breath why they are not the last word either. People sent for an operation are healthier before anyone touches them; a propensity score adjusts only for variables a database happens to record, and cannot rebuild an operability judgement. There is a technical problem underneath too: the same recurrence lands in a different column on each side — a recurrence in another lobe of the same lung is usually "distant" in a radiotherapy study and "locoregional" in a surgical one. Radiotherapy patients get no nodal dissection, while up to 12% of clinical stage I patients are upstaged after an operation[6].

"Cannot be operated on" is not a fixed property. It is a judgement read off a set of numbers

The trial that founded this treatment defined medically inoperable as: forced expiratory volume in one second below 40% predicted, predicted post-operative value below 30%, carbon monoxide diffusing capacity below 40%, severe pulmonary hypertension or severe cerebral and cardiovascular disease among others — judged by an experienced thoracic surgeon or chest physician[11]. A separate category, "high risk but still operable", uses different thresholds: expiratory volume or diffusing capacity of 50% predicted or less is a major criterion; age 75 or over, expiratory volume or diffusing capacity of 51–60%, and pulmonary hypertension are minor criteria, of which two are needed[8]. A chest physician takes another route: calculate the predicted post-operative values first; both above 60% is low risk, and 30–60% adds an exercise test — 22 metres of stairs, or a 400-metre shuttle walk[17].

So do not carry "I cannot be operated on" as a conclusion you reached yourself. The question is: am I medically inoperable, or high risk but still operable? The two answers open different options. How stage decides the purpose of treatment is in "Ask whether surgery is possible before you ask about chemotherapy".

In Taiwan this judgement is, by rule, not mine alone to make

In the National Health Insurance (健保) payment schedule (支付標準), stereotactic body radiotherapy (item 37047B) applied to the lung covers: a single primary lung lesion, no mediastinal or infraclavicular nodal metastasis, no metastasis to other organs, stage within T+N1M0, ECOG 2 or below (or KPS 70 or above). The clause that matters is the next: there must be an anaesthesiologist's assessment and a surgical consultation documented in the chart, or a tumour board conclusion that the patient is not suitable for curative resection, with the minutes attached. Prior authorisation is also required; the whole course runs two weeks with a maximum of six fractions, paid as a package[18]. That is the version effective 1 April 2026 (115 年 4 月 1 日), verified 2026-08-30.

A gap: a further amendment takes effect on 1 September 2026 (115 年 9 月 1 日) and I could not retrieve its text, so the wording and point values above may already have moved. That is "I could not find it", not "it is not covered". Please confirm the actual conditions and costs with your case manager (個管師) or the hospital billing office (醫務課). The direction of the rule is clear: the finding that you are not suitable for an operation has to be on the record before the insurer pays, and it is not something I can sign off alone.

The question is not "can I have radiotherapy". It is these three numbers

Am I medically inoperable or high risk but still operable, and which numbers was that decided on? What are my expiratory volume, my diffusing capacity and my predicted post-operative values? Has my case been to the tumour board, and what does the conclusion line say?

Ask those three and you hold everything anyone used to judge you — including the judgement of whether the thing I do is right for you.

From the physician

Which of the two categories you fall into is read off lung-function numbers and a tumour board conclusion, and both are things you can ask to see. Take the three questions at the end of this piece to your next appointment, and put them to the multidisciplinary meeting rather than to one doctor — including me.
