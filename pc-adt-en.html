SIDE EFFECTS AND LIVING WITH THEM

What hormone therapy costs you

Every cost itemised — heart, bone, metabolism, mood, memory — alongside the reason the injections are worth having.

Prostate Cancer Guide · Side Effects and Living With Them · 21 references
#Prostate#Hormone Therapy#Side Effects#Quality of Life#NHI Coverage

This piece is long on side effects and it is not an argument for stopping. Read the first section and the last one together before you read the middle.

Hormone therapy is the prostate cancer treatment most often waved through. The consent form says "you may get hot flushes and reduced libido", and the injections start. This piece sets out the costs in full, but first: it is not an argument against having it. In the populations where it belongs it lengthens survival — and I have met men who read a list of side effects online and stopped it themselves.

Why it is worth paying these costs

The MARCAP individual patient data meta-analysis pooled 12 trials and 10,853 men, median follow-up 11.4 years. Adding androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) to radiotherapy given with curative intent significantly improved metastasis-free survival, hazard ratio 0.83 (95% confidence interval 0.77 to 0.89); lengthening the ADT that follows radiotherapy also helped (0.84, 0.78 to 0.91); lengthening the stretch before radiotherapy did not (0.95, 0.83 to 1.09, P=0.50). The effect did not vary with radiotherapy dose, age or risk group[1]. A hazard ratio of 0.83 in plain terms: over the same follow-up, metastasis or death accrued at about 83% of the rate.

One more that matters as much. In a meta-analysis of 8 randomised trials and 4,141 men there was no difference in cardiovascular death between ADT and control (11.0% against 11.2%, relative risk 0.93, P=0.41), whether the course ran three years or more or six months or less; across 11 trials and 4,805 men, ADT reduced prostate-cancer death (0.69, 0.56 to 0.84) and all-cause death (0.86, 0.80 to 0.93)[2]. Read these two paragraphs alongside everything below.

On the heart there is a signal, but I cannot write "antagonists are safer"

The observational signal is real. In a US Medicare cohort of 73,196 men aged 66 and over, use of a GnRH agonist was associated with new-onset diabetes (adjusted hazard ratio 1.44), coronary heart disease (1.16), myocardial infarction (1.11) and sudden cardiac death (1.16)[3].

What follows is regularly reported backwards. PRONOUNCE is the only prospective randomised international trial designed to compare the cardiovascular safety of an antagonist against an agonist, and it enrolled men who already had atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. With enrolment and events far below expectation, it stopped early before reaching its target of 900, with 545 men actually randomised. On the primary endpoint (death, myocardial infarction or stroke combined) degarelix had 15 events (5.5%) against leuprolide's 11 (4.1%), hazard ratio 1.28 (0.59 to 2.79), P=0.53 — the point estimate pointing towards the antagonist being worse. The authors conclude that the relative cardiovascular safety of the two "remains unresolved"[4].

Evidence in the other direction exists too. The HERO trial compared the oral antagonist relugolix with leuprolide and found major adverse cardiovascular events of 2.9% against 6.2% (hazard ratio 0.46, 0.24 to 0.88), but that was a safety endpoint, not the question the trial was built to answer[5]; a meta-analysis of 9 randomised trials plus 5 real-world studies, 62,160 men, reported fewer cardiovascular events with antagonists (0.66, 0.53 to 0.82), the advantage appearing only in men with cardiovascular disease at baseline[6]. Then the Taiwanese reality: section 5 of the current National Health Insurance (健保) drug coverage rules contains no clause for relugolix; what is covered is the Gn-RH analogue class for prostate cancer (no prior review required), and degarelix restricted to adult men with advanced hormone-dependent prostate cancer[7].

So what I say in clinic: rather than agonising over which injection, get the blood pressure, lipids, blood sugar and smoking under control. That is the part you can be confident of changing.

Bone loss is real, and Taiwanese coverage sits on the far side of a fracture

The numbers are in full in "Back pain plus a weak leg: do not wait"; three are worth having here. ADT costs the lumbar spine a further 3.60 percentage points of bone mineral density on average[8]; among 50,613 American men with prostate cancer surviving over five years from diagnosis, 19.4% of those who had the injections fractured, against 12.6% of those who had not (P<0.001)[9]; and in a meta-analysis of 16 cohort studies and 519,168 men the odds ratio was 1.39 for any fracture and 1.55 for fractures needing admission[10] — an odds ratio divides one group's "with against without" odds by the other's; it does not mean the probability becomes 1.39 times as large.

This one can be blocked. The HALT trial randomised 1,468 men to denosumab or placebo: new vertebral fractures at 36 months were 1.5% against 3.9% (relative risk 0.38, 0.19 to 0.78)[11].

The Taiwanese gap comes down to one sentence: without bone metastases, bone loss caused by ADT alone falls under the osteoporosis coverage rules, and "currently receiving androgen deprivation therapy" is not on that list of high-risk factors[7] — so a man who wants a drug before he has fractured does not meet the criteria as the rules read. The full text and the two other routes are in "Back pain plus a weak leg: do not wait". The National Health Insurance Administration uses the WHO definition of osteoporosis and requires measurement by DXA[12]; for the conditions and frequency under which DXA itself is covered in men on ADT I found no formal document, so ask your case manager (個管師) or the hospital billing office (醫務課).

Which makes two questions enough next visit: "how long have I been on the injections, and should I have a baseline DXA?" Asking beats finding out afterwards.

The weight goes up and the muscle goes down — and this one has a randomised trial against it

A prospective study of 40 men on leuprolide for 48 weeks measured body composition: testosterone fell 96.3%, weight rose 2.4%, percentage body fat rose 9.4%, percentage lean tissue fell 2.7%, abdominal subcutaneous fat rose 11.1%, and intra-abdominal fat did not change significantly[13]. What men describe as going soft after starting the injections is this.

Exercise is the item here with randomised evidence behind it. Fifty-seven men on androgen suppression without bone metastases were randomised to 12 weeks of resistance plus aerobic exercise or usual care; that group improved in lean tissue, muscle strength, the 6-metre walk and 6-metre backwards walk, general health and fatigue, with no adverse events during the programme[14]. The POLARIS individual patient data meta-analysis (7 trials, 560 men, mean age 69.5) found resistance-based programmes improved whole-body and limb lean mass and skeletal muscle index, and the men in the worst baseline condition gained the most[15]. The man who feels he has no energy to exercise is often exactly the man who should start. Anyone with bone metastases or poor bone density needs an individual assessment before resistance work.

You do not have to put up with hot flushes, and mood and memory need separating

In castrated men the prevalence, duration and burden of hot flushes match those in menopausal women[16]. The Alliance A222001 randomised trial enrolled men with at least 28 hot flushes a week (mean 10.1 a day) and compared two doses of oxybutynin against placebo; at six weeks the mean reduction in daily flushes was 2.15 with placebo and 4.77 and 6.89 with the two doses, the bother scales improved too, and there were no treatment-related grade 3 or higher adverse events[17]. Something to discuss, not something to endure.

On cognition I keep the two halves apart, because they get compressed into one frightening sentence. For overall cognitive impairment defined by formal neuropsychological testing, a meta-analysis of 6 studies found the prospective data negative (odds ratio 1.57, 0.50 to 4.92, P=0.44) and the retrospective data raised but not significant, the authors concluding the results were "inconclusive"[18]. On the other side, a meta-analysis of 14 studies found ADT associated with new-onset dementia (hazard ratio 1.21, 1.11 to 1.33) and Alzheimer's disease (1.16, 1.09 to 1.24), 1.36 in men treated more than 12 months[19] — all observational, association rather than cause. The honest version: testing finds no consistent impairment, but the databases do see a signal for dementia diagnoses, and it tracks with duration of use. Both are true and they do not conflict. Low mood and irritability are just as common, and easier raised than endured.

"On and off is easier on the body" — proven in one population only

NCIC PR.7 enrolled men with a rising PSA after radiotherapy, 1,386 randomised: median survival 8.8 years intermittent against 9.1 continuous (hazard ratio 1.02, 0.86 to 1.21), non-inferiority met — the gap is small enough to accept, not that intermittent is better; 35% of that group had full testosterone recovery and 79% recovered to the entry threshold, with potential benefits in physical capacity, fatigue, urinary symptoms, hot flushes, libido and erectile function[20].

The metastatic population is different. SWOG 9346 analysed 1,535 men, median follow-up 9.8 years: median survival 5.8 years continuous against 5.1 intermittent (hazard ratio for death with intermittent 1.10, 90% confidence interval 0.99 to 1.23). The intermittent group did better only on erectile function and mental health at month 3, with no difference afterwards. The authors' conclusion is "statistically inconclusive": the confidence interval crossed the non-inferiority boundary, so a 20% higher risk of death with intermittent therapy cannot be excluded, while there were also too few events to confirm intermittent really is worse[21]. So this is discussed by population, not settled with "you can take a break". Stopping or delaying an injection is a decision to make with your treating physician, not before.

What means calling the team the same day, and four questions for the next clinic

The urgent one first. New back pain together with leg weakness, numbness, or a change in bladder or bowel function means being seen the same day, not at your next appointment. That is how spinal cord compression presents; the window is measured in hours, and function lost past it does not reliably come back. Chest pain, sudden breathlessness, one-sided weakness or slurred speech are same-day matters too.

Four that are not urgent but worth asking: how long is this stretch of ADT meant to run, and why that length? Have I had a baseline bone density measurement? Who is managing my blood pressure, lipids and blood sugar? And if hot flushes are breaking my sleep, is there something we can discuss? Write them down and take them in — that is what makes the ten minutes usable.

From the physician

How long your course runs, and why that length, is a question with an answer — ask it. Ask about a baseline bone density measurement while you are there. Nothing here is a reason to stop or delay an injection on your own; that decision belongs with your treating physician.
