Metastatic disease: what happens next

The course of metastatic breast cancer is decided mainly by subtype, with median survival running from 14 months to 42 months; and for oligometastatic disease the randomised breast-specific data do not yet support it.

Breast Cancer Guide · After Treatment · 17 references
#Recurrence#Decisions#Quality of Life#Survivorship

Underneath the phrase "metastatic breast cancer", the longest subtype and the shortest differ threefold — but that threefold spread is not all this article has to handle. Taiwan's CDK4/6 provision has three walls (bone-only metastasis is not covered, a lifetime cap of 24 months, and ER or PR must be above 30%), and one of them is shorter than the median progression-free survival in the first line of the trials.

"How long have I got?" It is the question I am asked most often in clinic and the one that least deserves a one-sentence answer. Not out of squeamishness, but because the words "metastatic breast cancer" contain several courses of disease that are a long way apart from one another. Before I give you a number, I have to see which one your report says.

One phrase, "metastatic breast cancer", four subtypes, a threefold spread in median survival

A German cancer centre in Heidelberg prospectively enrolled 1,000 patients with metastatic breast cancer between 2014 and 2022: 132 (13%) triple-negative, 189 (19%) HER2-positive, 609 (61%) hormone-receptor-positive/HER2-negative. Median overall survival for the whole group was 31.7 months; by molecular subtype it was 42 months for HER2-positive, 39 months for luminal A-like, 21 months for luminal B-like and 14 months for triple-negative. At the fifth year of follow-up, 17% of patients were still alive[1].

What a "median" means is this: line that group up, and half of them are above it and half below. It is a statistic about the group of people who came before, not a prediction about you; and these data come from a real-world cohort at one centre, not from a randomised trial.

I set the four numbers out to make two sentences fall over at the same time. "Metastatic means terminal" does not hold — luminal A-like is 39 months, and 17% were still there at year 5. "Metastatic breast cancer can now be lived with long term" does not hold either — triple-negative is 14 months. Both go wrong in the same place: no subtype attached.

Being metastatic from the outset and becoming metastatic after treatment are not the same curve either: median overall survival in 232 patients with de novo metastatic disease was 9 months longer than in 612 with recurrent metastatic disease (36.4 against 27.4 months), though part of that gap comes from the mix of subtypes[2] — a comparison at the level of populations, not a prognosis for an individual.

The goal of treatment changes to a different one, and that is not giving up

Treatment of early breast cancer all points at one thing: clear it out, do not let it come back. After metastasis that goal is exchanged for another one — hold it down at a cost you can bear, for as long as possible. Those two sentences are very different, and yet on the calendar they look much alike: the same appointments on time, the same scans, the same next line waiting. In clinic I separate "cure" from "control" when I speak, because people who have run the two together think, at their first change of line, that everything before it was wasted. Changing line is not a failure; it is the next step that was written into the plan from the start.

CDK4/6 inhibitors: five trials that cannot be compressed into one sentence

This is the passage most often squeezed down to "this class of drug prolongs survival", but the facts are more broken up than that. Three ribociclib trials reached a statistically significant improvement in overall survival. MONALEESA-2 enrolled postmenopausal women with hormone-receptor-positive/HER2-negative advanced breast cancer, median overall survival 63.9 against 51.4 months, hazard ratio for death 0.76 (95% confidence interval 0.63–0.93) — a hazard ratio divides the rate at which deaths occur in one group by the rate in the other, and 0.76 means the arm with the drug added moved more slowly, not that "24% fewer people die"[3]. MONALEESA-3, in postmenopausal women in the first or second line, gave 42-month overall survival of 57.8% against 45.9%, hazard ratio 0.72 (0.57–0.92); median progression-free survival in the first-line group was 33.6 against 19.2 months[4]. MONALEESA-7's population differs from the other four — 672 premenopausal or perimenopausal women with hormone-receptor-positive/HER2-negative disease, 42-month overall survival 70.2% against 46.0%, hazard ratio 0.71 (0.54–0.95)[5].

The other two did not. PALOMA-2 enrolled 666 postmenopausal women with advanced disease and no prior systemic therapy: median overall survival 53.9 against 51.2 months, hazard ratio 0.96 (0.78–1.18), one-sided P=0.34, not significant; the authors point out that an imbalance in the proportion with unknown survival status limits interpretation[6]. MONARCH 3 enrolled 493 patients under the same conditions: hazard ratio 0.804 (0.637–1.015), P=0.0664, not statistically significant, median overall survival 66.8 against 53.7 months[7].

Three walls in Taiwan: bone-only disease, 24 months, and 30%

The NHI provision for CDK4/6 inhibitors (9.72, postmenopausal, after distant metastasis) requires all of the following: ER or PR above 30%; HER2-negative; no visceral crisis and no central nervous system metastases; and bone must not be the only site of metastasis. Premenopausal patients and men with breast cancer must use it with an aromatase inhibitor and a GnRH analogue, on the same conditions. Prior review is required; ribociclib or palbociclib, one of the two, courses counted together, with a lifetime cap of 24 months[8].

Three of these get hit in practice. One, a hormone-receptor-positive patient with bone metastases only does not meet this provision, and none of the five trials above had that exclusion. Two, the lifetime cap of 24 months is shorter than the 33.6-month median progression-free survival of first-line MONALEESA-3[4]. Three, "ER or PR above 30%" is stricter than the "hormone-receptor-positive" the trials used. This is what the provisions look like at present; it is nobody's oversight, and what you can do is know which side of the line you are on. Abemaciclib has its own provision in the NHI (9.107), but that one is written for adjuvant treatment of early breast cancer, not after metastasis; CDK4/6 after metastasis runs through 9.72 above, and the products listed under 9.72 are ribociclib and palbociclib. Which provision your particular cell can reach needs confirming with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.

Oligometastatic disease is being talked up too far; breast cancer's own randomised data do not support it yet

"There aren't many deposits — wouldn't it be fine if we destroyed them all?" That thought is natural enough, and there is a frequently quoted trial holding it up — but the trial cannot carry that much weight. SABR-COMET enrolled 99 patients with a controlled primary and 1 to 5 metastases, randomised 1:2 to standard palliative treatment or to that plus stereotactic ablative radiotherapy to all lesions: median overall survival 28 against 41 months, hazard ratio 0.57 (0.30–1.10, P=0.090)[9].

Its limitations almost always drop out in the retelling: a randomised phase 2 screening design with two-sided α set at 0.20 (a P value under 0.20 counts as positive), and there were 3 treatment-related deaths, 4.5%, in the radiotherapy arm against 0 in the control arm; the authors state explicitly that a phase 3 trial is needed to demonstrate an overall survival benefit definitively[9]. For a breast cancer reader the crucial thing is the mix of primary sites: of the 99, only 18 had a breast primary[10] — in the trial most often cited in support of local treatment for oligometastatic disease, that is how many breast cancer patients there were.

There is only one set of randomised data done specifically in breast cancer, and it is negative. EXTEND is a multicentre randomised phase 2 basket trial enrolling patients with 5 or fewer metastases, and its breast basket randomised 43 patients. At a median follow-up of 24.8 months, adding metastasis-directed therapy did not improve progression-free survival (median 15.6 against 24.9 months, hazard ratio 0.91, P=0.86); overall survival and time to next line of treatment were not improved either[11]. The 2026 aggregated analysis of all baskets did see an improvement in progression-free survival overall (hazard ratio 0.54, 0.41–0.72), but broken out by basket, the breast and kidney baskets were labelled by the authors as inconclusive[12].

So the only statement I can stand behind is this one: the randomised breast-specific data do not yet support it. This is a direction being studied, not "suitable patients may consider it". There is one further phase 3 trial in de novo oligometastatic breast cancer that has only been reported at an international meeting; I could not find a journal paper I could cite, so I am not going to quote any figure from it or imply which way it went. For the reimbursement conditions of stereotactic radiotherapy to metastases in Taiwan I could find no specific provision, so that one needs confirming with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.

HER2-positive brain metastases: two datasets at two different levels of evidence

HER2CLIMB is a randomised placebo-controlled trial enrolling patients with HER2-positive metastatic breast cancer previously treated with trastuzumab, pertuzumab and T-DM1: 2-year overall survival 44.9% against 26.6% (hazard ratio 0.66, 0.50–0.88)[13]; in the 291 patients with brain metastases, the hazard ratio for intracranial progression or death was 0.32 (0.22–0.48) — 0.32 means intracranial events accrued at roughly a third of the rate of the control arm, which is not the same as "two-thirds of people will not progress"; median overall survival in that same group was 18.1 against 12.0 months[14]. In Taiwan, a full-text search of the drug reimbursement regulations for tucatinib returns zero hits — there is at present no corresponding NHI provision[8].

DESTINY-Breast12 is a single-arm phase 3b/4 study with no control group and no hypothesis testing: in the brain metastasis cohort (n=263), investigator-reported interstitial lung disease or pneumonitis was 16%[15], and a figure like that cannot be lined up alongside one from a randomised controlled trial. A dry cough, breathlessness or fever while on T-DXd is a same-day phone call; the blood counts and liver function on a CDK4/6 inhibitor need watching too. The thresholds for the other drugs used in the metastatic setting are each set out in "Which side effects mean phoning the same day".

Palliative care is not the referral you make once treatment stops working

The 2024 ASCO palliative care guideline update recommends referring patients with advanced solid tumours to a specialist palliative care team early in the course of the disease and alongside active cancer treatment, with particular urgency where symptoms are uncontrolled or quality of life is a concern[16].

The effect has to be reported as it is. The 2017 Cochrane review took in 7 randomised trials and 1,614 adults with advanced cancer: early palliative care improved quality of life, but the effect size was small (standardised mean difference 0.27, low-certainty evidence); and for survival (4 trials, 800 people) no difference was shown (hazard ratio for death 0.85, 0.56–1.28, very low certainty)[17]. Which is to say: it does not make people go sooner, and there is no evidence that it makes them live longer. How shared palliative care and outpatient referral actually work differs from hospital to hospital, so ask your case manager about that one.

The next twelve months, paced by lines of treatment and by scans

  • Confirm your subtype first. The numbers are far apart between subtypes, and until you know which cell you are in there is no number that applies[1].

  • Change the unit on your calendar to "line of treatment plus scan": roughly how long this line runs, when the next scan is, and what would have to be seen before changing line[4].

  • If you are on a CDK4/6 inhibitor, keep a note on the same page of how many months have been used, and ask in clinic what the next step is once the cap is reached[8]. That needs preparing in advance, not asking on the day it runs out.

  • Treat a palliative care appointment as one of the squares that can be booked this year, not the last square of all[16].

  • Drugs for bone metastases and the order of the dental work are in "Bone density, bone-targeted drugs and exercise"; what the alterations on your report can be connected to is in "After metastasis, genomic testing is about finding a drug".

From the physician

These are medians for a group, not a prediction for you. Confirm two things this month: which line of the report carries your subtype; and if you are on a CDK4/6 inhibitor, how many months have been used and what the next step is once the lifetime cap is reached — that has to be asked in advance, not on the day it expires.
