THE LOW-RISK BOX: HOW AGGRESSIVE TO BE

What the operation takes with it

For voice and for calcium, the published complication rates run 2.3% to 26% and 0.5% to 65%; the spread comes from how you look and how you define.

#Surgery#Side Effects#Voice#Rehabilitation

You assume you can put two hospitals' complication figures side by side. A hospital that scopes every patient after surgery will always look worse on paper — practising routine laryngoscopy is itself an independent factor associated with unilateral vocal cord palsy.

"Will my voice be hoarse afterwards?" "What are parathyroids, and why do they keep taking blood?" I get asked both at almost every preoperative clinic.

The hard part is that if you go home and look it up, you will find figures that are wildly far apart. Nobody is lying to you, and it is not that certain hospitals are bad. This number has no single denominator. It turns on two things: how you look, and how you define. This article settles that first, then gives a few sets of figures that do have a clear denominator, then explains why blood keeps getting drawn. Whether to operate at all is in 〈Watching instead of operating, on conditions〉, how much comes out is in 〈Half the gland, or all of it〉, and the node question is in 〈Should the nodes come out too〉.

Voice: 2.3% to 26%, and the difference is whether everyone gets scoped

A systematic review of 27 studies and roughly 25,000 people produced these averages: temporary recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy 9.8%, permanent 2.3%. The same review also writes that the reported rate varies with the method of examining the larynx, and ranges from 2.3% all the way to 26%[1]. A tenfold spread, and the source of it is not the surgery. It is whether anyone went and looked.

The more direct evidence comes from a Scandinavian audit of 26 departments and 3,660 operations: practising routine laryngoscopy is itself an independent factor associated with unilateral vocal cord palsy, odds ratio 1.92[2]. Put the other way round, the harder a hospital looks, the worse its figures read on paper. I want you to hold on to that, because you are very likely to compare two hospitals' numbers.

Parathyroids: 0.5% to 65%, and the difference is definition and cut-off

Another systematic review, of 45 studies and 23,164 people, opens with exactly this: the incidence of hypoparathyroidism in the literature ranges from 0.5% to 65%, mainly because each study defines it differently, and especially because they apply different time cut-offs to separate temporary from permanent[3].

What is interesting is that the same review calculated both cut-offs and compared them: permanent hypoparathyroidism came out at 4.11% using 6 months and 4.08% using 12 months, which is no difference at all. But that was one analysis applying both consistently; between separate studies the comparison still does not hold. The range the 2025 edition gives for itself is 14% to 43% temporary and 1% to 25% permanent[4] — a range so wide it stops looking like a medical figure, and the reason it is that wide is everything above.

Are there numbers with a clear denominator

Yes, but they have to be read together with the cohort they came from. That Scandinavian audit again (benign and malignant mixed, 3,660 operations): unilateral recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy 3.9%, bilateral 0.2%, still palsied at 6 months 0.97%; postoperative bleeding 2.1%, postoperative infection 1.6%, with an odds ratio of 8.18 for infection in those who had node surgery. Among the 1,648 who had bilateral operations, low calcium requiring active vitamin D was 9.9% at the first clinic visit and 4.4% at 6 months[2].

A second set comes from one high-volume surgeon whose patients all had fibreoptic laryngoscopy before and immediately after surgery, across 2,527 nerves at risk: impaired vocal fold motion afterwards 2.9%; permanent in 9 (0.4%), and 3 of those were nerves deliberately divided during the operation because cancer had invaded them[5]. This is the floor under conditions where everybody is examined.

For the gap between total thyroidectomy and lobectomy, the meta-analysis gives relative risks: temporary low calcium 10.67 (95% confidence interval 5.75 to 19.31), permanent low calcium 3.17 (1.72 to 5.83), temporary recurrent laryngeal nerve injury 1.69 (1.30 to 2.20), permanent 1.85 (1.28 to 2.69), postoperative bleeding 2.58 (1.69 to 3.93)[6]. The endpoints there are low calcium and nerve injury themselves, and the denominator is the studies that were included — not any one hospital.

Why they keep drawing blood for calcium

Definitions first. The 2018 statement written specifically about postoperative hypoparathyroidism is clear: it means a low parathyroid hormone level accompanied by a low calcium, both together. A postoperative parathyroid hormone below 15 pg/mL indicates increased risk of acute hypoparathyroidism. For management, the statement lists three strategies: give oral calcium and vitamin D to everyone up front, give them selectively according to the postoperative parathyroid hormone level, or simply follow a series of calcium levels. It also warns to watch for rebound hypercalcaemia afterwards[7]. The same document lists the risk factors: bilateral operations, autoimmune thyroid disease, central neck dissection, substernal goitre, surgeon inexperience and malabsorption.

Recommendation 24B of the 2025 edition advises that after a total thyroidectomy or a central neck dissection, calcium and vitamin D should be given directed by parathyroid hormone, which lowers rates of hypocalcaemia and shortens hospital stay compared with observation and serial calcium alone. Strong recommendation, moderate certainty. The same document says the parathyroid glands and their blood supply should be preserved during surgery, and that when a gland truly cannot be saved, it should be auto-transplanted into nearby muscle after frozen section confirms it is benign parathyroid tissue[4].

So the blood you are giving is watching two things: whether your calcium is enough right now, and whether those glands are still working. I am not going to give you doses or durations for calcium and active vitamin D here — that follows the postoperative protocol of the hospital that operates on you.

Permanent damage is not "just take calcium" — but look at whose data that is

A Swedish national registry followed 4,899 people who had total thyroidectomy for benign thyroid disease, defining permanent hypoparathyroidism as active vitamin D use beyond 6 months after surgery, which applied to 246 (5.2%); over a mean 4.4 years the adjusted hazard ratio for death was 2.09 (95% confidence interval 1.04 to 4.20)[8]. A second paper from the same registry, 4,828 people: hazard ratio 4.88 (2.00 to 11.95) for renal insufficiency, and among those who already had cardiovascular disease, 1.88 (1.02 to 3.47) for cardiovascular events[9].

The cohort in both papers had surgery for benign thyroid disease, not thyroid cancer. So I will not translate this into "if your parathyroids fail after thyroid cancer surgery you die early" — that is taking a number across a line. What it can tell us is this: permanent hypoparathyroidism is not a small thing you swallow a calcium tablet for, and it deserves to be counted before surgery, when you are deciding how much to take out.

A changed voice does not have to mean a damaged nerve

A single-institution survey deliberately excluded anyone with documented nerve injury, at a median 10.7 years from surgery; of the 308 who completed a standardised questionnaire by telephone, 113 (37%) reported a voice problem, but only 22 (7.1%) scored above the questionnaire's abnormal cut-off. The commonest complaints were "the clarity of my voice is unpredictable" (23%), "people have difficulty understanding me in a noisy room" (23%) and "I feel as though I have to strain to produce voice" (21%)[10]. The 2025 edition also writes in its text that long-term voice change in people without nerve injury runs over 30%[4].

Those two numbers must be read together. The 37% alone frightens people; the 7.1% alone makes anyone with symptoms feel they are making a fuss. Separately, the 2025 edition puts preoperative voice assessment at strong recommendation, moderate certainty — get on record what your voice sounded like before the operation, so there is something to compare against later[4].

Volume: three databases, three thresholds

Recommendation 6 of the 2025 edition reads: because high-volume thyroid surgeons (more than 25 to 50 thyroidectomies a year) have on average lower complication rates and better outcomes, patients with thyroid cancer should be offered referral to a high-volume surgeon, particularly for tumours requiring more extensive surgery. Strong recommendation, moderate certainty[4]. Note that what it gives is a range, not a number.

Because the thresholds people calculate really do differ. A 1998 analysis of 5,860 discharges from a single American state: in-hospital complications 5.1% in the highest-volume group, 6.1% in the two middle groups, 8.6% in the lowest; and complications and length of stay were more determined by the surgeon's experience than by hospital volume, which had no consistent association[11]. A 2017 analysis of 16,954 total thyroidectomies in a national inpatient sample put the inflection at 26 cases a year: a surgeon doing 1 a year carried 87% higher odds of complications, 2 to 5 cases 68%, 6 to 10 cases 42%, 11 to 15 cases 22%, 16 to 20 cases 10%, 21 to 25 cases 3%[12]. The United Kingdom registry of endocrine and thyroid surgery saw the decline only above 50 cases a year, with permanent hypoparathyroidism at 3% and recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy at 2.6% in the highest-volume group (more than 100 a year); the authors say themselves that a high proportion of the data is missing and the result may be biased[13].

Three thresholds: 26, 50, 100. So there is no consensus on what counts as high volume in the first place. One direction is steady, though: total thyroidectomy carries more complications than a unilateral operation — in an analysis of 62,722 cases, 20.4% against 10.8%[14].

Two numbers to get before you go home

At the preoperative clinic you can read these out. "Do you scope every patient after surgery, or only the ones with symptoms?" "How many months on active vitamin D before you count it as permanent?" "Does my operation involve the central compartment nodes? If it does, how much does that change the risk to my parathyroids?" And one to ask before you sign the consent form: "Can I have a voice assessment first? I would like a record of how I sounded beforehand."

On the day you are discharged, get two more numbers: what my calcium is today, and what my parathyroid hormone is. Write them down, and ask how many more times they need checking and who is going to follow them.

From the physician

You can read these out at your next appointment: "Do you scope every patient after surgery, or only the ones with symptoms?" "How many months on active vitamin D before you count it as permanent?" "How many calcium tests will I need after discharge, and who follows them?" Before surgery, add one: "Can I have a voice assessment first?"
