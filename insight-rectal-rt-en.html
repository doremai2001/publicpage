Five fractions or twenty-five?

Two pre-operative schedules for rectal cancer. Does a fivefold difference in fractions mean a difference in outcome? The answer shifted again in the past two years.

Insights · Radiation Therapy · 4 references
#Rectal#Chemotherapy#Short Course#Clinical Trials

The sentence I hear most often is: "If it only takes five days, is it enough?" That is a fair worry. But the difference between these schedules is not about being enough — it is about which risk you would rather carry: local recurrence, or distant metastasis.

Two radiation schedules are in routine use before rectal cancer surgery. One is short-course radiotherapy: five treatments over five days. The other is long-course chemoradiotherapy, daily radiation across five to six weeks with an oral chemotherapy drug taken alongside it. A patient education article from the Koo Foundation Sun Yat-Sen Cancer Center in Taiwan notes that each short-course session runs only two or three minutes, causes little discomfort, and lets surgery follow sooner[1]. The question patients ask in clinic is the obvious one. If the number of sessions differs this much, does the result differ too? Researchers have chased that question for more than a decade, and the answer moved again in the past two years.

In 2023, Annals of Surgery published the five-year follow-up of the RAPIDO trial. This phase 3 trial randomized 920 patients with locally advanced rectal cancer, and at three years local control looked comparable in both arms. By five years, locoregional recurrence had reached 10% in the group given short-course radiotherapy followed by preoperative chemotherapy, against 6% with long-course chemoradiotherapy (p=0.027), while the short-course arm kept its advantage in reducing distant metastases[2]. The investigators noted that the short-course arm more often received older three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy, which may explain part of the gap. A different signal comes from a 2026 network meta-analysis in BMC Medicine, which pooled 7 randomized trials and 1,132 patients with mismatch repair proficient (pMMR) tumors and ranked short-course radiotherapy plus an immune checkpoint inhibitor first for treatment response[3]. That ranking rests on indirect comparison across trials, and the authors state plainly that phase 3 confirmation is still needed.

So the answer is not that the short course is weaker. The trade-off has moved. ASCO released its first guideline written specifically for locally advanced rectal cancer in 2024, and on scheduling it currently leans toward long-course chemoradiotherapy while waiting for head-to-head trials such as ACO/ARO/AIO-18.1 to settle the question; the same guideline recommends total neoadjuvant therapy for low rectal tumors and for patients at higher risk of metastasis[4]. Medical centers in Taiwan run both schedules[1]. What actually decides the choice is tumor height, whether the circumferential resection margin is threatened, the status of lateral pelvic nodes, and whether six weeks of daily travel is workable for you. Only your radiation oncologist and colorectal surgeon hold that full picture. The question worth taking to your next appointment is which of these situations applies to you, rather than asking to switch or stop a schedule already underway.

From the physician

What decides the choice is tumour location, whether the circumferential resection margin is threatened, lateral nodal status, and whether you can manage six weeks of daily travel. That information sits with your radiation oncologist and colorectal surgeon. The question worth asking at your next visit is "which situation am I in", not whether to switch schedules because of an article.
