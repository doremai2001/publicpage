HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

Surgery or radiotherapy: not operating is not giving up

A randomised trial followed for twenty years found no survival difference between the two early-stage roads; what really needs avoiding is having both — in that trial, 64% of the surgery arm ended up having radiotherapy too.

Cervical Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 13 references
#Decisions#Surgery#Brachytherapy

This article is written for two readers at once: the person scheduled for chemoradiation who suspects she is beyond saving, and the person insisting on surgery who does not know that about half of those with tumours of 2 to 4 cm need further treatment after the operation. The answer is not in any specialty — it is in five fields of your pathology report.

My position first: definitive chemoradiation and brachytherapy are the treatments I deliver every day. So every sentence about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it — not more warmly.

"The doctor says I won't have surgery, just radiotherapy — does that mean I'm already inoperable?" I have heard this in clinic too many times, often from someone who has already cried in the corridor. So the conclusion goes first: not operating is not giving up. In cervical cancer, "surgery or radiotherapy" is a division of labour by stage and tumour characteristics, not a ranking of how desperate your situation is. This article lays out the evidence behind that division of labour — including a point that rarely gets explained properly: the price of choosing the wrong side is usually not "a worse result", it is "ending up having both treatments".

Early stage: two roads, and a randomised trial that followed them for twenty years

The Italian Landoni trial randomised 343 patients with stage IB to IIA disease (by the FIGO staging of the time) between 1986 and 1991 to radical surgery or radical radiotherapy, publishing in 1997: 5-year overall survival was 83% in both groups, disease-free survival 74% in both[1]. The 20-year update in 2017, with a minimum follow-up of 19 years: overall survival 72% with surgery and 77% with radiotherapy — no statistical difference (p=0.280); and in the multivariable analysis, what determined survival was histology, tumour size and lymph nodes — not which treatment was chosen[2]. Twenty years on, the authors wrote down the same conclusion: early cervical cancer has no single treatment of choice. One reminder belongs here: that was the surgery and radiotherapy of more than thirty years ago, and both sides are better today — so read this "no different" as a direction, not as your precise probability now[1][2].

In the same trial, the number that matters more is actually 64%

In Landoni's surgery arm, 64% (108 of 169) went on to receive radiotherapy after the operation because of what the pathology showed: 54% of those with tumours of 4 cm or under, and 84% of those above 4 cm[1]. Severe complications: 28% in the surgery arm against 12% with radiotherapy (p=0.0004), and the authors' own words were that the combination of surgery plus radiotherapy carried the worst morbidity[1]. So "the two are equivalent" and "the double-treatment problem" are two faces of the same dataset: insisting on operating first under the wrong conditions often buys the toxicity of two treatments for the effect of one. The 2023 guideline of the three European societies (ESGO/ESTRO/ESP) has made this a stated principle of treatment strategy: the combination of radical surgery and radiotherapy should be avoided, and when it is already foreseeable at diagnosis that adjuvant treatment would be needed after surgery, definitive chemoradiation with brachytherapy should be given instead — do not operate first[3]. That is the guideline in black and white, not one specialty's turf position.

Why does surgery so often need topping up — and what does the top-up buy?

Whether to add treatment after surgery depends on the pathology report. Intermediate risk (the GOG 92 trial's criteria: at least two of deep stromal invasion, lymphovascular invasion, larger tumour size): adjuvant radiotherapy cut recurrence from 28% to 15%, roughly halving the risk (relative risk 0.53 — a relative risk divides one group's event probability by the other's)[4]. But the 2006 long-term follow-up has to be reported as it is: the hazard ratio for overall survival was 0.70 and did not reach statistical significance (p=0.074) — a hazard ratio divides the speed at which events accumulate in one group by the speed in the other, and 0.70 leans favourable but did not cross the statistical threshold — so the benefit of adjuvant radiotherapy can be written at the level of "fewer recurrences", not "longer life"[5]. High risk (nodal metastasis, positive margins, parametrial involvement) calls for more than radiotherapy alone: the Peters trial showed 4-year overall survival of 81% with post-operative chemoradiation against 71% with radiotherapy alone[6]. How often does this happen? Across three surgical databases combined — 675 patients with FIGO 2018 stage IB2 tumours (2 to 4 cm) undergoing radical hysterectomy — 51% received adjuvant treatment after surgery[7]. And that probability can usually be estimated before anyone operates — which is exactly why the guideline demands the estimate be made, and chemoradiation chosen outright when the answer is foreseeable[3].

Locally advanced: chemoradiation is curative in intent, and here are the numbers

For locally advanced disease from IB3 up, the ESGO 2023 standard is definitive concurrent chemoradiation (evidence level I, A), with image-guided brachytherapy as a necessary component[3]. "Not operating is not giving up" cannot stay a slogan — it needs numbers: retroEMBRACE, a 12-centre cohort of 731 locally advanced patients, reported 5-year overall survival of 65%[8]; the prospective EMBRACE-I study of 1,341 patients delivered results in the same direction[9]; and in the contemporary randomised INTERLACE trial, the arm receiving standard chemoradiation alone had 5-year overall survival of 72%[10]. That is the report card of a treatment given to cure. Why brachytherapy cannot be substituted, and what those days are actually like, is the whole of "Brachytherapy: the scariest name, the decisive days"; why chemotherapy runs weekly through the radiotherapy is in "Why chemotherapy runs alongside radiotherapy".

"Once chemoradiation is done, why not take the uterus out as well?" — tried; it was not better

The GOG 71 trial randomised 256 patients with stage IB tumours of 4 cm or more, comparing radiotherapy alone with radiotherapy plus extrafascial hysterectomy: no statistical difference in survival, and in the authors' own words, "overall, there was no clinically important benefit"[11]. The 2022 Cochrane meta-analysis reads the same: chemotherapy followed by surgery versus chemoradiation gave an overall survival hazard ratio of 0.94 — no difference — and pooling two trials, the surgery arm's 5-year disease-free survival was actually worse (57% against 65.6%)[12]. The ESGO guideline states plainly that it is not recommended[3]. "I'll only feel safe if someone has operated" has no evidence to stand on here.

No winner — each has its home ground

Reading this article as "radiotherapy beats surgery" is as much a misreading as "it only counts as cure if you can operate". An early-stage patient suited to surgery who has the operation and is finished with a single treatment — that is a good outcome. A locally advanced patient having definitive chemoradiation is heading for cure just the same — it is not the consolation prize. The genuine grey zone is the cell of IB3/IIA2 with negative nodes — where the guideline itself concedes the evidence is limited and asks for both options to be laid out with their trade-offs, discussed by a multidisciplinary team, with referral to a centre that has one[3]. So the right way to open this decision is to have both gynaecologic oncology and radiation oncology look at your file, hear both, then decide.

Before that conversation, find these five fields

The data that decide this question are already in the reports you hold. The pathology or biopsy report: histological type (squamous or adenocarcinoma), tumour size, lymphovascular invasion (LVSI), depth of stromal invasion; the imaging report: tumour size on MRI and nodal status. These five fields are precisely what the Landoni multivariable analysis and the guideline's risk stratification run on[2][3]. The Taiwan side: radical hysterectomy for cervical cancer (80413B) and brachytherapy are both items listed in the NHI fee schedule[13]; for the detailed coverage conditions and self-paid items, confirm with the medical affairs office or your case manager. Once the papers are together, this is the sentence to get out of your mouth: "With my stage and tumour characteristics, if I have surgery first, how likely is it that I will need radiotherapy or chemoradiation afterwards?" If the answer is more than half, take the second section of this article with you and talk to both specialties before deciding.

From the physician

If you have been told the plan is chemoradiation, hold on to the words "curative intent" — that is a route to cure, not a consolation. At the next visit, bring the pathology and imaging reports and ask one question: with my characteristics, if I operate first, how likely is it I will need further treatment afterwards?
