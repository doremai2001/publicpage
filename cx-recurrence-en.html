After recurrence: the roads that remain

Inside the pelvis and distant are two roads: one still holds options with curative intent, the other's goal is control and quality of life. The numbers, the costs and Taiwan's reimbursement, each carrying its label.

Cervical Cancer Guide · After Treatment · 11 references
#Recurrence#Decisions#Clinical Trials#NHI Coverage

Recurrence is not one diagnosis; it is a set of questions: where, how many sites, confirmed by pathology or not. Different answers, completely different roads — from an operation with five-year survival above half (but 4.3% in the wrong candidate), to self-paid immunotherapy, to the palliative care clinic that deserves an early slot — every road has its numbers and its preconditions.

My position first: definitive chemoradiation and brachytherapy are the treatments I deliver every day. So every sentence about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it — not more warmly.

When the word "recurrence" comes down, most people can hear only one meaning: it's over. But in the clinic, that word has to be followed at once by a question: recurred where? A single recurrence inside the pelvis and multiple distant metastases are two completely different roads — on the first, options with curative intent still exist; on the second, the goal is control and quality of life. Laying those two maps open is why this article exists.

Pin down the position before discussing roads — this step cannot be skipped

The ESGO 2023 guideline writes the thresholds clearly: suspected recurrence should be confirmed pathologically wherever possible; multiple nodal or distant metastases (beyond oligometastasis), or multifocal disease extensively invading the pelvic wall, should not be regarded as candidates for curative treatment; conversely, oligometastatic and oligorecurrent disease should be considered for treatment with curative potential; and treatment decisions in recurrence are to be centralised in a multidisciplinary team[1]. In plain words: first nail down "where it is and how many sites" with imaging and biopsy, then decide which road — and that decision should not be made by any single specialty on its own.

Central recurrence inside the pelvis: one very large operation, and both ends of its numbers

A pelvis that has been irradiated and recurs leaves few weapons, but they exist. For a tumour sitting centrally, not reaching the pelvic wall, with no disease outside the pelvis, the guideline's recommendation is pelvic exenteration — the major operation that removes the recurrent tumour together with the invaded bladder or rectum, level [IV, B][1]. Its modern results must be shown at both ends: in a 2025 international multicentre retrospective study of 862 patients, those whose prognostic factors fell in the most favourable group had five-year overall survival of 54.3%; those in the least favourable group, only 4.3%[2]. And a meta-analysis pooling 46 studies and 4,417 patients puts mortality within thirty days of the operation at 5.1%, with sepsis the leading cause[3]. Put together, these numbers mean: in highly selected patients this operation can buy long-term survival, and in the wrong patient it is an enormous price paid for a very small chance — which is why "can this be operated on" must be assessed by the multidisciplinary team of a specialist centre; and these data are retrospective, spanning eighteen years — keep that label in mind. Re-irradiation — irradiating again an area already treated — is another option, guideline level [IV, C], restricted to specialist centres[1]; the review literature says of itself that the studies are heterogeneous, large prospective series are lacking, and no firm conclusion can be drawn[4] — it is a frontier, not a standard.

First line for distant recurrence: every number has to be read with its population label on

For recurrence or metastasis that cannot be treated curatively, the first-line backbone is platinum-based chemotherapy, onto which two drugs can be added. For the immunotherapy pembrolizumab, the evidence is KEYNOTE-826, a double-blind phase III trial[5], and the numbers must be read with the PD-L1 label attached: in patients with CPS≥1 (about nine in ten), median overall survival was 28.6 against 16.5 months (the median is the number belonging to the person standing exactly in the middle when the whole group is lined up — half last longer than it; it is a scale for a group, not a deadline for you) — hazard ratio 0.60 — meaning death in the added-drug group arrived at only six-tenths the speed of the control group; in all patients, 26.4 against 16.8 months; at CPS≥10, 29.6 against 17.4 months[6]. The three populations contain one another — they are not three separate sets of patients; and in the small group with CPS below 1 the benefit has not been established — which is why the guideline writes the precondition in stone: pembrolizumab in first line, restricted to CPS≥1[1]. The cost, in the same paragraph: adverse events of grade 3 or above, 82.4% against 75.4%[6].

The other is bevacizumab, the anti-angiogenic drug: in the GOG-240 trial, adding it to chemotherapy extended median overall survival by a little over three months (16.8 against 13.3 months at the final analysis)[7][8]. It carries one cost that readers of this topic have to know about: fistula — an abnormal passage forming between the vagina and the rectum or bladder — of any grade in 15% against 1%, and every case occurred in patients who had been irradiated[8]. Almost every reader of this topic has been irradiated, so I am not going to hide that number inside "slightly more side effects". While on the drug: abdominal pain together with fever, or stool-like or urine-like discharge from the vagina, means contacting the team the same day; the other emergency thresholds are in the master table of "Getting through five or six weeks of pelvic radiotherapy". Adding immunotherapy during the concurrent chemoradiation phase is a different scene, written in "Why chemotherapy runs alongside radiotherapy".

After first line there are real options — not miracles, and not the end of the road

There are drugs beyond first line, with the numbers given straight. Cemiplimab (another immune checkpoint inhibitor): a randomised trial of 608 patients who had progressed after platinum and had not had immunotherapy; median overall survival 12.0 against 8.5 months[9]; the guideline lists it as a second-line recommendation for those who have not had immunotherapy[1]. Tisotumab vedotin (an antibody-drug conjugate): 11.5 against 9.5 months, response rate 17.8% against 5.2%, with single-agent chemotherapy as the comparator[10] — a two-month difference in medians and a response rate below one in five: a real option, not a new saviour. And for someone who already had pembrolizumab in first line, does immunotherapy again in second line work? The cemiplimab trial excluded anyone who had received immunotherapy[9]; that question currently has no answer, and I am saying so as it stands.

Reimbursement in Taiwan: one drug in writing, three at zero

I have been through section 9 of the NHI drug reimbursement regulations (the August 2026 edition) line by line. Bevacizumab has explicit wording (9.37.4): persistent, recurrent or metastatic cervical cancer, in combination with cisplatin plus paclitaxel, or — for those unable to take platinum — with paclitaxel plus topotecan, which are exactly GOG-240's two combinations; prior approval is required, each application is limited to 15 weeks, and continuation needs imaging evidence of no progression[11]. Pembrolizumab for cervical cancer: zero provisions — the cancers listed in the immune checkpoint inhibitor section do not include the cervix; cemiplimab is likewise at zero; tisotumab returns no hit anywhere in the full text[11]. Zero provisions means self-paid at present — though the provisions can be amended at any time; for the actual costs and the current reimbursement status, confirm with your case manager and the hospital's medical affairs office. I am not going to tell you whether paying for it yourself is "worth it" — that has to be worked out with your disease and your family together; it is not arithmetic an article can do for you.

Oligometastasis: it can be considered — and the blank in the evidence has to be said out loud

The metastases number only one or two — cut them out, ablate them, irradiate them: can that be done? The guideline does write it: local treatment of oligometastatic organ lesions may be considered (surgery, thermal ablation, stereotactic radiotherapy), and localised nodal recurrence outside the irradiated field may be considered for radical chemoradiation — but the evidence level of every one of these recommendations is IV, the expert-consensus level[1]. Cervical cancer's own randomised-trial evidence I have checked in the literature databases: it is zero. So the only sentence I can stand behind is this one: it can be considered after multidisciplinary assessment, on condition of honest expectations — it is a direction under study, not a standard proven to extend life.

Clinical trials and palliative care are two roads, not two endings

The guideline writes both as formal recommendations, not words of comfort. Patients with recurrent or metastatic disease are strongly encouraged to enrol in clinical trials ([V, A]); advanced patients should receive palliative care that is integrated with cancer treatment and started early ([IV, A]) — the original's emphasis is on early and in parallel, not a hand-over once treatment stops working[1]. On palliative care, I have written it up with randomised-trial data in the breast cancer topic's "Metastatic disease: what happens next": it does not make people go sooner, there is as yet no evidence it makes them live longer, and what it buys is days that are easier to live. How to judge whether "the new one is actually better", and where trial information can be looked up, is in "New does not mean better"; for Taiwan's trial-search channels, ask your attending physician or case manager for help.

Make "this line" the unit, and lay out the twelve months

After recurrence, the calendar's unit is no longer the "year"; it is "this line of treatment plus the next scan". Over the coming twelve months, put four things on it. One: write your recurrence site and its pathological confirmation into a single sentence — "central, inside the pelvis" or "multiple distant sites" — because whether each number in this article applies to you begins from that sentence[1]. Two: if pembrolizumab is being considered for first line, first confirm the CPS score on your report, then confirm how the cost will be handled[6][11]. Three: before every change of line, ask "is there a clinical trial that fits me" — in the guideline that sentence is a strong recommendation, not a courtesy[1]. Four: treat the palliative care clinic as a box that can be scheduled early, not the last box on the sheet[1]. Twelve months on, this table may have changed lines once, or not at all; its purpose is not prediction — it is that every step was one you chose.

From the physician

Every number in this article is tied to a population label — before you copy a number away, check which box you are in. Ask three questions at the next visit: which type is my recurrence? Is the goal cure or control? Is there a clinical trial that fits me? The answers will decide what the next twelve months look like.
