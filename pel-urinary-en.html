DURING TREATMENT AND AFTER

Those weeks of bladder and passing urine

One person in three develops new urinary symptoms during the course, most often pain on passing urine; it looks a great deal like an infection and mostly is not one — telling them apart takes urine testing plus culture.

Pelvic Radiotherapy Guide · During Treatment and After · 5 references
#Urinary Function#Side Effects#Urgent

For when the symptoms arrive I have cumulative dose in hand and no number of weeks: three-quarters of onsets fall between 16 and 45 Gy. The dose-volume relationship for the bladder is the weakest box in the whole dataset to begin with, which is why you will not find "at such and such a dose, such and such happens" anywhere in this article.

Halfway through the course you start going more often, getting caught short, and hurting when you pass urine. Most people's first thought is "I've got a water infection", and the next thing is a round of antibiotics. That judgement has a one in two chance of being wrong, and it can be wrong in more than one direction: treat radiation cystitis as an infection and the antibiotics are wasted; sit out a genuine infection as though it were a radiotherapy reaction and something goes wrong. This article is about that dividing line — and about something I have to admit first, which is that we know less about the bladder as an organ than we do about the bowel.

My position first: pelvic radiotherapy is what I do every day, so every good word about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it. Implants such as the rectal spacer are not something my department provides; I write about them as an option you can go and ask about, and for cost and for who performs them, please ask that department and the medical affairs office.

One person in three, and mostly it is pain on passing urine

There is not much data covering five cancers at once, so what I am using is a Brazilian single-centre prospective cohort of 72 patients, made up of 36% prostate, 31% rectal, 23% cervical, 6% endometrial and 4% anal canal. New or worsening urinary symptoms appeared during the course in 33% of them, which is 24 of the 72[1].

What those symptoms consisted of is not what most people picture. Among the 24 with symptoms, pain on passing urine accounted for 83% and nocturia for 16%, while new urgency and new haematuria were both 0%[1]. That 0% needs careful reading: in the same population, 49% already had symptoms before treatment started, and 63% of those already had urgency[1]. So it does not mean "radiotherapy does not cause urgency"; it means "in this group, urgency was mostly already there before radiotherapy". The study had only 72 patients, was single-centre, and stopped recruiting early because the results diverged so far from what had been expected, which is also why the predictor analysis the authors had planned was never done[1] — all three labels travel with the citation.

When it arrives: I have dose in hand, not weeks

The same study recorded the cumulative pelvic dose at the point symptoms appeared or worsened: below 5 Gy 0%, 6 to 15 Gy 8%, 16 to 25 Gy 25%, 26 to 35 Gy 25%, 36 to 45 Gy 25%, 46 to 55 Gy 4%, 56 to 65 Gy 8%, and above 65 Gy 4%[1]. Add the middle three together: three-quarters of onsets fall between a cumulative 16 and 45 Gy.

I cannot turn that into "which week". The unit that study used is cumulative dose, and everyone's prescription dose, dose per fraction and course length differ; a number of weeks forced out of it would be something I made up rather than something anybody measured. The only thing I can say is that most of them land in the middle stretch of the course. For a week-by-week script of acute urinary reactions in the pelvis, I could find no citable source, so this space stays blank.

It looks like an infection, and mostly it is not one

That was the question that study set out to answer. The 24 people who developed symptoms during the course had urine sent for culture, and only 1 was positive — 1.4% of all 72, and 4% of those with symptoms. The authors' conclusion is put plainly: the incidence of urinary tract infection was far below what had been expected, indicating that previously asymptomatic bacteriuria had become symptomatic because of the radiotherapy[1]. The introduction of the same paper puts the clinical difficulty more sharply still: the frequency, dysuria and haematuria caused by radiotherapy "may mimic the symptoms of urinary tract infection", and the community habit of giving empirical antibiotics to women as soon as they have symptoms "probably cannot be applied to patients undergoing pelvic radiotherapy"[1].

"Mostly not an infection" is not the same as "no need to test"

The data pointing the other way matters just as much. A retrospective study of women having pelvic radiotherapy for gynaecological cancer, 134 patients and 241 specimens, found bacterial growth in 34.9% of urine cultures[2]. That does not contradict the 1.4% above: the first was prospective, triggered by symptoms, and excluded anyone already on antibiotics or with a catheter; the second was retrospective and included only specimens somebody had already decided clinically to send. Different denominators, so of course different numbers.

That retrospective study also gives three useful details. First, on the dipstick, either nitrite or leukocyte esterase positive gave the highest sensitivity at 91.7%; both positive gave the highest specificity at 95.5%; but the negative predictive value of "no leukocytes" — the proportion of dipstick-negative results that really have no infection — reached only 87.0% at best, not 100%[2], so a negative dipstick cannot be used to rule infection out. Second, Escherichia coli accounted for only 22.6% in this population, a different distribution from ordinary community infection[2]. Third, the resistance figures are not low: 23.8% to TMP-SMX, 16.7% to ciprofloxacin and 11.1% to nitrofurantoin[2]. The authors conclude that urinalysis is less accurate in this population than in the general one, but remains useful, and that culture and sensitivity testing must be done[2].

What the two together give you is one sentence: the frequency, urgency and pain on passing urine that appear during a course are mostly not infection; but the way to tell is not by how it feels, it is urine testing plus culture; and in this population, going straight to empirical antibiotics is not a safe default.

We know less about the bladder than we do about the bowel

That is not modesty, it is sourced. In the collated review of organ dose tolerance, the bladder chapter says of itself that the partial-organ data are suspect; the small bowel and the rectum, by contrast, both have clear thresholds and model parameters — the wording and the citation for that passage are in this topic's "What you buy is side effects, not survival". So I am not going to sit in clinic and tell you "at such and such a bladder dose, such and such happens". That sentence structure will not hold up on the bladder.

The one set of bladder dose numbers I am willing to give covers a very narrow range. The EMBRACE-I cervical cancer cohort (concurrent chemoradiotherapy plus image-guided brachytherapy, and after excluding anyone whose bladder was invaded by tumour, 1,153 patients, median follow-up 48 months) reported grade 2 or worse cystitis at 8.8%, bleeding at 2.7% and fistula at 0.7% — that being the crude incidence of the bladder variety alone after the exclusion of bladder invasion; the five-year actuarial figure for the whole cohort is in "Fistula: risk, warning signs, order of care" — and found that raising the dose to the most exposed 2 cubic centimetres of bladder from 75 Gy to 80 Gy took 4-year grade 2 or worse cystitis from 8% to 13%[3]. Another analysis in the same cohort reported grade 2 or worse frequency at 13% and incontinence at 11%, and found that above an ICRU bladder reference point dose of 75 Gy, 5-year grade 2 or worse incontinence rose from 11% to 20%[4]. That is one cancer, one technique and one particular set of dose points; it is not a general rule to be applied to every pelvic patient. The acute and late urinary figures in prostate cancer, and the comparison between fractionation schedules, are written up more fully in the prostate topic's "That 13.7% is being read wrong"; late bleeding is in "Late bleeding: treatments and their evidence", and fistula in "Fistula: risk, warning signs, order of care".

What can wait for clinic, and what cannot

Expected, and fine to note down and raise at your next appointment: frequency, urgency, nocturia and burning on passing urine, from during the course through to a few weeks after it. Most of these fade with time.

The following three mean contacting the team or being seen the same day. First, blood in the urine together with being unable to pass urine or with a distended, painful lower abdomen — a clot can block the urethra, and that is dealt with on the spot. Second, being completely unable to pass urine. Third, fever together with pain on passing urine, or with shaking chills. One honest label on those: that dividing line is drawn from symptom logic and clinical convention, and I could find no patient-facing guideline provision I could quote word for word; there is a fuller list of urinary warning signs in "That 13.7% is being read wrong". As for how catheterisation and what follows it are actually managed once someone cannot pass urine, I have no source I can cite, so I am not opening that up here — if you cannot pass urine, come back the same day rather than holding on until your next appointment.

How to describe passing urine so that it gets dealt with

Two facts at the Taiwan end, first. The full NHI fee schedule contains 28019C cystoscopy at 1,800 points and 50011C bladder instillation at 260 points[5]. The prior-review provision for bladder irrigation solution as a special material in haemorrhagic cystitis is quoted in "That 13.7% is being read wrong"; that annex was filed some time ago, so have your case manager or the medical affairs office check the current version.

Then how to say it. "Passing urine is uncomfortable" almost never gets dealt with in clinic, because it maps onto ten different things at once. What works is a few numbers and one time: roughly how many times in the day, how many times you get up at night, which part of passing urine the pain comes in, and which day the change started. Those few lines feed back into something else as well — your bladder preparation. The bladder holding less well in the later part of the course is a measured norm, and that article is "Bladder and bowel: copying the same state each day"; if you cannot hold it, or cannot empty properly, say so the same day. That is not you failing to co-operate, it is information the team is meant to adjust on.

From the physician

Blood in the urine with being unable to pass urine or a distended, painful lower abdomen; being completely unable to pass urine; fever with pain on passing urine — those three mean coming back the same day, not holding on until your next appointment. At clinic, saying "how many times in the day, how many at night, which part of it hurts, and which day it started" is worth far more than "passing urine is uncomfortable".
