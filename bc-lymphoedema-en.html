A swollen arm: the real odds of lymphoedema

The incidence of lymphoedema is decided mainly by which axillary operation was done, from 8% to 30%; and the three precautions people are warned about longest are not in fact supported by the prospective data.

Breast Cancer Guide · After Treatment · 11 references
#Lymphoedema#Late Effects#Surgery#Exercise

In a prospective study that accumulated 3,041 measurements, blood draws, injections, blood-pressure measurements and air travel were none of them associated with an increase in arm volume; what was associated, in that same study, was body weight and cellulitis. What needs guarding against is not quite what is on the list of warnings.

"Is my arm going to swell up?" There is no one answer to that for all breast cancer patients, because the odds are set mostly not by the breast cancer but by which operation was done in your axilla. Within one and the same prospective study, the lowest group and the highest differ by close to fourfold. So before we talk about probability, please dig out your operation note and read exactly what it says: sentinel lymph node biopsy, or axillary lymph node dissection.

8% or 30%, and what decides it is what happened in the axilla

The cleanest set of figures comes from a prospective screening study at Massachusetts General Hospital: 1,815 patients with invasive breast cancer enrolled between 2005 and 2018, each measured at baseline before surgery and then followed with regular instrument-based measurement of arm volume, with lymphoedema defined as a relative increase in arm volume of 10% or more at least 3 months after surgery, median follow-up 52.7 months for the whole cohort. Five-year cumulative incidence divides into four cells: sentinel lymph node biopsy alone 8.0% (n=1,340), sentinel biopsy plus regional nodal irradiation 10.7% (n=121), axillary dissection alone 24.9% (n=91), axillary dissection plus regional nodal irradiation 30.1% (n=263)[1].

After adjustment for age, body mass index and the type of surgery and reconstruction, the risk with axillary dissection alone was significantly higher than with sentinel biopsy plus regional nodal irradiation (hazard ratio 2.66, P=0.02; a hazard ratio is the ratio between the rates at which events occur in two groups, and 2.66 means the first develops it a good deal faster). Between axillary dissection with and without regional nodal irradiation, and between sentinel biopsy with and without it, there was no significant difference (hazard ratios 1.20 and 1.33). The authors' conclusion: irradiating the regional nodes does add some risk, but the main determinant is which axillary operation was done[1].

What you will more easily find online is a different set of figures: a systematic review of 72 studies gives a pooled estimate of 16.6% (95% confidence interval 13.6–20.2 — the confidence interval is the band the true figure can reasonably sit in), and 21.4% (14.9–29.8) when only the 30 prospective cohorts are taken[2]. That total mixes sentinel biopsy and axillary dissection together, and for most people today, who have had a sentinel biopsy only, it overestimates by two- to threefold. The same review is perfectly clear when it stratifies: axillary dissection 19.9% (18 studies, 13.5–28.2), sentinel biopsy 5.6% (18 studies). And the only risk factors graded as strong evidence fall into two classes — extent of surgery (axillary dissection, a higher number of nodes removed, mastectomy) and being overweight or obese[2].

On the randomised side, AMAROS enrolled 4,806 patients with a clinically node-negative axilla, of whom 1,425 with a positive sentinel node were randomised to axillary dissection (744) or axillary radiotherapy (681): 10-year axillary recurrence was 0.93% against 1.82%, with no difference in overall survival or disease-free survival, and the updated 5-year lymphoedema rate was 24.5% against 11.9% (P<0.001)[3]. In the original 5-year report the dissection arm had significantly more at years 1, 3 and 5[4].

One sentence to nail down: among people who had a sentinel biopsy only, 8.0% still developed lymphoedema by 5 years[1]. "The nodes were clear, so it won't swell" is not true. The other way round, these figures are not there to veto treatment either — how far the axillary surgery should go is the subject of "How much of the axilla has to go?", and whether the regional nodes should be irradiated is in "Should the axilla and supraclavicular area be treated too?". Their use here is one thing only: to let you see which cell you are standing in, and whether you should be in monitoring. As for timing, most of the rise is concentrated in the first two years after surgery (the 12 to under 24 month segment accounts for 18.9%, 14.2–24.7)[2].

Blood draws, blood-pressure cuffs, air travel — the data are not on the side of those warnings

I write this section with some hesitation, but leaving it out would be worse. The same team accumulated 3,041 measurements in a prospective screening cohort between 2005 and 2014, measuring both arms with an instrument each time and recording, for the interval since the last visit, the number of blood draws, injections, blood pressure measurements, injuries and episodes of cellulitis on the at-risk arm, along with the number of flights and their duration. The result: relative arm volume change was not significantly associated with "at least one blood draw" (P=0.62), "at least one injection" (P=0.77), number of flights (1 to 2, P=0.77; 3 or more, P=0.91), or the duration of a single flight (1 to 12 hours, P=0.43; over 12 hours, P=0.54)[5].

In the same analysis, what multivariable analysis did find associated were four things: body mass index of 25 or above (P=0.0236), axillary lymph node dissection (P<0.001), regional nodal irradiation (P=0.0364), and cellulitis (P<0.001)[5]. So the thing genuinely worth guarding against is skin infection, not the act of taking blood. Redness, swelling, heat, pain or fever in that arm is something to deal with the same day, not something to "watch for a while".

This does not mean your team is wrong to warn you. It is an example of a field's convention not yet having caught up with the evidence, and there is a reason conventions update slowly — the study above is a single-institution prospective observation, and what it measured was change in arm volume, not every harm that might occur. If your hospital still has a rule about this, please do not use this article to break it; take the data to clinic and ask "can blood actually be taken from this side?" — that is what this section is for.

Body weight is the one item on the list you hold yourself

The extent of surgery is already settled, and the decision about the radiation fields is not in your hands. Of the risk factors that have been established, one is left that you can move: being overweight and obesity appear both on the systematic review's strong-evidence list and in the prospective cohort's multivariable analysis[2][5]. I put it here because it is something that can be done, not as a lecture about what ought to be.

Exercise does not make the arm worse — but that was a supervised programme in a compression garment

Two randomised trials turned this one over. The first enrolled 141 breast cancer survivors who already had stable lymphoedema and randomised them to twice-weekly progressive weight lifting or to a control group, with a well-fitted compression garment compulsory during training: at 1 year the proportion with an increase of 5% or more in arm or hand swelling was the same in both groups (11% against 12%, cumulative incidence ratio 1.00, 95% CI 0.88–1.13), while the weight-lifting group had greater improvement in self-reported lymphoedema symptom severity (P=0.03) and fewer exacerbations as adjudicated by a certified lymphoedema therapist (14% against 29%, P=0.04), with no serious adverse events related to the intervention[6].

The second enrolled 154 survivors who did not yet have lymphoedema, 1 to 5 years after surgery, with at least 2 nodes removed, randomised to 1 year of weight lifting (including gym membership and 13 weeks of supervision) or to no exercise; 134 completed the year of follow-up. Lymphoedema occurred in 11% (8/72) of the weight-lifting group against 17% (13/75) of controls, 6.0 percentage points lower in the weight-lifting group, 95% confidence interval −17.2 to 5.2 percentage points — the interval crosses 0, so in this cell no difference can be seen. In the subgroup with 5 or more nodes removed it was 7% (3/45) against 22% (11/49), a difference of 15.0 percentage points lower (−18.6 to −11.4), and this interval does not cross 0[7]. Note that both trials ran supervised, progressively loaded programmes with a compression garment worn, not "go and lift some weights on your own".

On the treatment side, the evidence is a great deal weaker than on the incidence side

The PREVENT trial enrolled 1,200 newly diagnosed breast cancer patients, randomised 963 after eligibility was reconfirmed post-operatively, and analysed 879: one arm was measured with bioimpedance spectroscopy, the other with a tape measure, and either method detecting subclinical lymphoedema triggered a 4-week intervention of a compression sleeve and glove worn 12 hours a day. At a median follow-up of 32.9 months, the bioimpedance arm triggered the intervention less often (20.1% against 27.5%); among those triggered, the proportion who still progressed to needing complete decongestive therapy was 7.9% (7/89) in the bioimpedance arm against 19.2% (23/120) with the tape measure, relative risk 0.41 (95% CI 0.13–0.81), an absolute reduction of 11.3 percentage points[8]. That is reducing progression, not curing it — people in both arms still progressed. Measuring your own arm circumference with a tape can serve as a record, but it is not the same thing as the instrument the trial used, and the number it gives you cannot be treated as a diagnosis.

The surgical side needs saying even more carefully. The meta-analysis of immediate lymphatic reconstruction (the class of operation often called LYMPHA) took in 9 prospective studies and 791 patients, of whom 460 had the operation, with a mean follow-up of only 20.6 months: cumulative lymphoedema incidence 9% (95% CI 1–16%) against 29% (11–46%), relative risk 0.42 (0.26–0.68)[9]. This is a pooling of prospective cohorts, not of randomised trials, and the follow-up is around a year and eight months. You can say there are data supporting it; you cannot say it is established, and it is not done at every hospital nor suitable for everyone. On the rehabilitation side, the American Physical Therapy Association's 2020 guideline grades its recommendations by strength of evidence and stage of lymphoedema, and the authors themselves point out that the studies differ widely in methodology and populations[10]. One more thing belongs here because it concerns radiation fields: a Japanese study reports that in patients who have had an axillary dissection and taxane-containing chemotherapy, adding supraclavicular irradiation induces lymphoedema[11] — that is the item you will want when you discuss the fields with your radiation oncologist.

In Taiwan, I searched the NHI Administration site and the Executive Yuan gazette for rehabilitation treatment of lymphoedema and for compression garments and found no specific provision, and the same for bioimpedance screening. Confirm the reimbursement conditions for these with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office.

What to start recording about that arm from now on

  • Put yourself in one of the four cells: sentinel, sentinel plus irradiation, dissection, dissection plus irradiation[1]. The first two years are the stretch where the probability climbs[2], so make "the arm" a standing item in this year's appointments.

  • Redness, swelling, heat, pain or fever: contact them the same day. In that multivariable analysis, cellulitis was the one factor both confirmed and treatable there and then[5].

  • Two questions at your next appointment: is there regular lymphoedema monitoring here and who does it; and if I want to start weight training, should I be fitted for a compression garment first and who teaches me the starting load[6].

From the physician

Among people who had a sentinel lymph node biopsy only, 8.0% at five years is still not zero. Make "the arm" a fixed item at every appointment this year; and on the day the skin turns red, swollen, hot or painful, or a fever starts, contact the team rather than saving it for the next visit.
