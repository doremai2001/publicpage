What the first month after diagnosis looks like

The order and division of labour across the tumour board, staging tests and the catastrophic illness certificate — and how Taiwan's nationwide data answer whether the weeks of testing are safe.

Cervical Cancer Guide · After the Diagnosis · 7 references
#Getting Ready#Treatment Pathway#Tumour Board#NHI Coverage

In Taiwan's nationwide data, 96.4% of patients started treatment within 90 days of diagnosis, and the harm signal appears only past the three-month mark — finishing the tests and letting the tumour board meet is exactly what this window was designed to hold. This article turns the month into a timetable you can read.

My position first: definitive chemoradiation and brachytherapy are the treatments I deliver every day. So every sentence about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it — not more warmly.

After diagnosis day, the hardest part is often not any single test — it is the blank space in the calendar: the next appointment is ten days away, nothing in between, and you feel every extra day the cancer stays is a loss. This article lays out what the first month is actually running: what question each step answers, which certificate to sort out first, and how Taiwan's own nationwide data answer the question "is this waiting safe".

Your case will be looked at by a roomful of people

The European gynaecological oncology societies' 2023 guideline (ESGO/ESTRO/ESP) recommends that the treatment plan be decided at a multidisciplinary tumour board — gynaecologic oncology, radiation oncology, medical oncology, pathology and imaging all in the room[1]. Cervical cancer needs this more than most cancers, because the main treatment can sit entirely in a different specialty — early disease is mostly surgery by gynaecologic oncology, while the standard for locally advanced disease is chemoradiation led by radiation oncology — so "which specialty leads" is itself a question the tumour board is there to answer. Taiwan's corresponding system is the cancer care quality accreditation the Health Promotion Administration has run since 2008: accredited hospitals must hold regular cross-specialty team meetings to discuss each patient's results, with the cancer case manager as a fixed member of the team; in 2024 the government announced 67 accredited hospitals nationwide, treating more than 85% of Taiwan's cancer patients[2]. The case manager is your single window — save the phone number; every section below will use it.

The first month's main job: pin the stage down

The tests that can be listed at guideline level are these: pelvic examination and biopsy (colposcopy where needed); pelvic MRI as a mandatory part of the initial work-up (except in very early disease with negative cone margins); PET-CT added for locally advanced disease or suspicious imaging, otherwise chest-abdomen CT; cystoscopy and proctoscopy are no longer routine and depend on clinical findings[1][3]. What each answers and how to read the reports is the whole of "Staged by examination and imaging, not surgery". For blood panels and anaesthesia assessment, I could not find a unified list I could cite — that follows each hospital's own pathway; if you want to know where you are in the queue, asking your case manager for a timetable beats guessing.

Will the disease get worse during these weeks of tests?

Taiwan's own data can answer this. A nationwide study linking the cancer registry with the NHI database for 2004 to 2010 (9,693 newly diagnosed cervical cancer patients): 96.4% began treatment within 90 days of diagnosis; after adjustment, those starting at 90 to 180 days had a hazard ratio for death of 1.33, and beyond 180 days, 1.36 — read 1.33 as: in the late-starting group, death arrived about a third faster[4]. The harm signal appears after the delay passes three months. So using the first month to finish the tests and let the team settle the treatment plan is not squandering your window — it is exactly what the window was designed to hold. One honest sentence has to be added: data like these carry confounding — people who wait a long time are often people in a harder situation to begin with — so this is a continuous slope, not a cliff where day 89 is safe and day 91 is dangerous; I will not use it to invent a day-count threshold, and please do not use it to bargain with the hospital either[4].

For anyone who needs to discuss fertility or ovarian preservation, this test period is precisely that window. A 321-patient cohort study comparing diagnosis-to-treatment intervals of under 30 days, 30 to 45 days and over 45 days found no difference in three-year survival, and the authors' conclusion was that taking time before treatment for fertility-preservation procedures is acceptable[5]. The conditions and options are written in "Young patients: fertility and the ovaries" — this conversation has to happen before treatment starts; once it starts, there is no way back.

"No need to rush" has a clearly drawn boundary

The "no need to rush" above applies only before treatment begins. Once radiotherapy starts, the logic flips entirely: interruptions that stretch the overall treatment time directly cost you the chance of control, and you cannot simply take days off in the middle. Why that is, what each of those five to six weeks actually looks like, and which situations mean contacting the team the same day, are all in "Getting through five or six weeks of pelvic radiotherapy" — if the team has already told you the plan is chemoradiation, read that one before you start.

The catastrophic illness certificate: 3 years for stage I, 5 for other stages — and carcinoma in situ is not on the list

Under the NHI catastrophic illness schedule applicable from 1 January 2025, malignant tumours of the cervix fall under "cancers requiring active or long-term treatment": the certificate is valid 3 years for stage I, while other stages come under other malignancies with 5 years, renewable if the conditions are still met[6]. What it does is waive copayments for care related to that illness — it does not make everything free; self-paid items and room differences are not inside it[6]. The application usually goes through the hospital, with the physician issuing the diagnosis certificate; ask your case manager to walk it through, and confirm the details with the NHI Administration or the hospital.

Carcinoma in situ is not on the list[6]. If your diagnosis is carcinoma in situ and the certificate cannot be obtained, nobody dropped the paperwork — administratively it is not counted as cancer, and why the line is drawn there is written in "Before it is cancer: CIN and carcinoma in situ".

One thing the women in your family can do along the way

Your diagnosis has already happened, but your family's risk can be managed: from 2025 the publicly funded cervical smear age was lowered to 25, and publicly funded HPV testing was added at specific ages[7] — the policy details, together with your own post-treatment follow-up and vaccine questions, are written in "Follow-up, vaccines and smears from here on".

Four things to get asked at your first meeting with the treatment team

  • "When are my tests expected to finish? Once they are done, when will the treatment plan be settled, and which specialty leads?"

  • "Will my case go to the tumour board? Who will tell me the conclusion, and when?"

  • "Who files my catastrophic illness certificate? What do I need to prepare?"

  • "If I want to discuss fertility or ovarian preservation, is there still time? Which clinic do I book, and by when must I decide?"

What these four have in common is that each has a named person and a date. Get the names and dates in hand, and this month stops being a blank — it becomes a timetable you can read. Once the waiting has content, the anxiety has somewhere to go.

From the physician

Actual day counts vary a great deal between hospitals; this article gives the order, not the days. Call your case manager today with two questions: which day my tests run to, and who files my catastrophic illness certificate. If you need to discuss fertility or ovarian preservation, ask for that referral in the same phone call.
