HOW TREATMENT IS DECIDED

Squamous and adenocarcinoma are two different diseases

Over 90% of oesophageal cancer in Taiwan is squamous cell carcinoma, and adenocarcinoma takes a different treatment road. This article separates the two diseases' origins, risk factors and default treatments.

Oesophageal Cancer Guide · How Treatment Is Decided · 24 references
#Staging#Decisions#Quitting#Evidence

This is written for two readers at once: the squamous cell carcinoma patient, who is the great majority and will find answers in the dozen articles that follow, and the adenocarcinoma patient, who is few in number but will take a wrong turn by following the squamous map. Work out which one you are before reading on.

When the pathology report reaches my hands, the first word I check is not the stage. It is whether the line says "squamous cell carcinoma" or "adenocarcinoma". In the oesophagus these two names are two different diseases: they grow in different places, arrive by different routes, respond differently to chemoradiotherapy, and even the default treatment path is different. The great majority of Taiwanese patients have the former, so the main line of this topic is squamous cell carcinoma (SCC); but an adenocarcinoma patient who follows the SCC map will take a wrong turn, so I separate the two roads first, then carry on.

Over 90% of oesophageal cancer in Taiwan is squamous, and almost all of it is in men

Numbers first. The Ministry of Health and Welfare's cause-of-death statistics for 2024 (ROC year 113): oesophageal cancer took 2,076 people in a year, 1,926 of them men; the median age at death was 63, against 71 for all cancers, making oesophageal cancer one of the earliest-killing of the ten major cancers[1]. The most recent official incidence figure I can find stops at 2018 (ROC year 107), 2,778 cases, over 90% male, peaking between 50 and 70[2]; the 2022 (ROC year 111) cancer registry puts it more bluntly: the age-standardised incidence of oesophageal cancer in men is 12.5 times that in women[3], and in 2023 (ROC year 112) it was still the sixth most common cancer in men[4]. I cannot get hold of an official breakdown by histology; what I can cite is a study linking the Taiwan Cancer Registry to the NHI database (2008 to 2014, ages 40 to 79, 7,763 people): SCC 96.6%, adenocarcinoma 3.4% — the denominator here counts SCC plus adenocarcinoma only — 94.4% male, and 77.1% already stage III or IV at diagnosis[5]. That is what oesophageal cancer in Taiwan looks like: a middle-aged man, squamous, locally advanced.

Two things have to be said together or the picture gets read wrong. One: "over 90% squamous" does not mean Taiwan has no adenocarcinoma. In a trend analysis of registry data from 1985 to 2019, the age-standardised incidence of adenocarcinoma in men rose on average 1.2% a year; SCC in men rose faster, 4.2% a year[6]. Two: this is the opposite of the West. Of the 572,000 oesophageal cancers worldwide in 2018, 482,000 were SCC and 85,000 adenocarcinoma; East Asia has the highest SCC incidence in the world, northern Europe the highest adenocarcinoma, and in more and more high-income countries adenocarcinoma has already overtaken SCC[7]. So the English-language patient information you read online is often about the other disease.

Tobacco, alcohol, betel quid: a question of dose, not of morals

I ask about tobacco, alcohol and betel quid in clinic because they determine how high the risk was, and they also determine whether a second cancer comes after treatment. Taiwan's own case-control studies have measured the quantities carefully. A multicentre study (513 SCC patients, 818 controls): all three substances showed a dose-response; alcohol was the strongest single factor, and for alcohol the amount mattered more than the number of years, whereas for tobacco the years mattered more than the amount; any two used together gave odds ratios of 8.8 to 19.7, all three together 41.2[8]. An odds ratio needs translating: it is the ratio of "exposed to unexposed" among the patients, divided by the same ratio among the controls; a big number means a strong association, not "the probability becomes 41 times higher". Another all-male study from Kaohsiung (165 SCC patients, 255 controls): adjusted odds ratio 5.4 for smoking, 17.6 for drinking; betel quid chewing on its own was only borderline significant (1.7, 95% confidence interval 0.8 to 3.1), but quid with inflorescence of Piper betle was 4.2 and habitually swallowing the juice was 3.3, which the authors put down to the juice contacting the mucosa directly[9]. So I will write betel quid as weaker than tobacco and alcohol; the figure of 41 is for all three combined, and betel quid alone is nowhere near that. The Health Promotion Administration's public page uses the wording "betel quid chewers have a 5-fold increased risk of upper digestive tract cancers, and users of all three 10.5-fold" — that is the official plain-language version, and the numbers underneath are likewise odds ratios[3].

The attributable fraction for the three combined is 83.7%[8] — in plain words, more than 80% of the disease among Taiwanese SCC patients can be laid at the door of these three things. Read that number forwards: only after you quit does the risk of a second cancer start to come down, which is why quitting is part of the treatment. How to quit, and why it bears on how well treatment works, is in "Things to settle before treatment starts".

Why I make a point of asking whether alcohol makes you flush

I ask this every week. Going red in the face and getting a racing heart after a small amount of alcohol usually means reduced activity of the enzyme ALDH2 — acetaldehyde, the intermediate product of alcohol metabolism and itself a carcinogen, does not get cleared and builds up in the body. A 2009 review in PLoS Medicine estimated that about 36% of East Asians have this constitution: inherit one broken copy of the gene from a parent and the enzyme barely works, so acetaldehyde stays in the body far longer than in other people[10]. A Taiwanese multicentre study (406 SCC patients, 656 controls) looked at this alongside how much people drank: at the same intake, people with reduced ALDH2 activity carried higher risk; reduced activity plus low-to-moderate drinking (0.1 to 30 grams of alcohol a day) gave a combined adjusted odds ratio of 14.5; plus heavy drinking (more than 30 grams a day), 102.6[11]. Flushing does not mean you will get cancer; it means that for the same glass, your oesophagus pays a much higher price than someone else's. And among low-to-moderate drinkers, this effect was mainly seen in smokers[11]. So when I ask about flushing, I am estimating how much alcohol weighs for you specifically.

Hot drinks, reflux, Barrett's oesophagus: adenocarcinoma comes from somewhere else

The official list of oesophageal carcinogens also includes: drinks above 65 °C, pickled and smoked foods containing nitrosamines, past corrosive injury to the oesophagus, swallowing disorders, gastro-oesophageal reflux, obesity and poor oral hygiene[2]. The grade for hot drinks needs stating precisely: in 2016 the International Agency for Research on Cancer classified very hot beverages above 65 °C as Group 2A[12] — the "probably carcinogenic" grade; the evidence has not reached "established"[2].

Adenocarcinoma arrives by another route: long-standing gastro-oesophageal reflux turns the lining of the lower oesophagus into intestine-like cells (Barrett's oesophagus), and adenocarcinoma grows out of that. A Swedish nationwide case-control study (189 oesophageal adenocarcinomas, 820 controls): people with recurrent reflux symptoms had an odds ratio of 7.7 for oesophageal adenocarcinoma, those with long-standing and severe reflux 43.5; in the same study, reflux had no association with SCC, odds ratio 1.1[13]. That 7.7 is relative to people without symptoms, calculated in a population where adenocarcinoma is rare, and cannot be read as "reflux leads to adenocarcinoma" — the vast majority of people with reflux never get it. The annual rate at which Barrett's oesophagus progresses to cancer is a figure I have not verified to a citable source, so I am not writing one here.

Adenocarcinoma takes another road: FLOT is the default, not an option

The treatment split between the two diseases hardened in 2025. The German ESOPEC trial randomised 438 patients with resectable oesophageal adenocarcinoma between perioperative chemotherapy (FLOT) plus surgery and preoperative chemoradiotherapy (the CROSS regimen) plus surgery. At a median follow-up of 55 months, three-year overall survival was 57.4% against 50.7%, hazard ratio 0.70 (95% confidence interval 0.53 to 0.92) — a hazard ratio divides the rate at which deaths occur in one group by the rate in the other, and 0.70 means the FLOT group's rate was 30% slower; 90-day postoperative mortality was 3.1% against 5.6%, and grade 3 or higher adverse events 58.0% against 50.0%[14]. ESMO's 2025 interim update changed its recommendation accordingly; the original text reads: "Patients with resectable, locally advanced AC of the oesophagus or OGJ should be treated with perioperative FLOT followed by surgery [I, A]" — level I evidence, grade A recommendation, the strongest box a guideline has; chemoradiotherapy was moved down to "Neoadjuvant CRT may be considered if the patient is not suitable for FLOT [I, C]"[15]. In plain terms: for resectable locally advanced adenocarcinoma of the oesophagus and gastro-oesophageal junction, the default is FLOT plus surgery, and chemoradiotherapy is the road you consider only when FLOT is not suitable.

Two honest footnotes. Europe's Neo-AEGIS (362 adenocarcinomas) compared perioperative chemotherapy with CROSS: three-year survival 55% against 57%, a draw; but 85% of the chemotherapy arm received the older ECF/EOX, not FLOT[16]. The SANO trial's authors also point out that the pathological complete response rate in ESOPEC's chemoradiotherapy arm was only 10% and its completion rate 75%, where the original CROSS trial had 23% and 91%[17] — in the match FLOT won, the opponent was not in good shape. Neither point overturns ESMO's [I, A], but they tell you it is not a landslide.

Why the main line of this topic is squamous

Because chemoradiotherapy works very differently on the two diseases. The Dutch CROSS trial enrolled 75% adenocarcinoma and 23% SCC[18], and the long-term follow-up looked at the two groups separately: for SCC patients, chemoradiotherapy before surgery gave a median overall survival of 81.6 months against 21.1 months for surgery alone, hazard ratio 0.48; for adenocarcinoma it was 43.2 against 27.1 months, hazard ratio 0.73[19]. The proportion in whom no cancer cells could be found in the resected specimen after chemoradiotherapy (pathological complete response, pCR) was 49% for SCC and 23% for adenocarcinoma[20]. Same treatment, twice the response in SCC — which is exactly why SCC patients are the ones who get asked "after chemoradiotherapy, do you still need surgery", and why the dozen or so articles that follow are written around SCC. If you have adenocarcinoma and are reading this topic, remember two things: staging tests, feeding tubes, oesophagitis and eating after surgery are shared by both diseases; the choice of treatment route is not — yours is the FLOT road, and wherever another article mentions adenocarcinoma it will point back here in a sentence. Japan's proportions are close to Taiwan's, about 86% SCC and about 7% adenocarcinoma[21], which is why the Japan Esophageal Society guideline will appear often in this topic.

The line to check first when the report is in your hands

Before your next appointment, turn to the diagnosis line of the pathology report and find "squamous cell carcinoma" or "adenocarcinoma". If it is adenocarcinoma, take the fifth section of this article with you and ask: "I have adenocarcinoma. Why am I being scheduled for chemoradiotherapy and not FLOT?" The answer may be entirely legitimate — kidney function, fitness, tumour location — but you are entitled to hear it. If it is SCC, the next questions are the staging tests and whether surgery is possible, and those are in "The tests that decide whether surgery is possible". The tobacco, alcohol and betel quid part you can act on today: the Health Promotion Administration's quitline, 0800-636363, Monday to Saturday, 9 a.m. to 9 p.m.[22]; the Ministry of Health and Welfare's Department of Oral Health keeps a directory of betel-quid cessation clinics by county and city[23]; for alcohol dependence treatment, the Ministry has a 2026 (ROC year 115) subsidy for out-of-pocket treatment costs, capped at NT$40,000 per person per year and limited to designated institutions — this money comes from the public budget and has nothing to do with the NHI[24]. Keep these three phone numbers next to the pathology report. They are part of the treatment.

From the physician

The histology line on your pathology report decides how you read this topic. Squamous patients, go on to the staging-tests article; adenocarcinoma patients, take ESMO 2025's [I, A] sentence to clinic and ask whether FLOT or chemoradiotherapy is being arranged, and why. The three phone numbers for tobacco, alcohol and betel quid can be dialled today.
