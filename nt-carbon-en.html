Carbon ions: heavier is not better

The only carbon-ion randomised trial ever to read out was a safety comparison against protons, and it found no difference. This article unpacks what RBE really means, and which Taiwan facts can — and cannot — be looked up.

Next-Generation Therapy Guide · The four technologies · 13 references
#Carbon-Ion Therapy#Evidence#Costs & Self-Pay

Japan treated tens of thousands of people over twenty years, yet the largest group in its national registry is prostate cancer — a cancer photons also treat very well. This article reads from that gap: what "strong" means in physics, what record the randomised trials have left, what Taiwan has published, and which number is missing.

Warning 1 (what this displaces): Most of the treatments in this topic are paid out of pocket, and most sit outside standard treatment. Before you decide, you need to know that what they displace is usually not time but three things of real value: standard treatment you have not yet used up, money you will need for later lines, and your eligibility for clinical trials (many trials exclude people who have recently received other experimental treatments). And there is one more, harder to see — physical reserve. The strength burnt through in a course that does not work may no longer be there when a genuinely useful option appears. So the question is not whether this is worth trying — it is what you give up by doing it.

Even the veterans' medical-subsidy scheme states in writing that heavy-particle therapy is excluded from subsidy and must be paid in full out of pocket[1] — and nowhere in Taiwan is there an officially published total price, so the only reliable number you can get is the one the medical affairs office writes down for you in black and white; this money and the out-of-pocket cost of later-line drugs or trial co-payments come out of the same pocket.

Warning 2 (finding the right doctor): Do not make this kind of decision alone, and do not make it on the word of the person offering the treatment. The doctor you are looking for is one willing to tell you "this is not right for you" — if they never once say who this is NOT for, that is not a consultation, it is a sale.

My position first: proton therapy and hyperthermia are both technologies within my own field, and the hospital I work at has the equipment for them. So I draw every line in this topic tighter, not looser.

The marketing for carbon-ion therapy almost all circles one word: strong. This article takes that word apart — what it is in physics, what it has proven in the clinic, and what it means on the bill. It is the most badly misread word in this field.

"Heavy" changes what happens on arrival, not the power

Carbon ions have a Bragg peak just as protons do; the difference lies in what happens after arrival: along its track a carbon ion deposits energy at high density, the DNA damage it causes comes in clusters that cells find hard to repair, and so the same physical dose produces a larger biological effect. The number that converts this difference is the RBE (relative biological effectiveness): it is a factor for converting a carbon-ion dose into an "equivalent photon dose", it sits roughly between 2 and 3 inside tumours, and it shifts with dose, fractionation and tissue[2]. You may have heard claims of the kind "carbon ions are twelve times stronger than protons". Even if that multiple has a source, it is a comparison of physical quantities; the biologically equivalent dose used in clinical prescriptions has already folded the RBE in — a prescription of 60 GyE of carbon ions is designed to be equivalent to 60 Gy of photons, not "60 times twelve". Reading a multiple of a physical quantity as a multiple of treatment effect is mistaking a unit for efficacy; and "strong" is not the right verb either — the RBE is a dose-conversion factor, not a strength multiplier[2]. What high energy density may genuinely buy, biologically, is that its killing of hypoxic, radioresistant tumour cells depends less on oxygen[2][3] — and that "may" is exactly where the evidence currently stops; the next section is the evidence itself. There is also a physical cost that usually gets left out: beyond the carbon Bragg peak sits a nuclear-fragmentation tail, so the dose past the peak does not fall as close to zero as it does with protons[2][3].

The randomised-trial record takes two sentences to write out

As of August 2026, exactly one carbon-ion randomised trial anywhere in the world has read out: Germany's ISAC, in sacrococcygeal chordoma, with 82 people randomised to carbon ions or protons — note that the comparator is protons, not photons — a primary endpoint of safety and feasibility, and no difference between the arms; local progression-free survival for the whole cohort was 84% at two years and 70% at four, and the authors' own conclusion was that the early response was good but local control did not hold[4]. CIPHER — the phase 3 trial of carbon ions against photons in pancreatic cancer on which so much hope was pinned — was withdrawn before it enrolled a single patient[5]. So the current state fits in one sentence: one phase 2 safety trial against protons, which found no difference; and not one randomised efficacy trial against any comparator has read out.

Tens of thousands treated — whose data is that?

Carbon-ion therapy is not short of patient numbers; it is short of comparison groups. Japan's earliest national centre began treating in 1994 and passed eight thousand patients over twenty years; the review that draws this experience together itself names the factors holding the technology back: cost, and the lack of randomised trials[6]. The representative series look like this: 188 people with unresectable sacral chordoma, five-year local control (the proportion whose tumour has not progressed again at the irradiated site within five years) of 77.2%, overall survival 81.1%, and 97% keeping the ability to walk[7] — disease where surgery would cost enormous function is carbon's most persuasive setting, but the comparator is historical surgical series, not random assignment. A multicentre series of 260 people with mucosal melanoma of the head and neck had two-year overall survival of 69.4%[8] — likewise retrospective, with no comparison group, in selected patients. The data for adenoid cystic carcinoma and skull-base chordoma — the rest of that "radioresistant shortlist" — all have the same shape[6]. Now put down an honest anchor: Japan's national registry took in 13,224 people from 2019 to 2022, and 62.4% of them had prostate cancer[9] — a cancer photons also treat very well. Set against the narrative that carbon is reserved for the untreatable cancers, that is the size of the gap between the story and the actual case mix. There are observational data on second cancers too: a retrospective study of 1,455 prostate-cancer patients treated with carbon ions, compared against photon and surgery groups, found a hazard ratio of 0.81 for subsequent primary cancers — the hazard ratio here means that during follow-up the carbon group grew new cancers at about 80% of the speed of the photon group; it is a ratio of speeds, not "19% fewer people" — with a 95% confidence interval of 0.66 to 0.99, its upper edge right against 1, and selection bias between the two data sources that could not be fully corrected; the authors themselves say it needs prospective validation[10].

Japan covers it; that does not mean "Japan has proven it better"

Japan has been bringing carbon ions into its public health insurance indication by indication since 2016, and by now more than ten are covered[9]; which cancers are covered, and since when, with the full current list and its caveats, is in "Approved, covered, effective: three different things". Coverage is an institutional decision, not proof of efficacy — that article spends its whole length taking this apart, and I will not repeat it here.

Carbon ions in Taiwan: the indications are published, the price is not

The hospital that runs Taiwan's heavy-particle centre publishes an indications table on its official page, and the note on its first line is the thing to copy down first: it applies to patients whose tumour "cannot be operated on, or who decline surgery", and the tumour must be visible on imaging; the table lists photon-resistant head and neck tumours (adenoid cystic carcinoma, melanoma and so on), early oesophageal and lung cancer, liver cancer, pancreatic cancer, recurrent colorectal cancer, prostate cancer and others, each with a set number of sessions[11]. The price is another matter: I could not find any officially published amount — the centre's frequently-asked-questions page has no fee entry[12], and a total-price figure has circulated in the media, but it matches no official page, so I am not citing it. The NHI currently has no payment item for heavy particles: the fee-schedule amendment that took effect in 2026 added the three proton items and did not include carbon[13]. So before deciding there is only one move: have the medical affairs office confirm the whole-course cost in writing. The warning at the top of this page turns concrete here: the official veterans' medical-subsidy explanation states in writing that heavy-particle therapy sits outside the subsidy and must be paid for in full[1] — and once this money is spent, the later lines' out-of-pocket targeted drugs, immunotherapy or trial co-payments are what the same pocket now holds that much less of.

What a reasonable case for carbon ions looks like: a very short list

A reasonable discussion looks like this: the tumour is on the radioresistant shortlist (adenoid cystic carcinoma, chordoma, mucosal melanoma and their kind); surgery is impossible or would come at an enormous cost in function; and the option was put on the table by a multidisciplinary team, not by an advertisement. In that setting, the single-arm numbers — though they have no randomised control — are data that can be spread out and talked through[7]. The unreasonable situation: a common cancer, complete evidence already in hand for photons or surgery, no randomised comparison showing carbon ions better in your disease — and you are about to spend the money the later lines will need here first. For the great majority of patients with common cancers, what decides the outcome is the stage, the subtype, and whether the course of treatment gets properly completed — not which particle is used. One sentence you can ask out loud in clinic: "In my cancer, does carbon-ion therapy have a randomised comparison that has read out?" — having read this article, you already know what the honest answer looks like as of August 2026.

Facts checked: August 2026.

From the physician

The reasonable setting for carbon ions is a very short list: a radioresistant tumour, surgery not possible, and a multidisciplinary team behind the discussion. If someone recommends it to you, ask one question first: in my cancer, is there a randomised comparison that has read out? Then ask the medical affairs office for the whole-course cost in writing.
