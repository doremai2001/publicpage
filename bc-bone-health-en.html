Bone density, bone-targeted drugs and exercise

Aromatase inhibitors do increase fractures, the benefit of adjuvant bisphosphonates holds only in postmenopausal women, and a complete dental assessment goes before any bone-modifying agent.

Breast Cancer Guide · After Treatment · 14 references
#Bone Health#Exercise#Late Effects#NHI Coverage

In the EBCTCG meta-analysis of 18,766 women, the recurrence rate ratio in the whole population was 0.94, which did not even reach statistical significance; split apart, the benefit holds only in the 11,767 postmenopausal women. The word most often dropped from this item is "postmenopausal".

Whether the anti-hormone tablets will wreck your bones, and whether the bone injection might also hold recurrence off, get asked in one breath in clinic — but they are in fact three separate questions: bone loss caused by aromatase inhibitors, using a bone-modifying agent in early breast cancer in the hope of also lowering recurrence, and treatment once bone metastases are already there. The populations, the doses and the endpoints are different in all three, and the numbers cannot be moved between them.

Aromatase inhibitors do increase fractures, and that is still not a reason to stop the drug

The EBCTCG pooled individual patient data on 31,920 postmenopausal women with ER-positive early breast cancer (from randomised trials comparing an aromatase inhibitor with tamoxifen): 5-year fracture risk 8.2% against 5.5%, hazard ratio 1.42 (95% confidence interval 1.28–1.57). A hazard ratio is the ratio between the rates at which events occur in the two groups, and 1.42 means they occurred about 40% faster; put in absolute terms it is 2.7 more people per 100[1].

The other half belongs in the same paragraph: in the same dataset, 10-year breast cancer mortality with 5 years of an aromatase inhibitor against 5 years of tamoxifen was 12.1% against 14.2% (hazard ratio 0.85, 0.75–0.96, 2p=0.009)[1]. The way to handle the fracture risk is monitoring, adding a drug and exercise — not stopping the tablets yourself. How many years to take them is the subject of "Five years of endocrine therapy, or ten?"; the joint pain and hot flushes that make people unable to keep going are in "The side effects that actually make people stop".

"The bone injection prevents recurrence" is missing one word: postmenopausal

A second EBCTCG meta-analysis took in individual patient data on 18,766 women with early breast cancer (97% from trials using 2 to 5 years of a bisphosphonate). In the whole population (all subtypes, pre- and postmenopausal combined) the recurrence rate ratio was 0.94 (95% CI 0.87–1.01, 2p=0.08), distant recurrence 0.92 (0.85–0.99), breast cancer mortality 0.91 (0.83–0.99) — all only marginal, and the first not even reaching statistical significance; bone recurrence was clearer, at 0.83 (0.73–0.94)[2].

Split it apart and it looks entirely different. For premenopausal women, the authors' own conclusion is that there was no apparent effect on any outcome. In the 11,767 postmenopausal women: recurrence 0.86 (0.78–0.94), distant recurrence 0.82 (0.74–0.92), bone recurrence 0.72 (0.60–0.86), breast cancer mortality 0.82[2]. The authors also put the brakes on: even for bone recurrence, the difference in effect by menopausal status was only borderline significant (trend 2p=0.06; by age 2p=0.03), and it was not significant when stratified by type of drug, schedule, ER status or nodal status[2].

The single trials point the same way. AZURE randomised 3,360 patients with early breast cancer to standard adjuvant treatment with or without zoledronic acid, median follow-up 59 months: disease-free survival was 77% in both arms (adjusted hazard ratio 0.98, 0.85–1.13, P=0.79); there were 17 confirmed cases of osteonecrosis of the jaw in the treated arm (cumulative incidence 1.1%) and 0 in the control arm. The authors concluded that the results do not support routine use in the adjuvant setting[3].

The 2022 ASCO and Ontario Health update recommends that adjuvant bisphosphonates should be discussed with all postmenopausal patients (whether naturally or through treatment) with primary breast cancer who are going to have adjuvant systemic therapy, regardless of hormone receptor and HER2 status; but the same passage states that the benefit varies with baseline recurrence risk, that the improvement in overall survival is modest, that the decision has to take in recurrence risk, side effects, cost and comorbidity, and that it does not replace standard anticancer treatment[4].

"Postmenopausal" in a breast cancer patient is not a question of age — menopause caused by chemotherapy or by ovarian function suppression counts too, and what the guideline writes is "natural or treatment-induced"[4]. Somebody in her thirties can land in this cell, and it needs checking with your doctor.

The two big denosumab trials reached different conclusions; do not smooth that over

ABCSG-18 enrolled 3,425 postmenopausal patients with hormone-receptor-positive early breast cancer who were taking an aromatase inhibitor, randomised to denosumab at the osteoporosis dose (60 mg every 6 months) or placebo: hazard ratio for time to first clinical fracture 0.50 (0.39–0.65, P<0.0001), with 92 fractures against 176; and under case-by-case active adjudication by an international expert panel there was not a single case of osteonecrosis of the jaw[5]. At a median follow-up of 8 years, 9-year disease-free survival was 79.4% against 75.9% (hazard ratio 0.83, 0.71–0.97); overall survival differed by 1.0 percentage point (hazard ratio 0.80, 0.64–1.01 — the interval crosses 1, so no difference can be seen)[6].

D-CARE enrolled 4,509 women with stage II or III breast cancer across 39 countries and used the oncology dose (120 mg every 3 to 4 weeks for 5 years): bone metastasis-free survival was no different between the arms (hazard ratio 0.97, 0.82–1.14, P=0.70); adjudicated osteonecrosis of the jaw was 122/2,241 (5%) against 4/2,218 (under 1%), and hypocalcaemia 7% against 4%[7].

The two trials look opposed because they were not asking the same question: the doses differ by more than tenfold, and so do the populations and the primary endpoints. The 2022 ASCO and Ontario Health position is that adjuvant denosumab is not recommended for preventing breast cancer recurrence, because the studies did not show a consistent reduction in recurrence in any subgroup of early breast cancer[4].

The odds of jaw osteonecrosis vary hugely with dose, and the dentist comes before the drug

Side by side it is clear enough: zero cases at the osteoporosis dose under active adjudication[5], 1.1% with adjuvant oncology-dose zoledronic acid[3], and 5% with denosumab 120 mg in D-CARE[7]. "It rots your jaw", with no dose and no setting attached, amounts to a wrong number.

The 2019 MASCC, ISOO and ASCO guideline defines medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw as three things holding at once: exposure to a bone-modifying agent or an antiangiogenic agent; exposed bone in the maxilla or mandible, or bone that can be probed through a fistula, persisting for more than 8 weeks; and no history of radiotherapy to the jaws and no metastasis in the jaws[8]. The phrase to keep is "exposed bone lasting more than 8 weeks".

The sequence has no alternative arrangement: a complete dental assessment has to be finished before a bone-modifying agent is started, and non-essential dentoalveolar surgery avoided while on it. The guideline also honestly admits that whether a bone-modifying agent should be stopped before dentoalveolar surgery remains uncertain[8] — where a guideline says it has no answer, I am not going to supply one for it. What to do is this: see the dentist before the drug and get whatever needs doing done; while on the drug, tell both oncology and dentistry before any extraction. Hypocalcaemia is a known risk[7], and calcium and vitamin D are dealt with at the same time as starting.

How often the bone density scan gets repeated, and what exercise can really do

ASCO's 2019 guideline on managing osteoporosis in survivors of adult cancers with non-metastatic disease recommends assessing fracture risk with an established tool and obtaining bone mineral density testing in those at appreciable risk; where a drug is needed, a bisphosphonate or denosumab at osteoporosis-indication doses is preferred[9]. But this guideline does not give a repeat interval in years that applies to everyone, and for the "every one to two years" you often hear, I could not find a provision I could cite; the interval has to be decided individually from your baseline bone density and your risk factors. In Europe, the 2020 ESMO guideline on bone health in cancer is the counterpart to consult[10].

Once the follow-up interval turns out to have no single answer, the next question is always "then does it help if I move about more myself?" That one has randomised data, and the answer is a good deal smaller than the version in circulation.

The exercise data have to be reported as they are. A randomised trial of 71 survivors within 5 years of treatment-induced premature menopause, mean age 46.5, compared 3 sessions a week of impact plus resistance training for 1 year against stretching: in the intention-to-treat analysis of the full sample there was no significant difference in bone outcomes; only in the analysis restricted to those at least 1 year past menopause did the training group gain femoral neck bone density (+0.004 against −0.010 g/cm², P<0.01)[11]. That is a signal, not "exercise grows bone". The 2018 international multidisciplinary roundtable consensus statement draws the line clearly: exercise is broadly safe for cancer survivors and inactivity should be avoided by everyone, and the outcomes the evidence is strong enough to support are anxiety, depressive symptoms, fatigue, physical function and quality of life[12]. Recurrence and survival are not on that list.

Once bone metastases are there, it is an entirely different set of numbers

In patients with advanced breast cancer who already have bone metastases, a randomised double-blind trial compared denosumab 120 mg (1,026 patients) with zoledronic acid 4 mg (1,020 patients): hazard ratio for delaying the first skeletal-related event (pathological fracture, radiotherapy or surgery to bone, spinal cord compression) 0.82 (0.71–0.95, P=0.01); osteonecrosis of the jaw 2.0% against 1.4% (P=0.39)[13]. What the course of the disease and the goals of treatment look like after bone metastases is not this article; it is in "Metastatic disease: what happens next".

The Taiwan end of this needs reading closely. In the NHI drug reimbursement regulations, zoledronic acid 4 mg is restricted to malignancy-related hypercalcaemia, or to multiple myeloma, breast cancer and prostate cancer with osteolytic bone metastases; injectable ibandronic acid and oncology-dose denosumab are likewise restricted to osteolytic bone metastases. A patient with early breast cancer and no bone metastases, taking either of these classes as adjuvant treatment, is outside the scope of reimbursement. The osteoporosis-dose provision is restricted to postmenopausal women with a DXA T-score of −2.5 or below who have already sustained a vertebral or hip fracture, or with osteopenia (−2.5 < T-score < −1.0) causing 2 or more vertebral or hip fractures or 2 or more such events; another provision allows use at a T-score of −2.5 or below with rheumatoid arthritis, insulin-treated diabetes, or long-term glucocorticoid use[14]. "Currently taking an aromatase inhibitor" is not on that list of high-risk factors — a patient whose bone density has fallen only because of the drug, and who has not yet fractured, does not meet the conditions for reimbursement as the provisions currently stand. That is not the same as saying it needs no attention; it means that in most cases it will be self-paid. For the reimbursement conditions of bone density testing I could not obtain an official provision I could cite, so confirm that one with your case manager or the hospital's medical affairs office; the logic for judging whether to pay for something yourself is in "This test is self-pay. Is it worth it?".

Dentist, bone density, exercise: the order cannot be swapped round

  • If any bone-modifying agent might be started soon, book the dentist first. That square goes before the drug, not after[8].

  • Three questions at your next appointment: am I counted as premenopausal or postmenopausal (including treatment-induced); has my fracture risk ever been assessed; do I need a baseline bone density scan and when would the next one be[9].

  • Put exercise into fixed weekly slots rather than doing it when it occurs to you. Aim it first at fatigue, physical function and quality of life — that is the part the evidence supports[12].

From the physician

"Postmenopausal" in a breast cancer patient includes menopause caused by chemotherapy or by ovarian function suppression, so you cannot place yourself by age. If any bone-modifying agent might be started soon, book the dentist this month — that square comes before the drug.
