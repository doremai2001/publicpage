BEFORE AND JUST AFTER DIAGNOSIS

A nodule you neither cut out nor forget

A 7 mm ground-glass nodule is not an emergency and not nothing; this is how the follow-up intervals are set, and who sets them.

Lung Cancer Guide · Before and Just After Diagnosis · 16 references
#Lung#Screening & Nodules#Imaging#Decisions#Getting Ready

Two opposite mistakes end up in my clinic: the person who has a 5 mm nodule cut out, and the person who reads about overdiagnosis and never comes back. This piece is written to block both.

"Doctor, the report says there is a 7 mm ground-glass nodule in my left upper lobe and recommends follow-up. Wouldn't it be safer to just have it taken out?" I hear that most weeks, usually from a woman in her fifties who has never smoked and feels perfectly well.

This piece has to block two opposite mistakes. One is walking into an operating theatre holding a 5 mm nodule. The other is finishing the article thinking small nodules can be ignored, filing the follow-up slip in a drawer and coming back three years later. I have met both, and the second is harder to rescue.

The word "positive" gets mistranslated on the way out of the room

NLST randomised 53,454 high-risk people to three annual rounds of low-dose CT (LDCT) or chest X-ray. Across the three rounds the positive rate was 24.2% with CT and 6.9% with X-ray, and of the positive CT results 96.4% were false positives[1].

Taiwan's own data sit closer to you. TALENT enrolled 12,011 people who had never smoked or had smoked very little; 2,094 of the baseline scans (17.4%) were read as positive, and the positive predictive value of a positive scan was only 14.0%[2].

In plain language: "positive" means there is something to look at again, not "probably cancer".

The evidence that screening lowers mortality grew in heavy smokers

The official sentence has a source: "Based on international research evidence, compared with chest X-ray, chest LDCT can reduce lung cancer mortality in heavy smokers by 20%"[3]. NLST's original figure is a relative reduction in lung cancer mortality of 20.0% (95% confidence interval 6.8–26.7), P=0.004[1]. In Europe, NELSON read nodules by volume instead: 13,195 men followed for ten years, cumulative rate ratio for lung cancer mortality 0.76 (0.61–0.94)[4].

Both trials enrolled smokers and ex-smokers (NLST: aged 55 to 74, at least 30 pack-years). The group most frightened in my clinic — middle-aged women who never smoked — was in neither. To this day, no randomised trial has shown that low-dose CT in a never-smoking population lowers mortality; TALENT is a single-arm prospective cohort whose primary endpoint was detection rate[2].

Overdiagnosis, which I am not going to skip

The overdiagnosis estimate from the same NLST participants: the probability that any screen-detected lung cancer is overdiagnosed is 18.5% (95% confidence interval 5.4–30.6), rising to 78.9% for bronchioloalveolar carcinoma. In the same set of figures where screening 320 people prevents one lung cancer death, there are 1.38 overdiagnosed cases[5].

That number cannot stand alone. By a median 11.3 years in the same participants, the total number of lung cancers was 1,701 against 1,681, a rate ratio of 1.01[6] — a good share of the "extra" cancers were simply found earlier, not cancers that would never have surfaced. The honest way to put it is a range: the overdiagnosed fraction sits somewhere between close to zero and close to 30%, depending on how long you follow people. Not one number to pick.

Taiwanese women have local evidence. Among 57,898 women with lung cancer in the cancer registry from 2004 to 2018, the incidence of early disease (stage 0 to 1) rose from 2.3 to 14.4 per 100,000 while advanced disease barely moved, 18.7 to 19.3 (absolute difference 0.6, −0.5 to 1.7), and mortality stayed flat. The authors' conclusion is blunt: virtually all the additional cancers detected represent overdiagnosis[7].

Another figure from the same paper is routinely misused in Taiwan: five-year survival in women with lung cancer rose from 18% to 40%[7]. That is not evidence of better treatment or effective screening — put people in the denominator who were never going to fall ill and the survival rate is bound to look good.

One sentence has to follow immediately, because this is the part most easily misread. Overdiagnosis says that some cancers need not be found. It does not say that your nodule needs no follow-up. What separates them is the timetable below, not a feeling.

How slow ground-glass is: slow enough to count in years, not slow enough to ignore

Eight Japanese institutions followed 795 patients and 1,229 subsolid nodules prospectively for a mean of 4.3 years. Of 1,046 pure ground-glass nodules, only 56 (5.4%) developed a solid component, taking on average 3.8 ± 2.0 years. Every nodule that proved to be invasive adenocarcinoma after resection already had a solid component, 1% of all the nodules followed[8].

A pure ground-glass nodule is very rarely invasive cancer in itself, so "look before you cut" has a basis. But once a solid component appears the thing has changed character, and that is the line at which to be aggressive — not a permit for endless observation.

People with a family history cannot relax to the general rhythm. In a health-check cohort of 10,151 East Asian women who had never smoked, 515 (5.1%) had a persistent ground-glass nodule; growth occurred in 1.3% of those with a family history against 0.4% without, and on multivariable analysis family history was independently associated with nodule growth within 10 years, hazard ratio 2.14 (1.03–4.44)[9]. A hazard ratio compares the relative rate at which events happen over the same period; it does not mean the probability becomes 2.14 times as large.

How often to look — and which setting the thresholds belong to

The numbers below come from a classification built for screening, not a general rule for a nodule found at a health check; the thresholds differ. Lung-RADS v2022 from the American College of Radiology (released November 2022) puts baseline solid nodules under 6 mm and pure ground-glass under 30 mm in category 2; solid 6 to 8 mm, or pure ground-glass 30 mm or more, in category 3, rescanned at 6 months; solid 8 to 15 mm in category 4A, at 3 months; 15 mm or more, or new or growing by 8 mm, in category 4B[10].

The NCCN 2026 patient guideline matches it on solid nodules: under 6 mm, yearly; 6 to 8 mm, at 6 months; 8 to 15 mm, at 3 months or arrange PET; 15 mm or more, contrast CT, PET or biopsy. But the two documents do not agree on the ground-glass row — NCCN says under 20 mm yearly and 20 mm or more at 6 months, a step earlier than Lung-RADS' 30 mm, and an existing ground-glass nodule that has grown by more than 1.5 mm moves to 6 months[11]. When two documents disagree, do not take the easier one. Ask the doctor who ordered the scan which one your nodule was scheduled from.

"Growth" has a definition: mean diameter increasing by more than 1.5 mm within 12 months. And mean diameter is the average of the long and short axis, not the longest measurement — Lung-RADS requires both to be measured and averaged[10]. Take the biggest number on the report to that table and it is easy to land in the wrong row.

If your report says "follow-up as recommended by the Fleischner Society", that is a different system, for nodules found incidentally outside screening; the 2017 version raised the minimum threshold for routine follow-up and changed the intervals into ranges[12]. I am not reproducing its table. The setting and version have to match your report; the doctor who ordered it is a more reliable source than I am.

The publicly funded screening criteria, copied out word for word

Taiwan opened the national lung cancer early detection programme (肺癌早期偵測計畫) on 1 July 2022 (民國 111 年 7 月 1 日), offering low-dose CT every two years to high-risk groups[13]. The criteria were widened on 1 January 2025 (114 年 1 月 1 日). The official news release reads, verbatim: "1. Family history of lung cancer: men aged 45 to 74 or women aged 40 to 74 whose blood-related (有血緣關係之) parent, child or sibling has been diagnosed with lung cancer. 2. Heavy smokers: people aged 50 to 74 with a smoking history of 20 pack-years or more who are still smoking, or have quit for less than 15 years (仍在吸菸或戒菸未達15年)."[14]

A second official source adds two conditions: "if you smoke, you should receive smoking cessation services", and "people with any one of the following lung cancer risk factors and holding National Health Insurance (健保) status may have one chest low-dose CT every 2 years; for those who are not high risk, it is advisable that a doctor assess whether screening is warranted"[15]. A pack-year is packs per day multiplied by years smoked: one pack a day for 30 years is 30 pack-years[13].

There is a reason for quoting word for word. Official pages issued at different dates do not word this identically: some drop 「仍在吸菸或」 ("still smoking, or"), some drop 「有血緣關係之」 ("blood-related"). The two above are the complete January and April 2025 versions. Verification date 30 August 2026. The criteria will change again; check once more before you go.

As of 30 June 2025 (114 年 6 月 30 日), 217,744 people had completed screening nationally and 2,506 were diagnosed, of whom 2,069 were early stage (0 or 1). In the national distribution for 110年 (2021), before screening began, early stage was only 35.3% and advanced 61.3%[16]. That is a shift in stage, not yet the answer on mortality.

Not qualifying is not the same as not needing attention. Eligibility for a funded programme is a threshold for allocating resources, not a threshold of risk. None of the following depends on it: a persistent cough that does not improve, blood-streaked sputum or coughing blood, breathlessness or wheeze, persistent chest pain, hoarseness, bone and joint pain, unexplained weight loss or loss of appetite[16]. The other direction needs saying too. I often quote the Health Promotion Administration (國民健康署) director-general's own words: "LDCT can only detect lung cancer early; it cannot lower the incidence of lung cancer" — prevention is still stopping smoking[15].

What a self-paid scan costs for someone who does not qualify, and whether follow-up after a nodule is covered, I found in no citable current provision. Ask the billing office (醫務課) or case manager (個管師) at your hospital, not the queue outside the consulting room.

The two opposite endings I do not want to see in clinic

The first is someone taking a 5 mm pure ground-glass nodule into an operating theatre. Such nodules take a mean of over three years to develop a solid component, and every invasive adenocarcinoma appeared in one that already had it[8]. Looking first is not stalling.

The second is someone who reads the overdiagnosis section and never comes back. TALENT's negative predictive value of 99.7% holds only for people who went back for the scan[2]; and Lung-RADS carries a note in black and white: a negative screen does not mean that an individual does not have lung cancer[10]. Nothing in this piece gives anyone permission to stop following up.

At your next visit, open the report and ask three things. Is mine pure ground-glass, part-solid or solid? What is the mean diameter, and how many millimetres has it grown since last time? When is the next scan, and who books it?

If treatment then has to be decided: stage and the aim of treatment are in "Ask whether surgery is possible before you ask about chemotherapy", early disease in someone who is not a surgical candidate in "Inoperable does not mean incurable", and why this happens to people who never smoked in "I never smoked. Why me?".

From the physician

Which follow-up schedule applies to you depends on whether the nodule was found by screening or incidentally, on which document your hospital works from, and on your family history — none of which you can settle from a report alone. Take the report to your next appointment and ask what type it is, what its mean diameter is, and who is booking the next scan.
