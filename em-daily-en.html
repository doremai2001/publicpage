AFTER TREATMENT

Life after treatment

Non-cancer causes account for 41.5% of the deaths in this group. Exercise, weight loss and dilators rest on evidence of very different strengths, and each is labelled separately rather than blurred together.

Endometrial Cancer Guide · After treatment · 18 references
#Survivorship#Exercise#Work & Travel#Quality of Life

What most often disappears when treatment ends is the person who used to get things into your diary. This article works through the four blocks that have data behind them — causes of death, exercise, weight, sexual function — plus two pieces of paper you can deal with now: the five-year validity of the catastrophic illness certificate, and the cancer resource centres in 104 hospitals.

On the day treatment ends, most people are handed a slip with the date of the next appointment, and that is all; the years in which you have to look after yourself are the years that come with no instructions. Where there is data to hold something up, I will give you the numbers; where there is not, I will tell you who to go and find.

This cancer accounts for less than half the deaths in this group

Among 135,831 women with endometrial cancer in the American SEER database, 46,604 died during follow-up (34.3%). The causes divide as endometrial cancer itself 42.9%, other cancers 15.6%, non-cancer causes 41.5%; within the non-cancer causes the commonest are heart disease, cerebrovascular disease and diabetes, and the longer since diagnosis, the smaller the proportion dying of endometrial cancer[1].

These figures are easy to push too far, so the opposite direction goes up beside them. In an Iowa cohort matched for age and body mass index, 552 women with endometrial cancer compared with 2,352 cancer-free controls had lower cardiovascular mortality, not higher (hazard ratio 0.75, 95% confidence interval 0.56 to 0.99), while all-cause mortality was higher (1.50)[2]. So I am not going to say that having had endometrial cancer makes you more likely to die of heart disease. The correct statement is that this disease and heart disease and diabetes share the same set of risk factors, and that non-cancer causes account for more than 40% of the deaths, so what actually needs attention once treatment ends is often metabolic and cardiovascular. That is exactly what the ESGO 2025 guideline says — follow-up should include assessment of physical health (for example cardiovascular comorbidities and second cancers) and mental health (V, A)[3].

And patients know this themselves. In a questionnaire study of 55 women, 87% agreed that heart disease was a risk for them and 84% said they were ready to act; but when it was measured, blood glucose and glycated haemoglobin were in the intermediate or poor range in 60%, diet in 60%, exercise in 47% and total cholesterol in 53%, and 16% had not seen a family doctor in the past year, these last reporting financial difficulty more often[4]. What is missing is usually not willingness; it is somebody to help get it into the diary.

Exercise: the direction is clear, but the evidence stops at observational

The most-cited study is the Alberta prospective cohort: 425 women with invasive endometrial cancer followed for a median 14.5 years, in whom the highest category of post-diagnosis recreational physical activity compared with the lowest had a disease-free survival hazard ratio of 0.33 (0.17 to 0.64) and an overall survival hazard ratio of 0.33 (0.15 to 0.75)[5]. It looks powerful, but look at the denominator: there were only 60 deaths and 80 disease-free survival events in the whole follow-up, and an effect size that large is exactly what a small sample looks like.

When the same cohort was extended to 527 women and 16.7 years, it got complicated: stratified by primary treatment, meeting the activity guidelines before diagnosis was associated with fewer events in the surgery-only group but with more events in the surgery-plus-adjuvant-treatment group, and the interaction was statistically significant (P=.035)[6]. I do not read that as "exercise is harmful" — the likeliest explanation is that women who need adjuvant treatment had higher-stage disease and were in a different physical state to begin with; but it does pull "exercise prolongs survival" back from "the evidence is clear" to "observational, and not pointing the same way in every group". In another cohort of 1,016 women, those reaching twice the activity guidelines had an all-cause mortality hazard ratio of 0.71 (0.50 to 1.00), sitting exactly on the boundary of significance[7].

What randomised intervention trials can produce is something else. In a 68-patient trial of a wearable device plus an action plan, the intervention group gained 45 minutes a week of moderate-to-vigorous activity while the control group lost 21 minutes, and those with diastolic hypertension had a net fall of 9.89 mmHg[8]; a British randomised trial of 54 women improved the diet quality score at eight weeks (difference 7.5), with no difference at that point in physical activity or quality of life, and a difference in quality of life appearing only at 24 weeks[9]. Not one of them had survival as an endpoint. So what I can give you is the guideline's sentence: lifestyle counselling on physical activity, a balanced diet, a healthy weight and stopping smoking should be routinely offered to patients (V, A)[3] — and how to start and how far to go is something to check with your doctor before you move.

Between losing weight and "it will not come back", causation does not hold up

A lot of people care about this one, and the answer is not the one I would like to give. A Spanish cohort of 526 women with early-stage endometrial cancer who were overweight or obese, followed for a median 76 months, found on multivariable analysis that weight change at 12 months after treatment was not a significant factor for overall survival, adjusted hazard ratio 1.01 (0.97 to 1.05)[10]. The more honest figure is on the intervention side: a randomised trial of 80 obese endometrial cancer survivors using personalised text messaging for behavioural weight loss found no difference in weight change between the groups at six months, and no difference either between trial participants and the 278 who did not take part; the proportion who actually gained weight was 47.7% against 44.4%, and only 9.2% against 11.2% lost at least 5%[11]. Even the first step, getting people to lose weight, has not been achieved. Weight and the incidence of this cancer is a separate matter of a different strength, and it is in "Weight, diabetes and this cancer".

Vaginal dryness and sex: the evidence for dilators is "not a single trial"

To answer whether regular use of a vaginal dilator during radiotherapy prevents stenosis, Cochrane searched four databases from the 1950s to 2013, and the concluding sentence is: we found no trials and therefore analysed no data[12]. They also point out that observational studies do find that women who dilate frequently report less stenosis, but that this could be because dilation is effective or because women whose vaginas were in good condition to start with are the ones able to keep it up[12]. An earlier version carried a safety warning as well: dilating during radiotherapy or immediately after it can, in rare cases, cause damage, and some women may benefit from starting once the inflammation has settled[12]. So I am not going to say you must use one, and I am not going to say the earlier the better — when to start is a question for your radiation oncologist.

There is one dataset from endometrial cancer itself: 131 women who had post-operative external beam radiotherapy followed by vaginal vault brachytherapy, median follow-up 60 months, in whom multivariable analysis identified "dilator use for less than 9 months" as an independent prognostic factor for late vaginal side effects (hazard ratio 3.07)[13] — a retrospective association, not a randomised trial. Adherence in practice is not high either: in a study of 54 women, overall adherence was 66.6%, and 18 (33.3%) never used one at all[14]. How to use one and how to raise it in clinic is in "Dilators and sex: the question nobody raises first". Menopausal symptoms, hormones and bone are in "Hormones after treatment", and I do not open them here.

The things still bothering you five years on are not yours alone

The international survey of 677 long-term gynaecological cancer survivors (more than five years from diagnosis, 32.9% of them endometrial cancer) gives a very usable set of figures: 36.9% still had physical or psychological symptoms, in order lymphoedema 36.2%, hot flushes 22.4%, difficulty concentrating 21.1%, fatigue 20.9%, vaginal dryness 20.1%, urinary incontinence 18.9%; self-rated general health was poor or very poor in 13.5%, and 13.6% were not receiving any follow-up care at all[15]. In the same survey, 33.7% did no exercise at all or less than an hour a week[15].

None of these is "overthinking it"; each is something that can be dealt with: swollen legs, "Lower-limb lymphoedema: prevention and treatment"; the more you fear recurrence the less you sleep, "Treatment ended. Why am I more afraid?"; the symptom list, "How follow-up is scheduled, and what to watch for".

Going back to work: this is a block where I have no Taiwanese numbers

For return-to-work rates and time to return to work in endometrial cancer patients, I could not find citable Taiwanese official statistics or research; that is a genuine data gap and I am not making it up. What I can describe is the shape of the obstacles, and they are in that survey above: lower-limb swelling makes standing and sitting for long periods hard, fatigue 20.9%, difficulty concentrating 21.1%[15]. Some hospitals in Britain have moved low-risk, FIGO 2009 stage I endometrioid patients onto patient-initiated follow-up and traded the saved appointments for exercise classes, and a service evaluation of 98 women found 91% would recommend it[16] — a service evaluation with no control group, so it can only be written as "somebody has done this", not as "Taiwan should do the same".

Two pieces of paper and one phone call you can deal with now treatment is over

The first piece of paper is the catastrophic illness certificate. Cancer of the uterine corpus, diagnostic code C54, falls under appendix 1 as "malignant tumours other than those in the preceding four subparagraphs", with a validity of five years[17]. What needs attention is the timing of re-application: Article 5 of the Regulations for Exempting Insured Persons of the National Health Insurance from Cost Sharing states, in translation, that where "the period of validity is two years or more: three months before the validity expires" the application is to be made afresh, and that "where a fresh application is made within the period specified in the preceding paragraph and the insurer approves continued possession of the catastrophic illness certificate, the periods of validity may run on"; an application made after the deadline takes the date the application is made as its effective date[17]. In plain words: remember to apply three months before the five years are up; if you re-apply within that window and it is approved, the validity can run on. Apply late and the clause says only that the effective date is the day you apply; how the interval in between is counted is not written any further down, and that is a question for the medical affairs office or your case manager. Other certificates you can apply for are in "Beyond the Catastrophic Illness Certificate: what else you can claim"; when commercial insurance pays and who decides is in "When will insurance pay, and who decides?".

The second piece of paper is a list of resources. The Ministry of Health and Welfare's own press release puts it clearly: the Health Promotion Administration has funded the Hope Foundation for Cancer Care to help establish "cancer resource centres" in 104 hospitals, providing three kinds of service — information, psychological support and access to resources — with the Taiwan Cancer Resource Network given as the entry point[18]. Most people do not know that the hospital they attend has one; it is usually tucked into a corner of some floor of the outpatient building.

The phone call is to family medicine or metabolic medicine. If it has been a year since your blood pressure was taken or your glucose and lipids checked, put it in the diary either side of your next gynaecological oncology appointment — that SEER data is saying the same thing: non-cancer causes account for more than 40% of the deaths in this group, and that group of causes does have somebody looking after it, just not in the gynaecological oncology clinic.

From the physician

Treatment ending is not the same as health coming back. The one thing I most want you to do this year is book a family medicine or metabolic clinic and get your blood pressure, blood sugar and lipids up to date — non-cancer causes account for 41.5% of the deaths in this group, and nobody in the gynaecological oncology clinic is looking after that part for you. The second thing is more trivial and yet goes wrong exactly when it is most needed: copy the expiry date of your catastrophic illness certificate into your phone calendar and set the alarm for three months before. The clause says to re-apply three months before validity expires, and that if it is approved the periods of validity may run on; apply late and the effective date runs from the day you apply. How the interval in between is counted is not written any further down — that is a question for the medical affairs office or your case manager, not something to guess at.
