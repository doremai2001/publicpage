HOW THE TECHNIQUE IS CHOSEN

Protons in the pelvis: where the evidence stands

The gap in dose distribution is measurable, and the only randomised comparison that has read out found no difference; in anal cancer the marrow dose came down and the haematologic toxicity did not follow it down.

Pelvic Radiotherapy Guide · How the Technique Is Chosen · 24 references
#Proton Therapy#Evidence#Costs & Self-Pay#NHI Coverage

For an adult with a pelvic cancer wanting protons, the shape in the official documents is entirely self-paid — the scope of all three NHI proton codes opens with "patients under nineteen years of age". The physics and the regulations are in the two existing articles on protons; here I only set out where the evidence has got to.

"Are protons kinder to the bowel?" That question splits into two layers before anything else: one is dose distribution, where the gap can be measured; the other is clinical endpoints, where what gets measured is something else again. In the pelvis one set of data shows that gap more clearly than any other: in anal cancer the marrow dose comes down across the board on the proton side, and the haematologic toxicity does not follow it down. The physics of protons and the regulatory position are in "Protons: precise. Then what?" and "Is proton therapy actually better?"; here I only lay out which box the evidence has reached in each cancer.

My position first: pelvic radiotherapy is what I do every day, so every good word about radiotherapy in this topic is written more conservatively than anyone else would write it. Implants such as the rectal spacer are not something my department provides; I write about them as an option you can go and ask about, and for cost and for who performs them, please ask that department and the medical affairs office.

The dose-distribution box: the gap is real, and protons have trade-offs too

The physical difference protons make, brought down to your own pelvis, has a quantified sentence. A comparison of 10 plans each showed that in the pelvic field for advanced prostate cancer, intensity-modulated proton therapy left roughly half as much tissue volume receiving low dose (within 10 CGE) as photon modulation did, with no discount on target coverage[1]. A 14-patient planning comparison for whole-pelvis treatment after gynaecological surgery shows a bigger gap: marrow V10 58% against 86%, small bowel V20 21% against 56% — but the reduction in toxicity that study estimates is calculated from a model, not observed[2].

Protons have trade-offs inside their own box as well. A study of 140 plans in 20 patients with cervical cancer showed that pushing the mean marrow dose down from 17.3 to 12.0 GyRBE cost the other organs only a few tenths of a GyRBE; but at maximum marrow sparing the mean rectal dose rose by 5.8 GyRBE, up to as much as 12.4[3]. Every one of these is a planning study in 10 to 20 people. The same prescription can come out with rather different rectal doses at two different hospitals, so the number you want in your hand is the one from your own plan.

Prostate cancer: the only randomised comparison that has read out found no difference

The one place in the pelvis where a randomised comparison of protons against photons has read out is prostate cancer. PARTIQoL randomly assigned men with low to intermediate risk localised prostate cancer to protons or to photon modulated radiotherapy, with the primary endpoint being the patient-rated bowel score 24 months after radiotherapy, and 48% of the men used a rectal spacer[4]. When I checked the trial registry on 1 September 2026: 454 enrolled, primary completion date December 2025, and no results at all posted on the registry[5]; the primary results paper I could not find, so all I can cite are two second-hand accounts — a 2026 urology review states that "the preliminary results of the PARTIQoL randomized clinical trial did not demonstrate significant differences in quality-of-life outcomes" between proton therapy and photon modulated radiotherapy[6], and the report of the World Forum on Particle Therapy says in its own words that there were "no differences in toxicity or progression-free survival" in low to intermediate risk prostate cancer[7]. So the correct sentence is this: the only randomised comparison that has read out showed no difference — not "there is no randomised evidence yet", and not "protons were shown not to work". Another prospective comparison, of about 3,000 people, has a primary completion date of February 2026 and likewise has no results on the registry yet[8] — so it is not "there is nothing after PARTIQoL" either.

The retrospective data run in both directions and both have to be set out. The American SEER-Medicare analysis propensity-score matched protons against photon modulated radiotherapy (1,368 patients) and came out the other way round: the photon modulated group in fact had the lower rate of gastrointestinal complications, 12.2 against 17.8 per 100 person-years, relative risk 0.66[9] — and the labels have to be carried in full: a proton cohort from 2000 to 2009 (mostly passive scattering, not the pencil beam scanning of today), a retrospective design, and outcomes taken from insurance diagnosis codes. There is a reading favouring protons as well: a retrospective comparison at one medical centre from 2015 to 2018 found a meaningful decline in bowel function at 24 months in 47.37% on photons against 25.93% on protons, with no difference in either of the two urinary domains[10] — single-centre, retrospective, and the people who get to have protons have usually been selected.

The anal cancer pair is the cleanest demonstration of the line

Two studies from the same team, two years apart, are worth reading side by side. In a comparison of 41 patients with anal cancer, every dose metric for the pelvic bone marrow was lower with protons than with photon modulated radiotherapy, and the mean dose to active marrow was significantly lower; but there was no statistical difference in haematologic toxicity — at least one grade 3 or worse haematologic event occurred in 70% on photons against 86% on protons (p=0.48, 41 patients; numerically it is the proton group that is higher, but at this sample size the true direction cannot be made out), and the conclusion reads, in its own words, that proton therapy reduced bone marrow dose but was not associated with lower haematologic toxicity[11]. The same team's prospective feasibility trial in 14 patients is blunter still, saying that in this underpowered trial clinician-reported toxicity was not improved with intensity-modulated proton therapy[12]. The marrow dose came down and the blood counts were no better — both of those are true at the same time, and that is exactly what the line between "dosimetrically superior" and "clinically superior" looks like.

Gynaecological cancers, rectal cancer and re-irradiation: no randomised comparison yet

If your cancer is a gynaecological one, that box holds only single-arm and retrospective work. A prospective phase II trial in 25 patients met its primary endpoint (no grade 3 or worse acute gastrointestinal or genitourinary toxicity and no early termination of treatment) in 100% of them, and the authors say plainly that larger randomised trials are needed[13]. A retrospective comparison in 155 patients showed acute non-haematologic grade 2 or worse toxicity of 27% on protons against 43%, while the authors write in the same paper that there are no randomised trials comparing protons and photons in this indication and that the retrospective data are limited[14]. Rectal cancer is emptier still: a systematic review of 8 studies states that there is insufficient evidence to judge adverse treatment outcomes with protons relative to conventional radiotherapy, and that no study to date has evaluated radiation toxicity or tumour outcomes[15].

Re-irradiation is the box where protons have the strongest clinical motivation in the pelvis, but everything published is a single-arm series, and the complications are real. A retrospective series of 29 gynaecological re-irradiations: 1-year local control 83.5%, with grade 3 toxicity in 3 people[16]. Long-term follow-up of 23 people with locally recurrent rectal cancer: 3-year and 5-year overall survival 72.1% and 44.6%, but 12 of them recurred locally again, and 3 had grade 4 late gastrointestinal toxicity[17]. The denominators are all in the twenties and thirties, and the previous doses vary widely too.

The standard option stands up: the numbers on the photon side

To judge whether a gap has any weight for you, you first have to know the starting point. Prostate cancer at 86.4 Gy with daily fiducial-based image guidance: grade 2 or worse urinary toxicity at 3 years 10.4%, rectal 1.0%[18]; the 308-patient prostate data at 74.8 Gy: late grade 2 or worse urinary toxicity at 5 years 7.3%, gastrointestinal 6.2%[19]; and in the randomised trial in cervical cancer after surgery, image-guided modulated radiotherapy gave 3-year late gastrointestinal toxicity of 21.1%[20]. The proton side has a set of its own: a prospective single-arm study of 55 men having prostate plus pelvic nodal treatment reported late grade 2 or worse gastrointestinal toxicity of 16% and urinary toxicity of 41% at 5 years, with the authors concluding in their own words that phase III studies are needed to assess whether proton therapy offers a therapeutic gain relative to photon radiotherapy[21].

These two sets of numbers must never be subtracted from one another: the fields differ (one treats the prostate alone, the other includes the pelvic nodes), the doses differ, the fractionation differs, the length of follow-up differs, and the populations differ. The only things that can be compared directly are the two arms of a randomised trial — and that randomised trial found no difference. The conclusion runs in both directions: the photon side, with modern technique, has already pushed moderate to severe side effects down into the single figures and the low twenties per cent, and that is true; the gap in the proton dose distribution is measurable too, and whether it buys a difference in side effects has for now only the answer "not seen yet".

The shape of the money: one in-house fee page is all I could find

On the NHI side, since 1 January 2026 there have been three proton reimbursement items (36025B, 36026B, 36027B), the scope of all three opening, in their own words, with "patients under nineteen years of age", and noting that prior review is required and that reimbursement is limited to once in a lifetime per person per primary cancer; among the cancers listed in the announcement there is no adult pelvic cancer at all[22]. In the full fee schedule the eight items relating to adult protons are all marked "HTA item" with 0 points[23]. For an adult with a pelvic cancer wanting protons, the shape in the official documents is entirely self-paid.

On amounts there is only one citable source I could find, and what it is has to be said first: it is the in-house fee schedule page that the Kaohsiung Chang Gung Memorial Hospital proton therapy centre publishes on its own website — not an announcement by a regulator. Proton beam therapy there is charged in three levels according to the complexity of the modulation, at NT$21,750, NT$26,000 and NT$34,500 per session, and the page itself says the charge follows the physician's treatment plan[24]. I cite it only because it is the one price I could find with a source that can be checked; I have no cooperative or referral relationship with that hospital, and listing it is not a recommendation. That is that hospital's charge, not a quotation specific to pelvic cancers; for other hospitals I could find no announcement. The number of fractions in a pelvic course covers a wide range, so I do not multiply it out into a total, and I do not comment on the amount — please ask each hospital's medical affairs office for a written quotation.

Before you go for a consultation, get your own photon numbers in hand

Do not do the referral consultation in the wrong order. Ask two questions at your own hospital first: for my field, what rectal, bladder and small bowel doses does the photon plan actually come out at? And for my cancer, is there a randomised comparison in the proton box — and if there is not, what kind of study is it that can be cited at the moment? Only then go to the proton side and ask about the number of fractions and a written quotation. With your own photon numbers already in your hand, that consultation becomes a comparison; without them, you are only comparing adjectives. If you have had pelvic radiotherapy before and are there to discuss re-irradiation, take those three grade 4 toxicity cases above in with you and ask about them as well.

From the physician

Before the referral consultation, get the rectal, bladder and small bowel doses of your own photon plan from your own hospital first, and only then go and hear the written quotation from the proton side. If you are there to discuss re-irradiation, take the grade 4 toxicity case counts from those single-arm series in with you and ask about them as well.
