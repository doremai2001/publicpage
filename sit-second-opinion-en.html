THE FIRST MONTH

Asking for a second opinion, out loud

Medical Care Act Article 74 tells the original hospital it "shall not refuse". The fifteen-day clock is data protection law. Slides are specimens.

Situation Guides · The First Month · 11 references
#Law & rights#Getting Ready#How to ask#The system#Decisions

Something that may give you pause first: in that Dutch series of seventy, 90% heard the same advice. I have still written the procedure out, because what changed significantly was uncertainty, not the diagnosis — and because the wrong order wastes a trip to the medical records office.

"Can I go and hear what somebody else says? Would that be rude?" By the time someone asks me this, they have usually rehearsed it for a fortnight. The answer first: none of this has to be done behind anyone's back, because the road for getting your material moved was built into the law, and built wider than most people realise.

This piece is about the rights you already hold, and how to ask so what comes back is worth having. Every provision below is the version I checked on 30 August 2026.

The most useful provision, and the one most people have never heard of: Medical Care Act Article 74

The text runs: "Where a hospital or clinic diagnoses and treats a patient, it may, as required and with the consent of the patient or the patient's statutory agent, spouse, relatives or a person with a relevant connection to the patient, approach the hospital or clinic that previously treated the patient to provide a copy of the medical record or a medical record summary and the various examination report data. The hospital or clinic that previously treated the patient shall not refuse; the costs incurred shall be borne by the patient."[1]

Take it apart. The one who initiates is the hospital you are going to see, provided they consider it necessary and you have consented; the duty falls on the original one — the statute's words are "shall not refuse". So the first thing to say at a second-opinion clinic is: "Do I fetch this material myself, or will you request it?" Both routes are lawful; the only difference is who does the legwork.

You can apply yourself, and "medical record" includes the test reports

Medical Care Act Article 71: "A medical institution shall, at the request of a patient it has treated, provide a copy of the medical record and, where necessary, a Chinese-language medical record summary; it shall not delay or refuse without proper cause; the costs incurred shall be borne by the patient."[1] Note the subject: it is the patient who requests. Family members doing it is common in practice, but it usually takes the patient's authorisation and both parties' identity documents, and hospital rules differ — telephone the medical records office (病歷室) first to ask what to bring; the Ministry of Health and Welfare's Department of Medical Affairs (衛生福利部醫事司) hosts a model authorisation form for retrieving medical record data in its medical record summary section[2].

That "Chinese-language medical record summary" clause has an official reading, and it runs in your favour: Article 49-1 of the Enforcement Rules of the Act provides that where a patient requests a medical record summary, a Chinese-language summary shall be provided unless the patient indicates otherwise[2]. As for what a medical record contains, Article 67 puts "the various examination and test report data" into the text explicitly[1].

The Medical Care Act gives no number of days. The Personal Data Protection Act does

Article 71 says only "shall not delay without proper cause", with no period attached. But a medical record is also personal data as listed in Article 2 of the Personal Data Protection Act (個人資料保護法), and Article 13, paragraph 1 provides that where a data subject's request for a copy is accepted, a decision to grant or to refuse shall be made within fifteen days, extendable once by no more than fifteen days, with written notice[3]. Article 3 of the same Act adds that the right to request a copy may not be waived in advance or restricted by agreement.

Fifteen days is the deadline for deciding whether or not to grant. It is not "you will have the disc in your hand within fifteen days".

No national ceiling on the fee — but the schedule has to be on display

Medical Care Act Article 21 gives the approval to local government: "The standards on which a medical institution charges medical fees shall be approved by the competent authority of the special municipality or the county or city." Article 22 requires a receipt stating the items and amounts charged, and forbids overcharging or inventing items[1]. So it is lawful for the copying fee to differ between hospitals, and I will not write any amount here.

What you can look up: point 7 of the Ministry of Health and Welfare's Reference Principles for the Approval of Medical Fee Charging Standards (醫療費用收費標準核定作業參考原則) requires a medical institution to display its approved charging items in a conspicuous place and keep them published on its website[4]. To check a price, read the schedule the hospital has published, rather than negotiating at the counter.

Slides, blocks and the imaging disc: the two pieces the law does not reach

The medical record as defined by Article 67 is the record made by the physician, the various examination and test report data, and other health professionals' records; Article 74 says likewise "the various examination report data"[1]. Slides and paraffin blocks are specimens, not reports. I could not find any central regulation or interpretive letter listing pathology slides or paraffin blocks as something a patient may request be handed over, or that a hospital ought to lend out, and I could not find any central provision on how long pathology specimens must be kept.

In practice, the pathology department of the hospital you are going to see borrows the slides or blocks from the original hospital. So the correct order is to register first, take the reports and images across, then let the new team decide whether slides need calling for — not to go to a counter and ask for a paraffin block. The imaging disc likewise has no unified central provision; how it is handed over and what it costs each hospital handles under its approved charging schedule — ask the medical records office or the radiology department.

What a second read actually changed

First, a word easily misread: "discrepancy" in these studies does not mean misdiagnosis. One American institution laid out all 4,239 second-opinion pathology cases from 2018: major discrepancies that did not change management, 3.7%; major discrepancies that did change clinical management, 1.0%; by site, thyroid fine-needle aspiration had the highest discrepancy rate (15.3%), bone and soft tissue 9.6%, thoracic 3.0%[5]. At a comprehensive cancer centre, 9,289 consecutive outside cases reviewed in 2015 gave an overall surgical pathology discrepancy rate of 14.2%, splitting into major 2.2% and minor 12.0%[6]. Among 740 patients referred within one system, 14.1% had a discordant diagnosis, 4.1% a change of care as a result[7].

These percentages cannot be carried straight over to Taiwan, nor read as "send it for re-review and 14% get overturned". The reason is clearest in a British breast pathology referral series: another 740 cases, from 2019 to 2022, difficult ones already selected by an external specialist before being sent, and not the same 740 patients as in the paragraph above; on review, 79% confirmed the original diagnosis or one of the differential diagnoses originally offered[8]. Different denominators, so the numbers cannot be compared.

Imaging gives a similar signal. 153 outside staging MRI studies in endometrial cancer, re-read by radiologists specialising in gynaecological oncology: 37.9% differed from the first interpretation, and 24.8% affected pre-operative staging and could therefore change treatment[9]. These studies conclude for themselves that where review might change management — for instance where major medical or surgical treatment is about to begin — reviewing outside pathology material is worth doing. That is an institutional quality practice initiated from the medical side, not a verdict on any individual pathologist, and not every report needs sending out again.

The honest half: most people get the same answer

A systematic review pulled together 13 studies: the proportion of patients seeking a second opinion ran from 6.5% to 36%; the proportion in which the second opinion changed the diagnosis, the treatment recommendation or the prognosis, anywhere from 12% to 69%; the proportion in which the original diagnosis or treatment was confirmed, 43% to 82%. The same review says outright that data on patients' own outcomes and on the quality of second opinions are missing[10].

A Dutch prospective follow-up of 70 consecutively enrolled patients is more concrete: 90.0% received the same medical advice as at the first opinion, though 65.7% had expected to hear something different. What was measured as significantly changed was a fall in uncertainty; anxiety did not reach statistical significance[11]. Worth knowing beforehand: you will most likely hear the same answer, and its value is that you no longer have to guess.

This order is the least awkward and the least wasteful

One thing first: wanting a second opinion does not mean stopping what has already been arranged. Investigations, surgery, radiotherapy or chemotherapy that are already scheduled go ahead as planned; do not cancel or postpone a clinic appointment on your own because you are waiting for material, for an appointment slot or for another report. If you want to hear a second opinion before you decide, say so directly to your current team, and how the timing is arranged is decided together with the medical team.

Step one, tell your current team you would like another opinion, and ask what to take. They know which investigations are done and which are not yet settled. Deliberate concealment costs you this: you arrive with incomplete material and come back with an equally incomplete opinion.

Step two, go to the medical records office and ask what is needed to apply for a copy of the record, and how the fee is worked out. Step three, when you book the second-opinion clinic, ask whether they will approach the original hospital for you. Article 70 of the Medical Care Act requires medical records to be kept for at least seven years, and the records of a minor for at least seven years after majority[1] — if you want to go back for material years later, that decides whether it is still there to fetch. The order of the whole first month is in "The first month: the boxes that actually have to be ticked", and the team meeting box is in "Has my case been discussed by a team?".

From the physician

The order is what matters: tell your current team you want another opinion and ask what to take; ask the medical records office what an application needs; when you book, ask whether they will approach the original hospital for you. Slides and blocks are specimens, not something to collect at a counter. Investigations and treatment already scheduled go ahead as planned.
