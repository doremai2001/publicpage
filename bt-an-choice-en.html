VESTIBULAR SCHWANNOMA

Watching, radiosurgery, surgery

The three paths are not backed by evidence of the same hardness: only two of them have ever been compared at random, and what was compared was tumour volume, not hearing. Every preservation rate here carries four labels.

Benign Brain Tumour Guide · Vestibular schwannoma · 21 references
#SBRT / SRS#Surgery#Hearing#Decisions

There is one randomised trial on this question, and what it asked was whether the tumour shrank, not whether you are living well. Which is why this article is full of brackets: behind every percentage I have filled in the scoring system, the number of years, the number of people and the treatment — including the few that go against my own side.

Three paths sit in front of you: watch first, radiosurgery, surgery. The state of the evidence decides how hard every number below can be stated — and on this question there is one randomised trial, what it compared was radiosurgery against watching, and its primary endpoint was tumour shrinkage; how surgery compares with radiosurgery is, to this day, still only retrospective cohorts and meta-analyses.

My position first: stereotactic radiosurgery and fractionated radiotherapy are the part I deliver myself; the other side of every comparison in this topic is my neurosurgical colleagues' operations, my neuro-otology colleagues' hearing assessments, and my endocrinology colleagues' drugs. That is why every comparison here comes with its primary source, so you can take it to neurosurgery, to neuro-otology and to endocrinology for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

That randomised trial did not compare what you think it compared

V-REX was a single-centre, observer-blinded randomised trial at one Norwegian hospital: 100 people with a newly diagnosed small or medium-sized vestibular schwannoma — also called an acoustic neuroma — were randomly assigned either to upfront radiosurgery or to watching first and treating if it grew, and followed for four years. Randomised means the allocation was decided by chance rather than by anyone's preference; observer-blinded means those measuring the outcome did not know which arm a patient was in. The primary endpoint — the one result the trial was designed and sized to answer — was the tumour volume ratio: at four years the radiosurgery arm was at 0.87 times its original volume (95% confidence interval 0.66 to 1.15, the range the true value plausibly sits in) against 1.51 in the observation arm, a ratio of 1.73, p=.002[1]. The tumours got smaller. But of 26 prespecified secondary endpoints, 25 showed no detectable difference, and hearing and quality of life were both among those 25. Within four years 21 of the observation arm (42%) had radiosurgery because of growth and 28 (56%) were never treated at all; in the radiosurgery arm 45 (94%) needed no further treatment[1].

A 2026 review from the same Norwegian group — which states that it is not a systematic review — writes that V-REX remains the only randomised study in this field[2]; the Brazilian Society of Otology guideline gives the reason in plain terms: random allocation in this disease raises ethical problems[3].

A hearing-preservation rate without its four labels is not worth believing

Denmark followed 156 people with an intracanalicular vestibular schwannoma, pure observation, for nine and a half years. Same ears, audiograms from the same day, and the serviceable-hearing preservation rate recalculated under a different system: AAO-HNS classes A to B gives 34%, word-recognition-based classes 1 to 2 gives 58%[4]. Change the ruler, and there are 24 percentage points between the answers.

So every hearing-preservation rate has to carry four labels: which scoring system, how many years of follow-up, how many people, which treatment. What each system puts in its boxes is in "Hearing is what this decision is really about".

The one document that puts all three paths in a single table

I found only one document that puts them into the same table, using one definition of serviceable hearing (AAO-HNS class A or B, or Gardner-Robertson class 1 to 2) and one set of time points: the Congress of Neurological Surgeons guideline of 2018. The probability bands at two, five and ten years are laid out like this — and the gap goes first: these are ranges the guideline assembled from a systematic review, not a single study, and it gives no pooled number of patients. Radiosurgery 50% to 75%, 50% to 75%, 25% to 50%; microsurgery (small to medium-sized sporadic tumours) 25% to 50% in all three boxes; conservative observation 75% to 100%, 50% to 75%, 25% to 50%; and for people starting at class A, the ten-year observation box reads "insufficient data to make a determination"[5].

All of these recommendations rest on Level 3 evidence, and the verb in the original is "should be counselled that there is a … probability" — these are probability bands to explain with, not a ranking. The three paths share exactly one set of prognostic factors: the better the word recognition and pure-tone threshold before treatment, and the smaller the tumour, the higher the chance of preservation[5].

Watching is not a free option, and what it costs is hearing

The other columns from those same Danish patients over nine and a half years: the pure-tone average fell from 51 to 72 dB, word recognition from 60% to 34%, and the proportion in AAO-HNS class A went from 19% to 3%[4]. Another observational cohort, 636 people: 53% had word recognition above 70% at diagnosis and 31% still did ten years later; but of those who started at 100% word recognition, 88% still had good hearing at ten years[6]. The Brazilian guideline says the same thing more directly: "hearing may continue to deteriorate even when the tumour has not grown"[3].

The probability of growth: in the Danish national cohort of 2,312 people under observation, using a change of more than 2 mm in maximum diameter as the criterion, 21% of intracanalicular and 37% of extracanalicular tumours grew within five years[7]. The measurement method has to be labelled — this set is linear, while V-REX measured volume, and 2 mm on a diameter can be more than 20% on volume. The schedule itself is in "Watching is a form of treatment".

Hearing falls on both paths; what radiosurgery buys is something else

A Norwegian prospective comparison (237 people, mean follow-up 55 months) took loss of serviceable hearing as its endpoint: conservative management 54/71 (76%), radiosurgery 34/53 (64%), no statistical difference — while radiosurgery cut the probability of further treatment to roughly a tenth[8]. A Danish series of fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy (54 Gy in 27 to 30 fractions) is less gentle still: of 21 people with serviceable hearing before treatment, 8 (38%) still had it two years later, which was 1.8 times worse than a matched observation control group over the same period (p=.007), and at ten years nobody in the irradiated group retained serviceable hearing[9]. Both of those papers say only "serviceable hearing" without naming which classification — one of the four labels missing, so discount them as you read. How single-session and fractionated are chosen between is in "One session, or fractionated".

So "radiosurgery preserves hearing better" cannot be written, and neither can "watching preserves hearing better": hearing is falling on both paths, and in most comparisons the speed is indistinguishable. What radiosurgery buys is two other things — tumour control, and one fewer re-treatment. A systematic review of 24 studies and 4,079 tumours found a ten-year tumour control rate of 90.93% against 65.24% in 1,959 untreated tumours, an absolute difference of 25.7 percentage points and a number needed to treat of 4 — that is, four people treated for one to benefit; but for small Koos grade 1 tumours the absolute difference was only 18.31 percentage points and the number needed to treat 6[10].

The worry about malignant transformation has to be discussed in the same passage as that benefit: with long follow-up, malignant transformation after radiosurgery is of the order of two in a thousand[11], and people who have never been irradiated carry a baseline risk of their own to begin with — the denominators, the baseline and the annual risk are in "Can radiotherapy grow a second tumour".

"Tumour control" has no agreed definition in this literature

A meta-analysis of 23 studies laid out what each study had meant by tumour progression: a 20% increase in maximum diameter, a 10% increase in volume, a 2 mm increase in maximum diameter, needing further treatment, and so on. In the end the authors could only set their own — "did not receive further treatment" — and conceded that it may understate the failure rate. So do not set a radiosurgery control rate beside a recurrence rate after gross total resection; those are two different rulers. Whether transient expansion is counted as progression or as control also depends on each study's threshold and time window, and that passage is in "After treatment: balance, hearing, the face". The hearing figure from that same meta-analysis has all four labels attached: single-session radiosurgery, 1,409 people, median follow-up six point seven years, pure-tone average 50 dB or better with word recognition 50% or more, preservation 59.4%; the ten-year figures from the individual studies are scattered between 23% and 58%, a set that carries no per-study n[12].

On the surgical side, there are three things you actually need to ask

The anatomy of the approach is not yours to worry about; the things to ask are three: the chance of keeping hearing, the facial nerve, and postoperative pain and cerebrospinal fluid leak. A meta-analysis of 6,889 patients comparing three approaches found no significant difference in hearing preservation or facial nerve function, and the risk ratio for leak was not significant either[13]. Facial nerve results are reported on the House-Brackmann grade[14], which divides like this: grades 1 to 3 can still close the eye completely with effort, from grade 4 it will not close, grade 5 leaves only slight movement, grade 6 is complete paralysis[15]. That grade 4 line is the one that matters most: once the eye will not close, eye care becomes a daily job.

The numbers side by side: after radiosurgery, new facial nerve deficit (worse than grade 2) 1.3% and new trigeminal neuropathy 3.2%[12]; among surgical patients selected with hearing preservation as the goal, 8 studies with follow-up of five years or more give long-term serviceable hearing (AAO-HNS A or B, 310 people) of 60% and long-term House-Brackmann grade 1 facial function (228 people) of 94%[16] — that is a selected population; the same Brazilian guideline also gives a range of 3% to 46% for persistent facial palsy and a surgical mortality of 0.5%[3]; leak in one surgeon's series of 146 patients was 2.7%[17].

One more thing has to be laid open: three citable documents point in different directions. The Brazilian guideline states that surgery is superior to radiotherapy for preserving serviceable hearing (strong recommendation)[3]; the Congress of Neurological Surgeons probability bands point the opposite way[5]; and a systematic review of six retrospective cohorts writes that radiosurgery is superior[18]. I am not going to pick a side for you.

Set the three approaches against quality of life and the differences are smaller than the weight of the decision suggests. A meta-analysis of 16 studies and 3,745 people using PANQOL, the vestibular-schwannoma-specific instrument, measured at least a year after intervention or diagnosis: observation 69.1 points (95% confidence interval 66.0 to 72.2, n=1,430), radiosurgery 66.9 (62.7 to 71.2, n=864), microsurgery 61.3 (57.2 to 65.4, n=1,451), with no gap between any two of the three groups exceeding the minimal clinically important difference — the smallest change a patient would actually notice — and high heterogeneity between studies[19]. Another prospective comparison points against my own side: 455 people, mean follow-up four and a half years, and on 0 to 10 scales only tinnitus showed a detectable difference, rising by 0.8 points in the radiosurgery group (p=0.005), while the observation group's rise of 0.2 and the surgical group's fall of 0.2 were both non-significant, and dizziness, balance and headache differed in none of the three; the authors' own caution is that a subjective difference like that should not be used on its own to make a decision[20].

What the Taiwanese clause says, and four questions to take into clinic

The NHI fee schedule holds two items for stereotactic radiosurgery (37028B and 37029B, both effective in the current version on 1 April 2026 — 115.04.01 in the ROC calendar). The indications name vestibular schwannoma explicitly, and require one of six conditions A to F to be met at the same time; condition F is the vestibular-schwannoma-specific one, and the clause reads 「F.顱內單側小腦橋腦角聽神經瘤寬度小於2.5公分（不含內耳道）者。」 — translated, a unilateral intracranial cerebellopontine angle acoustic neuroma less than 2.5 cm in width (not counting the internal auditory canal). Each item also carries a general threshold of its own: 37028B a diameter of less than three centimetres or a volume of fifteen cubic centimetres or under, 37029B a three-dimensional diameter no greater than 3.5×3.5×3.5 cm or a volume of twenty cubic centimetres, and both are limited to three lesions or fewer. On top of that, every single case requires prior case-by-case application to the insurer[21]. These are the thresholds for reimbursement, not the thresholds for whether treatment is clinically warranted. The literature's own dividing lines disagree with each other, and this clause is yet another ruler.

I am not going to tell you which path to take. Four questions instead: given my size, my position and this audiogram, which treatment are you assessing; the preservation rate you have just quoted — which scoring system, how many years, how many people, which treatment; if I watch, how often is it scanned and what would change the decision; if I have surgery, what are your own group's facial nerve and leak figures. Take those four sentences unaltered to neurosurgery and to neuro-otology, ask each of them once, and put the two sets of answers side by side before you decide.

From the physician

Only two of these three options have actually been compared at random, and what was compared was volume, so I am not going to rank them for you. There is a cheap way to see whether a percentage can be trusted: ask the person quoting it which scoring system, how many years, how many people and which treatment sit behind the number, and discount it if even one of the four is missing. Take the same set of questions to neurosurgery and to neuro-otology, hear both out, and then come back and decide.
