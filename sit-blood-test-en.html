WHEN TREATMENT STALLS

One tube of blood, two opposite answers

Count cells, switch chemotherapy: no survival gain. Find a named resistance mutation, switch to its drug: progression-free survival improves.

Situation Guides · When Treatment Stalls · 19 references
#Biomarkers#Genomic testing#Decisions#Cost & benefits

There is one trial I looked for a long time and never found: a randomised comparison of choosing drugs by in-vitro sensitivity against the standard process. I can only say I could not find one; I cannot say none exists. The highest level published is small and retrospective, so I am not printing those numbers.

"Doctor, a blood test can tell whether the tumour is growing, can't it?" That sentence hides two entirely different tests: one catches whole tumour cells circulating in the blood and counts them, the other reads the DNA that a tumour sheds into plasma as it breaks down. They work on different principles and answer different questions. And the line that matters most between them has been tested head-on by randomised trials.

Circulating tumour cells predict well, and the cut-off is different in every cancer

In 177 evaluable patients with metastatic breast cancer, using five cells per 7.5 mL of blood as the threshold: those at or above five at baseline had median progression-free survival of 2.7 months (progression-free survival counts from the start of treatment until progression is visible on imaging, not how long someone lives) and median overall survival of 10.1 months; those below five had 7.0 months and more than 18 months (both p<0.001), and on multivariable analysis it was the most significant predictor[1]. Among 231 evaluable men with castration-resistant prostate cancer, those in the unfavourable group before treatment had median survival of 11.5 months against 21.7 months in the favourable group; it predicted survival better than a 30% fall in PSA (area under the curve 81 to 87% against 58 to 68%, p=0.0218; area under the curve measures how well two groups can be told apart, and 50% is guesswork), and this is the dataset on which the US Food and Drug Administration cleared the assay for castration-resistant prostate cancer[2]. A 430-patient study in metastatic colorectal cancer used a threshold of three cells, with median survival of 9.4 against 18.5 months between the unfavourable and favourable groups[3].

Three cancers, three different lines. There is no universal number you can hold your own report up against.

The trial that settles it: the count was accurate, and nobody knew what to switch to

Whether something predicts an outcome and whether treatment can be changed on the strength of it are two different questions.

SWOG S0500 enrolled patients with metastatic breast cancer starting first-line chemotherapy. Those with a low baseline count stayed in group A; those who started high and fell by day 21 were group B; the 123 whose counts were still high after 21 days of treatment were randomised to stay on the same chemotherapy or switch immediately to a different one, with overall survival as the primary endpoint[4].

The result was median overall survival of 10.7 months against 12.5 months, P = .98 — they switched, and it made no difference[4]. Inside that same trial the count remained a powerful predictor: median survival in groups A, B and C was 35, 23 and 13 months (p<0.001)[4]. The authors' conclusion is plain: for patients with persistently elevated counts after 21 days of chemotherapy, early switching to an alternative cytotoxic drug did not prolong survival.

A French trial, CirCe01, applied the same idea in third line and beyond, and survival again did not differ (hazard ratio 0.95, 95% confidence interval 0.6 to 1.4, p=0.8) — a hazard ratio compares the relative rate at which events occur in two groups over the same period, not a multiple of probability; 0.95 in plain words means "in this dataset events arrived at almost the same speed in both groups", not "a 5% lower chance of something going wrong". The authors' own conclusion was that, limited by accrual and compliance, the trial failed to demonstrate clinical utility for monitoring circulating tumour cells[5].

One tube of blood, opposite answers

PADA-1 monitored 1,017 patients with hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer for a rising ESR1 resistance mutation in blood; 279 (27%) developed a rise and 172 (17%) were randomised; median progression-free survival from randomisation was 11.9 against 5.7 months, hazard ratio 0.61 (0.43 to 0.86), p=0.0040[6]. SERENA-6, a double-blind placebo-controlled phase 3 trial of the same concept, screened 3,256 people and only 315 (9.7%) reached randomisation; median progression-free survival was 16.0 against 9.2 months, hazard ratio 0.44 (0.31 to 0.60), p<0.0001[7].

So the whole thing compresses into one sentence: count the cells, switch to a different chemotherapy, and survival is no different; detect a named resistance mutation, switch to the drug that matches it, and progression-free survival clearly improves. The difference is not in the act of drawing blood. It is in whether that tube points at something a drug can be matched to.

Read the boundaries of the two positive trials alongside them: both are confined to a specific cancer, a specific line of treatment and one named mutation, both had progression-free survival rather than overall survival as the primary endpoint, and SERENA-6 is an interim analysis. STIC CTC, which used the cell count to decide chemotherapy first or endocrine therapy first, reached non-inferiority (hazard ratio 0.94, 90% confidence interval 0.81 to 1.09): it shows that triaging this way is not worse than the physician's judgement, not that it is better, and 10 percentage points more of that group actually received chemotherapy[8].

"Drug sensitivity testing on circulating tumour cells" — I could not find that trial

I could not find, in the literature databases, a single clinical trial randomising drug choice by in-vitro sensitivity results against drug choice by the standard process. I can only say I could not find one; I cannot say none exists. The highest level published is small retrospective work: a retrospective analysis of 85 patients with advanced biliary tract cancer compared those whose drugs were chosen by sensitivity testing against two other groups[9] — retrospective, non-randomised, single-institution, with very unequal group sizes, and the authors describe it as the first report of its kind. A design like that cannot show that the sensitivity testing is what picked the right drug, so I am not printing those numbers. A Taiwanese technical paper showed these cells can be expanded outside the body, but its endpoint was technical feasibility, with sensitivity-versus-outcome comparison only as a preliminary case series[10]. This sits at "it can be done, and small studies are being run" — and data at that stage does not hold up an out-of-pocket test order.

Nothing found in plasma does not mean nothing is there — the figure is 30%

The NILE study enrolled 282 patients with newly diagnosed metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer; guideline-recommended biomarkers were found in 60 by tissue and 77 by plasma, and using both raised the number of patients identified by 48%, with plasma reporting a median six days faster than tissue (9 days against 15)[11]. In the plasma genotyping analysis from osimertinib, plasma detection of T790M had a sensitivity of 70%, and the authors concluded that, because of the 30% false-negative rate, patients with a negative plasma result still need a tissue biopsy[12]. When the blood is drawn changes the answer too: of 243 patients sampled contemporaneously, 81.1% had the tissue variant detected in plasma, against 56.1% of the 57 sampled at a different time[13].

There is also a false positive that comes from the bone marrow. A high-intensity sequencing study tested plasma and matched white-cell DNA together and found that in people without cancer, 81.6% of plasma variants carried the signature of ageing blood cells; in cancer patients it was 53.2%[14]. That is not to say 80% of reports are wrong. It explains why a good assay sequences the white cells at the same time and subtracts this out.

The 2018 joint review from the American Society of Clinical Oncology and the College of American Pathologists says that for most plasma DNA assays in advanced cancer the evidence of clinical validity and utility is insufficient, that plasma and tissue results can disagree, and that it therefore supports tissue testing to confirm a negative plasma result[15]. The European Society for Medical Oncology's 2022 position goes a step further: in advanced cancer, validated assays with adequate sensitivity may be used to find actionable variants, but tissue remains the first choice for many patients, and an uninformative result should trigger reflex tissue testing[16]. For minimal residual disease the same recommendations advise against routine use, for lack of evidence that treatment can be changed on it — and that question is covered in full in "What an undetectable ctDNA actually means".

The Taiwan section: the sequencing the NHI covers is done on tissue

Tumour next-generation sequencing, covered by the National Health Insurance (健保) since 113 年 5 月 1 日, is restricted by the wording to pathological tumour tissue from confirmed disease, with only germline BRCA1/2 done on blood[17]; the full text of that clause and the once-in-a-lifetime limit are in "It came back: the first move is not a new drug". Nineteen cancers are currently included, and whether yours is among them is a question for your treating team. It is also why doctors so often want to biopsy again at recurrence.

And circulating tumour cells? I checked all 5,159 records in the fee schedule (支付標準) line by line and found no matching item — zero hits for 循環腫瘤細胞, and 液態 hitting only acid-fast culture and liquid-nitrogen cryotherapy[18]. That is what is findable in the file, not a judgement about reimbursement; for the actual position, ask your case manager (個管師) or the hospital billing office (醫務課). The full table is the 114.03.13 version, verified 2026-08-30; the three sequencing codes appear in the amendment schedule effective 115 年 9 月 1 日, so points and rules need rechecking. Separately, out-of-pocket genetic or cellular tests are laboratory-developed tests, and both the laboratory and the test item must be approved and registered with the central competent authority before they can be performed[19].

For this self-pay order, one question is enough

Not "is this test accurate" but: will this result change what I do next? If it will not, why am I having it? That question works for a cell count, for sensitivity testing, for any panel sold out of pocket. Then a second: is this an approved laboratory-developed test item? How to ask about the money is in "How to ask about a self-pay item" and "The cost that isn't on the receipt". And whatever that tube shows, whether to change drug and when is a decision you make with your medical team. The report does not make it by itself.

From the physician

Predicting well and being able to change treatment on the strength of it are two different things, and this piece guards that line from start to finish. So for any test order the question is not whether it is accurate but whether the result will change what I do next; if it will not, there is no reason to do it. And whatever the tube shows, when to change drug is decided with your team.
