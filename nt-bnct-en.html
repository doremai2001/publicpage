THE FOUR TECHNOLOGIES

BNCT: the radiotherapy that does not aim

Its aim is biological: the selectivity lives in where the boron goes, which is both the clever part and the limit. Japan's approval covers one specific indication; in Taiwan it is trials and a compassionate route, not routine care.

Next-Generation Therapy Guide · The four technologies · 16 references
#BNCT#Evidence#Costs & Self-Pay#Clinical Trials

This article pulls apart three things that keep getting mixed together: a cell-level selectivity that is physically real, a Japanese drug licence earned with one 21-patient trial, and a Taiwanese compassionate route with an officially posted price. Once they are apart, you will find that what usually needs watching is not the technology itself — it is the headline.

Warning 1 (what this displaces): Most of the treatments in this topic are paid out of pocket, and most sit outside standard treatment. Before you decide, you need to know that what they displace is usually not time but three things of real value: standard treatment you have not yet used up, money you will need for later lines, and your eligibility for clinical trials (many trials exclude people who have recently received other experimental treatments). And there is one more, harder to see — physical reserve. The strength burnt through in a course that does not work may no longer be there when a genuinely useful option appears. So the question is not whether this is worth trying — it is what you give up by doing it.

BNCT's compassionate route has an officially announced price: NT$1.2 million for the first treatment and NT$1 million for each treatment after it[1] — and money of that size is exactly the money that later-line self-paid drugs, or seeing yourself through a clinical trial, would draw on.

Warning 2 (finding the right doctor): Do not make this kind of decision alone, and do not make it on the word of the person offering the treatment. The doctor you are looking for is one willing to tell you "this is not right for you" — if they never once say who this is NOT for, that is not a consultation, it is a sale.

My position first: proton therapy and hyperthermia are both technologies within my own field, and the hospital I work at has the equipment for them. So I draw every line in this topic tighter, not looser.

What "does not aim" means: two steps

Photons, protons, carbon ions — the logic of all three is geometric: point the beam at the tumour. BNCT is different. It works in two steps. Step one: an intravenous infusion of a boron-carrying drug, so that tumour cells take boron-10 in. Step two: irradiation with low-energy thermal neutrons; when boron-10 captures a neutron it splits into an alpha particle and lithium-7, and the energy is released within less than 10 micrometres — about the diameter of one cell, so the killing is locked inside the cell that took up the boron[2]. The boron drug BPA enters cells through the amino-acid transport protein LAT1, and aggressive tumours tend to express LAT1 at higher levels — that is where the selectivity comes from[3]. So its "aim" is biological, not geometric: the neutron field covers tumour and surrounding normal tissue alike, and the selectivity depends entirely on which cells took up the boron[2].

Where the selectivity lives, the weakness lives too

The beautiful part of the design and its limitation are the same thing: cells that took up plenty of boron are destroyed; a tumour cell that took up little is physically out of reach. And uptake is uneven, and it shifts: Taiwan's prospective trial measured the tumour-to-normal-tissue boron concentration ratio with positron emission tomography — for the first irradiation the median (line the values up and take the one in the middle) was 3.4; for the second, 28 days later, it had dropped to 2.5. A lesion whose uptake is too low cannot receive the prescribed dose even on the dose calculation itself[4]. Nor does "no aiming needed" mean normal tissue goes unharmed.

Japan's drug licence was earned with a 21-patient trial

The pivotal trial, JHN002, was an open-label phase II that enrolled 21 patients with recurrent or locally advanced head and neck cancer, treated with an accelerator neutron source plus the boron drug; the primary endpoint, the objective response rate, was 71% — the objective response rate is the proportion of patients whose tumours visibly shrink, which is not the same as cure and not the same as living longer[5]. On that basis Japan's drug regulator, the PMDA, approved the boron drug borofalan in March 2020, with the indication "unresectable, locally advanced or locally recurrent head and neck cancer", and it entered Japanese health insurance that June; the full regulatory and coverage context is in "Approved, covered, effective: three different things", so here I keep only one line: in Japan, for this one indication, it is an approved and covered formal treatment[6][7]. The post-marketing all-patient registry took in 162 patients; the common acute adverse events were hyperamylasaemia at 84.0%, stomatitis at 51.2% and hair loss at 49.4%[7]. What that shows is that the safety profile broadly holds up in the real world and that this indication stands; it is not a general endorsement of BNCT against cancer — as of February 2026, this is still the only indication on the licence[6].

A fast response is not a held response: glioma, and Taiwan's trials

In recurrent malignant glioma, Japan has a 27-patient single-arm phase II: one-year survival 79.2%, median survival 18.9 months — bright numbers, but the comparison was historical data: no concurrently randomised control group, only past studies from elsewhere to compare against; and in the same patients, the median progression-free survival judged by imaging criteria was only 0.9 months, its interpretation confounded by brain oedema and subsequent medication[8]. Glioma has no drug licence to this day[6]. Taiwan's own prospective trial is even more plain-spoken: of 17 patients with recurrent head and neck cancer, 6 had complete responses and 6 partial responses, but two-year locoregional control was only 28%[4]; in the follow-on trial combining BNCT with image-guided radiotherapy, the response rate was 64% and one-year local progression-free survival 21%, and the authors themselves wrote that recurrences fell mostly within and at the edge of the irradiated field, and that future combinations should be with something other than radiotherapy[9]. Outside the trials, a Taiwanese compassionate-use series in recurrent nasopharyngeal cancer, published in 2026, treated 10 patients: the response rate among those evaluable was 25%, and 1 patient developed temporal lobe necrosis[10] — change the population and the numbers change with it; you cannot take the seventy-odd percent from head and neck squamous cancer and imagine yourself with it.

The distance between "conquered" and the primary source

At the end of August 2026 there was a news story about "BNCT treating breast cancer". Take it apart: at the bottom is a published retrospective case series of 4 patients — all triple-negative breast cancer after multiple lines of treatment, and the paper describes itself as small, retrospective and short on follow-up, its results to be regarded as preliminary and in need of prospective validation[11] — plus 5 patients treated through the compassionate route. The official press release announcing the results did reach for a hot phrase in its own headline, "first in Taiwan", but its body text was careful: it says explicitly that the hope is to accumulate cases and then move towards a human clinical trial, and the word "conquered" appears nowhere in it[12]. "Conquered" appeared in the news headlines. The lesson has two layers. One: a single-digit compassionate case series was written up in headlines as a conquest — the magnification happened in the headlines, not in the research itself; those patients' tumour responses are real, and so is the publication. Two — the layer more useful to you: going back to the primary source, and reading inward into the body text and the paper, is usually more honest than any layer of headlines: the paper itself says "preliminary", and the press release's own body text says the trial is still to come.

In Taiwan today: where the door is, and how wide

Taiwan's current neutron source is a research reactor, not a hospital treatment machine. There are two doors. One is academic clinical trials. The other is what the official pages call "emergency treatment" — a compassionate route of a special-case nature, which since 2017 has delivered a cumulative 629 treatments (a count of treatment episodes, not of people)[13], with assessment and referral through several collaborating hospitals[14], charged under the official fee schedule in force since 13 June 2024: NT$1.2 million for the first treatment and NT$1 million for each planned subsequent irradiation[1]. It is not an NHI item, and it is not a routine self-pay service you can queue for by booking an appointment; the official pages do not state its exact regulatory category, and since I could not find a formal provision I could cite, I am not going to give it a name. Two things must be kept apart: the irradiation system — the device — received a medical device licence from the Ministry of Health and Welfare in June 2023, which is not the same as "BNCT for cancer" receiving an approved indication; the boron drug has no drug licence in Taiwan[15]; and the accelerator-based BNCT centre only broke ground in August 2025, with opening expected in 2027 — the official press release itself writes of the hope that it will become a treatment formally licensed by the Ministry of Health and Welfare, which means it is not one now, and cannot be booked now[16]. Whether irradiation can go ahead is decided by physicians, from boron uptake measured by positron emission tomography together with the condition of the lesion — being able to pay does not get you a slot[4].

What NT$1.2 million displaces

The compassionate route's premise is that standard treatment has been exhausted or is unsuitable; someone who has not yet finished standard treatment was never its intended candidate. For those genuinely at the later lines, money of this magnitude[1] comes out of the same pocket as later-line self-paid drugs, or the costs of carrying you through a clinical trial. I am not going to rank those for you; but before you sign, put the other two or three uses of the same money down in black and white and compare them once. Expectations need calibrating too: the often-quoted 70.2% response rate is an emergency-treatment statistic pooled across cancer types and counted per treatment episode, not the efficacy result of a prospective trial[13]; the 25% nasopharyngeal series above is the reminder: your situation may sit a long way from the average[10].

Who can put BNCT on the table — and who should not be pushed onto it

The people with whom BNCT can reasonably be discussed are of two kinds. Patients who fit the Japanese approved indication — unresectable, locally advanced or locally recurrent head and neck cancer, above all those who have already received full-dose irradiation and for whom surgery and re-irradiation are both unfeasible[5] (but know this first: Japan's coverage applies to people insured in Japan, and a foreign patient pays the full cost out of pocket — before setting out, read the medical tourism section of "Approved, covered, effective: three different things"). And patients in Taiwan who, after multidisciplinary discussion by their original treatment team, meet the compassionate referral criteria and whose boron uptake assessment is feasible[14][4]. Unsuitable is every other situation: any common cancer at a stage where standard treatment has not been used up; and anyone who has been told "this is a universal targeted neutron — it can hit any tumour". No such thing exists: the selectivity is built entirely on boron uptake, and where the uptake is poor, the selectivity is not there[2][4]. In every article of this topic I say the same sentence: for the great majority of patients with common cancers, what decides the outcome is the stage, the subtype, and whether the full course of treatment was completed — not whether neutrons were involved. At your next clinic visit, ask the question out loud: "With my disease, are the preconditions for even discussing BNCT met?" A doctor willing to answer that question head-on is the doctor the warning describes — the one you are looking for.

Facts checked: August 2026.

From the physician

If you are looking up BNCT for a family member with recurrent head and neck cancer or a brain tumour, the order is: first confirm with the original treatment team that the standard options really are exhausted, then have the team assess the compassionate referral criteria and the boron uptake; then list the official fee side by side with the other uses of the same money, and take that to clinic.
