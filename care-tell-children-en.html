THE DECISIONS THAT ARE YOURS

How you tell a child matters more than whether

What tracks with how a child does is the quality of the talking, not whether you told them. Three sentences, age principles, warning signs.

Patient Education · The Decisions That Are Yours · 18 references
#Family#Caregivers#Quality of Life

If you are waiting for a moment when you feel ready, you can stop waiting: in the research, parents consistently underestimate their children's distress, and the children's own questionnaire scores run much higher. The effort belongs in how you say it, not in which day.

The people who ask me whether to tell their children have usually been putting it off for a month or two, and they ask with their head down. So the conclusion goes first: starting now is not too late, and you are probably not doing as badly as you think. This piece is not about whether to say it. It is about how — because that is the half the data attach to how the child does.

Two frightening claims worth blocking first

The first is "not telling makes children ill". A systematic review of this literature concluded that on the whole these children did not show widespread serious psychosocial problems compared with reference groups; the finding that stands up better is a slightly raised risk of internalising problems — anxiety, withdrawal, somatisation — with adolescent daughters the most clearly affected[1]. The same review cautions that the field leans too heavily on psychopathology scales, which may hide distress that is closer to the situation and lower in intensity.

The second is "the child will be shut out at school". Data from 60 children aged 8 to 16 whose mothers had breast cancer, 58 teachers and 1,138 classmates, compared against matched classmates, found no group difference in peer acceptance or friendship, and behaviour in the two groups closely similar[2].

Do not tip over the other way, though. A separate review taking only the 25 studies that used validated instruments and measured diagnoses or clinically significant symptoms reads heavier: children and adolescents of a parent with cancer had a higher prevalence of anxiety disorders, depression and post-traumatic stress disorder than controls, internalising more than externalising, girls particularly[3]. Putting both sides together is the true statement: the risk does tilt upward a little, but this is not "damage is inevitable". A population survey from two German cancer registries even points the other way: the 1,449 children of 976 survivors had better quality of life than the age norms, and only the subgroup with support needs did worse[4].

When he seems fine, you are usually asking yourself rather than him

In 76 newly diagnosed families where parents and children were both interviewed, the parents' reports showed children with almost no emotional distress or behavioural problems, regardless of age, sex, or which parent was ill; but what the children themselves filled in differed significantly, with adolescent girls reporting the highest scores for anxiety, depression and aggression[5]. Another study visiting 116 school-age and adolescent children living with a parent who had cancer, with ratings from three sources, found behavioural problems significantly above the population expectation[6].

So "he seems fine" is weak evidence. Children are particularly good at performing normality in front of an ill parent. That is how they protect you.

What attaches to how the child does is not whether you tell, it is how you talk

212 adolescents with a parent who had cancer completed impact-of-event and parent-child communication scales: children reporting more "problem communication" with either parent had more post-traumatic stress symptoms, sons and daughters alike; daughters reporting more open communication with the healthy parent had fewer symptoms[7]. The same dataset recorded something very practical: when the ill parent was on more intensive treatment, sons reported more communication problems with that parent, and daughters reported less open communication with both. In the heaviest months of the course, the house goes quietest.

These are cross-sectional associations, not causation, and they cannot prove that a few more sentences buys a symptom less. What they do support is this: effort spent on the quality of the conversation makes more sense than effort spent on the yes-or-no question of telling.

What children want to know is not what adults assume

A meta-synthesis of 16 qualitative studies drew out five themes: being told about the parent's cancer, emotional worries, changes to daily life, actively seeking factual information, and seeking emotional support; it also noted that most children are in fact told the diagnosis[8]. Interviews with 12 children aged 5 to 17 using play and art as the medium produced four themes: ongoing worry and distress, working to understand the parent's diagnosis, disconnection from support systems, and needing someone to talk to[9].

Telling is not a one-off event either. 17 young adults who had lived through a parent's cancer during adolescence were asked to look back: most families did talk, but the range and content varied enormously, the facts of the illness and its treatment were what came up most, and the conversations often happened at night[10]. There is no need to keep waiting for a perfect moment in which to say it all at once.

There are really only three things to say

The nature of this section has to be stated first: what follows has no research numbers behind it — I could find no validated source supporting particular age-specific wording. This is what I have worked out with parents in clinic. Treat it as experience, not as evidence. The order matters too.

  • What the illness is called. Use the real name. "Mummy has an illness called breast cancer" beats "Mummy isn't feeling well", because a vague version gets completed by the child, and the version they supply is usually more frightening.

  • This is not your fault, and you cannot catch it. Those are the two commonest misunderstandings children hold — that something they did or thought caused it, and that being close will pass it on. Do not wait to be asked. Say it.

  • Who is going to look after you. What children care about most is who collects them tomorrow and whether the weekend match is still on. Spelling out the arrangements for the next fortnight works better than any reassurance.

On age, I will give principles only. Pre-school children need concrete, repeated answers about daily arrangements, and they will ask the same question again and again, which is normal. School-age children ask about cause and contagion, and they ask "are you going to die" — the wording I suggest is "the doctors are using the treatment they are most confident about, and we will keep you told about how it is going", rather than promising that it will definitely be fine. Adolescents need to be treated as people with a right to know while keeping a life of their own; turning one into a second carer is the commonest harm at that age.

One more honest sentence. The interventions designed for these children do not perform well. A systematic review of 8 studies covering 6 intervention programmes found designs heterogeneous and quality low, and concluded that the interventions currently available do not adequately support the children of patients[11]. So I will not say that telling makes it all right. I will say: you tell them so that they do not have to guess on their own.

The school box: what it actually does is act on the class teacher

Should you tell the school? My answer is yes, mention it, but be clear about the purpose. An education programme designed for school personnel had 244 participants complete pre- and post-tests, with significant improvement in self-rated knowledge and confidence in helping families affected by cancer[12]. What that measured was teacher preparedness, not child outcomes — telling the school is so the class teacher knows this child may be off form at the moment, not so the child gets treated as a special case.

The disruption to daily life is real. In the US National Health Interview Survey from 2010 to 2018, children aged 5 to 17 with a parental history of cancer had an unadjusted odds ratio of 1.33 for "more than one day of school absence" (an odds ratio divides one group's ratio of happened to did not happen by the other's; it is not a multiple of probability; 95% CI 1.11–1.59, P=.002), but this fell to 1.12 (0.93–1.34, P=.23) after stepwise adjustment for child, parent and family characteristics, no longer significant; "any child emergency department visit", meanwhile, fell from 1.56 to 1.36 (1.13–1.64, P<.001) and remained significant[13]. Danish national registers follow people for longer: among those who experienced parental cancer before age 15 or 18, the relative risk of low educational attainment was 1.20 (1.14–1.25) and of low income at age 30, 1.11 (1.06–1.16); the effect concentrated clearly in the subgroups whose parent had a severe cancer type or died of cancer (1.52 and 1.61)[14]. That is a population-level association under the Danish welfare system, not a prediction about your child.

When you cannot carry it, here is who to call in Taiwan

A few signals deserve to be taken seriously: persistent sleep disturbance, refusing to go to school, obvious regression (things they could do, suddenly they cannot), talk of hurting themselves. If those appear, go to the hospital social work office (社工室) or child psychiatry yourself; do not try to hold it alone. Your own sleeplessness and low mood are a separate line, written up in "The kind of sleeplessness that won't lift".

Taiwan has one free resource built for exactly this: the parent-and-child camp run by the HOPE Foundation for Cancer Care (癌症希望基金會), which helps children put things into words, teaches them about cancer, and connects families in similar situations. Eligibility covers the diagnosed patient, their partner and their children, with one child between the third year of primary school and the final year of senior high; sessions run in the north, centre and south each year, places are limited, and dates are on the free line 0809-010-580[15]. The same foundation's free counselling also takes family members and primary carers, and the third topic on its published list is disclosure and communication about illness within the family — you can rehearse the first conversation with a counsellor rather than going in cold in your own living room[16]. Session numbers, length and locations are on the free line.

If a child talks about hurting themselves, do not wait until tomorrow. The Ministry of Health and Welfare's 1925 helpline (依舊愛我) is 24 hours and toll-free, and a minor may call it themselves[17]; Lifeline 1995 and Teacher Chang 1980 are equally round-the-clock[18]. Those numbers are more use written on the fridge than saved in your phone. Whether National Health Insurance (健保) covers psychological counselling for children, and whether your hospital has a child psychologist, I could find no general answer to — ask your case manager (個管師) or the social work office directly.

One question to open with at your next visit: does the hospital have a social worker or psychologist who could sit with me for the first conversation with my child? Most people do not know that is available.

From the physician

How old your child is, who else is in the house, and how heavy your treatment is decide how far the first conversation goes; there is no standard answer. One thing to do this week: agree the version with the other adult, because when two accounts differ a child supplies the more frightening one. School refusal or persistent sleeplessness means the social work office.
