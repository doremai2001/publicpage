Eight in ten get oral mucositis

Eight in ten develop oral mucositis and a third to a half of cases are severe. Its timeline, what the guidelines recommend, and what they explicitly tell you not to do.

H&N Guide · During Treatment · 11 references
#Oral Mucositis#Side Effects#Daily Life#Supplements

You cannot prevent this one, so the preparation that pays off is a routine: what to rinse with, what to leave alone, and how early to say that it hurts. Pain control here is not about comfort, it is what keeps the course running.

By week three or four of radiotherapy, "Doctor, my mouth is in ruins — has something gone wrong?" is a sentence I hear almost every week. Nothing has gone wrong: the field has to cross the lining of the mouth, and those cells turn over faster than the tumour does, so they react first. What matters is whether you have a routine you can keep up daily in the weeks when it does.

This is not a complication. It is on the schedule.

A systematic review of 33 studies and 6,181 patients irradiated for head and neck cancer put the mean incidence of mucositis of any grade at 80%. For WHO/NCI grade 3 to 4 — bad enough to wreck eating and need active intervention — it was 34% with conventional fractionation and 56% with altered fractionation[1]. In modern chemoradiation trials, grade 3 to 4 mucositis "can be seen in over 40% of patients"[2].

If yours is bad, you are not the exception. You are the majority.

Two more numbers from the same analysis: 16% were admitted to hospital because of mucositis (32% with altered fractionation), and 11% had radiotherapy interrupted or modified because of it[1]. That 11% is what I use to make the case. The length of the course is part of the treatment, and every day lost buys the tumour time. Mouth care protects the chance this course has of working, not just your comfort.

Which week does it start, and when does it stop?

Mucositis appears "within or after 2 weeks from the beginning of radiotherapy", lesions last from 7 to 98 days, and "most lesions resolve within 2 to 4 weeks after stopping the radiotherapy or chemotherapy"[3]. Chang Gung's handout tells Taiwanese patients to expect it about 2 to 3 weeks in, as dose accumulates[4] — the same thing in local words.

In practice: two quiet weeks, trouble in week three, the worst in the second half, a few more days of deterioration after the last fraction, then healing.

One qualification I always add: healed mucosa is not everything back to normal. A mouth that has stopped ulcerating has not got its saliva or its taste back — salivary recovery runs over months and sometimes years. People get demoralised here, expecting both to arrive together.

What the guideline recommends, and what it tells you not to do

The 2020 MASCC/ISOO mucositis guideline sorts each measure into a level. I quote the levels as printed and do not upgrade them[5]. (There is no ASCO guideline on mucositis. If you see "ASCO recommends" attached to a mouthwash, put a question mark next to it.)

  • Benzydamine mouthwash: a Level I recommendation for head and neck patients on moderate-dose radiotherapy (under 50 Gy) without chemotherapy; a Level II suggestion once chemotherapy is added.

  • Photobiomodulation (low-level laser): a Level II recommendation for radiotherapy alone, a Level I recommendation for concurrent chemoradiation. The protocols specify wavelength and dose — not every laser counts.

  • 0.2% morphine mouthwash: a Level III suggestion for mucositis pain during chemoradiation. You rinse and spit; this is topical analgesia, not oral morphine.

  • Multiagent basic oral care protocols: a Level III suggestion — a low level, but the floor everything else stands on.

The explicit against list deserves as much airtime. Chlorhexidine to prevent mucositis in head and neck radiotherapy: Level III against. Sucralfate, topical or systemic, for prevention or for pain: Level II against. PTA paste and BCoG lozenges remain not recommended[5]. A bottle of chlorhexidine at home has uses in periodontal care, but it is not holding mucositis off. Doxepin became "no guideline possible" in the 2020 update.

Photobiomodulation needs a caveat. Its mucositis evidence is genuinely good, and the WALT 2022 position paper calls it strong evidence based on multiple randomised trials. But the same document states that robust evidence for the lack of malignant cell protection or enhancement of tumour growth has not been published, that vigilance remains warranted, and that it should not be delivered over the tumour site[6]. In Taiwan I could not confirm it is routinely available or reimbursed for this indication — some centres have it, so ask your own team rather than demanding it.

Glutamine and honey: Level II suggestions, which is not a recommendation

These arrive in a relative's shopping bag more often than anything else. In the guideline they are Level II suggestions — a suggestion, not a recommendation, one rung lower, meaning some evidence but not enough[5]. The honey trials are small and cannot be blinded. Oral glutamine carries a safety note: in the stem-cell transplant setting, intravenous glutamine was recommended against at Level I after a mortality signal. Oral and intravenous are not the same thing, but the note exists and should not be skipped. There is a question on glutamine on this site's patient-education page; it belongs in a conversation with your team, not a pharmacy basket.

One frequent mix-up: sucking ice chips does not apply to head and neck radiotherapy. Oral cryotherapy in the guideline covers bolus 5-FU chemotherapy and high-dose conditioning before transplant, nothing else[5]. Radiation hits the mucosa directly; a cold mouth does not shrink the field. "Magic mouthwash", whose recipe differs by hospital, is a symptom-relief option your team may prescribe, not a proven measure.

The few things you actually do every day

Basic oral care is unglamorous: bland rinses — 0.9% saline, or a sodium bicarbonate solution[7] — several times a day, and without fail after meals and at bedtime.

No alcohol-based commercial mouthwash. Chang Gung's handout says to avoid alcohol-containing ingredients, start at the lowest concentration on the principle of not stinging, clean with a sponge swab or soft brush and saline, and avoid very hot and very spicy food, medicinal wine and ginseng[4]. Chi Mei adds no smoking, no alcohol, nothing spicy, nothing very hot or cold, and rinsing with warm salt water[8]. Common sense, until your mouth is raw and one bowl of hot soup costs you an evening's eating.

Anything you hold in your mouth or swallow — honey, glutamine powder, herbal rinses, Chinese herbal medicine, tonics — the team hears about it first. The Chang Gung handout tells patients to avoid strong tonics (烈補) during radiotherapy[4].

Pain is for treating, not for enduring

Too many people hold out until they cannot eat, are dehydrated and five kilos down, then say "actually, the last two weeks have been very painful". Analgesia here is stepwise and prescribed: topical benzydamine or morphine mouthwash, then oral opioids, then a patch or an opioid rotation[5][7].

The World Health Organization's 2018 cancer pain guideline states the principles plainly: by mouth, by the clock, for the individual, with attention to detail. It also covers the first analgesic, the maintenance opioid, rescue dosing for breakthrough pain, and opioid rotation[9]. "By the clock" is what people get wrong: taking something only once the pain has arrived is harder work and usually takes more drug. Your physician sets the doses. Speak up before you reach your limit.

Do not wait for your next appointment — call the same day

  • A fever of 38 °C or above, especially during chemotherapy.

  • Not being able to swallow saliva or keep fluids down, or signs of dehydration: dizziness, very little urine, a mouth too dry to speak.

  • Pain not controlled although you are taking painkillers as prescribed.

  • Bleeding in the mouth that will not stop, or difficult or noisy breathing.

  • A clear drop in weight over a short period.

  • White plaques, or sudden worsening after a period of improvement — candidiasis or a secondary infection needs a different drug.

The worst fortnight: who to ask, and what to ask

First: does my course include concurrent chemotherapy, and roughly what dose? That tells you your risk band, and whether benzydamine is a Level I recommendation or a Level II suggestion for you.

Second: can this hospital supply benzydamine mouthwash, and do I pay for it? Morphine mouthwash has to be compounded — does our pharmacy make it? I could not verify the National Health Insurance (健保) coverage status of either from an official NHI Administration document, so ask your hospital pharmacy or case manager (個管師) directly rather than trusting what is online. The NHI Administration publishes the medical service benefit and reimbursement schedule[10].

Third: how bad does the pain have to get before I call, who do I call, and by what time of day? That number in your phone is worth more than any percentage here.

The full guideline and the twelve systematic reviews underneath it sit on the MASCC website[11]. I put the levels in so you can tell how much weight each sentence carries.

From the physician

These figures are population averages, and how badly your mouth breaks down depends on whether chemotherapy is given concurrently, on the dose and on the treated volume. Do not endure pain to the limit before mentioning it: 11% of patients interrupt radiotherapy because of mucositis, so asking for pain relief is also protecting the treatment.
