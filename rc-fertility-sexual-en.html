DURING TREATMENT

Fertility questions have no next month

What pelvic chemoradiation does to erections, ovaries and bladder function, which preservation options exist, and why Taiwan makes you pay for them.

Rectal Cancer Guide · During Treatment · 14 references
#Sexual & Fertility#Fertility#Late Effects#Getting Ready

In one survey of young colorectal cancer patients, about one person in ten had discussed sexual function with their oncologist. Rectal cancer is also absent from Taiwan’s fertility-preservation subsidy list, which is a bill worth knowing about in advance.

Fertility preservation runs on a clock, and the clock is unforgiving: once the first fraction has been delivered, the conversation is over. You can ask about recurrence rates next month, you can ask about surgery next month. Sperm and eggs do not have a next month.

I know it is not an easy thing to raise. The doctor is busy, your family is in the room, and part of you thinks "I have cancer, why am I talking about this". So I have written it plainly enough to print, underline and push across the desk.

It is not that nobody cares — it is that nobody says it out loud

ASCO's 2025 update to its fertility preservation guideline, built on 166 studies, is explicit: reproductive risk should be assessed and explained at diagnosis, fertility preservation approaches must be discussed before cancer-directed therapy begins, and anyone who is interested or undecided should be referred to a reproductive specialist[1].

What happens is something else. A single-centre survey of early-onset colorectal cancer patients (84 respondents, 59.5% women) found only 11.9% (8/67) had discussed sexual matters with their oncologist, and 45% (18/40) had received specific fertility preservation counselling. In the end, 3 women and 3 men went through with ovarian tissue or sperm cryopreservation[2].

One person in ten had the conversation about sexual function. That is nobody's fault in particular. It is what happens when something gets filed under "later", and this has no later.

Men: erection and ejaculation are two separate accounts

A cohort of 429 men aged 50 or under with locally advanced rectal cancer, median follow-up 6.3 years, found a five-year cumulative incidence of erectile dysfunction of 25% (95% CI 20–30%) after chemoradiation against 3.9% (95% CI 1.0–10%) without radiotherapy, p<0.001. On multivariable analysis with death as a competing risk, chemoradiation stayed independent: hazard ratio 3.55 (95% CI 1.34–9.44, p=0.011)[3]. A hazard ratio of 3.55 means roughly three and a half times the chance over the same period — not that 35% of men are affected.

Within the irradiated group, covering the inguinal nodes was an independent risk factor (hazard ratio 2.38, 1.30–4.34, p=0.005), and the further the tumour sat from the anal verge, the lower the risk[3]. That is a question you can ask before treatment: does my field need to include the groin?

In the same study, though, ejaculatory dysfunction did not differ between the irradiated and non-irradiated groups[3]. That fits the understanding that ejaculation depends mainly on autonomic nerves — the hypogastric plexus — put at risk during surgery. It goes on the surgical account, not the radiotherapy one. Keeping them apart is how you know which doctor to ask.

There is something to discuss on the surgical side. A randomised trial in 46 patients compared low with high ligation of the inferior mesenteric artery during total mesorectal excision (TME): the low-ligation group had significantly better urinary, female sexual and erectile function scores at 1, 6 and 12 months[4]. The trial is small, so it supports only a qualitative conclusion: how the operation is done is a separate variable, and one you are allowed to raise.

Passing urine more slowly is not "just your age"

A multicentre model built on robotic total mesorectal excision (290 patients modelling, 122 external validation) found preoperative radiotherapy an independent risk factor for postoperative urinary dysfunction, odds ratio 2.40 (p=0.025), alongside age, a low tumour, a tumour larger than 5 cm and the number of nodes dissected; low ligation was protective (OR 0.38)[5].

A slower stream, incomplete emptying, frequency or leaking are all worth reporting. Do not put them down to age and sit on them.

Women: stenosis, dryness, painful sex — and the truth about dilators

First, the size of the fall. In 99 women with rectal cancer followed prospectively, the median Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) total went from 21.9 at baseline to 16.4 at one year and 11.5 at two years[6]. That curve keeps descending; it does not rebound when treatment ends.

The next part needs handling honestly. The evidence for vaginal dilators is far weaker than patients are led to believe. The 2014 Cochrane review, with searches to 2013, is unequivocal: "We found no trials and therefore analysed no data." The authors' conclusion is that there is no reliable evidence that routine, regular vaginal dilation during radiotherapy prevents stenosis or improves quality of life. Observational studies do show women who dilate frequently after radiotherapy report less stenosis, but that could be because dilation works or because of something else; and one randomised trial found encouraging dilation did not improve sexual function scores[7].

More recent prospective data is no more encouraging: 67 cervical cancer patients using a 3D-printed dilator were followed for a year and most still developed vaginal stenosis, though mostly grade 1, with grade 2 (LENT-SOMA) in 15.79%[8].

So here is how I put it: dilators, lubricants and topical moisturisers are widely offered everywhere, and women who use them report doing better — but this is not a preventive measure proven by randomised trials. I say so for two reasons: so it does not become a heavy daily duty, and so that if stenosis happens anyway you do not conclude you failed to try hard enough.

The ovaries: this should not be called a risk. It is the expectation.

The "effective sterilising dose" calculated by Wallace and colleagues — the fractionated dose at which 97.5% of patients have ovarian failure immediately after treatment — falls with age: 20.3 Gy at birth, 18.4 Gy at 10 years, 16.5 Gy at 20 years and 14.3 Gy at 30 years[9].

The standard dose for long-course chemoradiation in rectal cancer is 50.4 Gy in 28 fractions, and pelvic fields usually fall between 45 and 50.4 Gy (set out in full in "How the treatment order is set, and why it is not a menu"). If the ovaries are in the field, that is far above every one of those numbers. For such a patient, premature ovarian insufficiency should be treated as all but certain, not as a probability. Not a pleasant sentence, but it is the one that decides whether you spend an extra fortnight before treatment.

Those two weeks before treatment: what can actually be done

The options ASCO's 2025 guideline sets out[1]:

  • Men: sperm cryopreservation should be offered before cancer-directed treatment starts; where a semen sample cannot be produced, testicular sperm extraction can be considered. Testicular tissue cryopreservation in prepubertal boys remains experimental and belongs in clinical trials only.

  • The guideline adds a specific warning: sperm collected soon after cancer-directed therapy begins or ends may carry a higher risk of genetic damage. That is exactly why "we'll bank it during the course" is not an equivalent alternative.

  • Women: the established methods are embryo cryopreservation, oocyte cryopreservation, ovarian tissue cryopreservation, ovarian transposition and conservative gynaecological surgery; in vitro maturation is an emerging option.

  • GnRH agonists must not be used in place of these methods. People hear "an injection to protect the ovaries" and give up on egg freezing. The guideline says clearly that this will not do.

Ovarian transposition is the one most directly relevant to pelvic radiotherapy, and it carries its own price: reported complications include ovarian torsion, cysts, and ovulation-related peritoneal irritation severe enough to need surgical release[10]. And plainly: its aim is to preserve ovarian function. It is not a guarantee of fertility, and it can fail.

The Taiwanese answer is blunter than most articles admit

Four things, and ask about them in this order:

One: National Health Insurance (健保) does not cover assisted reproduction. Article 51, subparagraph 3 of the National Health Insurance Act (全民健康保險法) lists assisted reproductive technology among the services excluded from benefit[11].

Two: the Health Promotion Administration's fertility preservation subsidy for cancer patients does not include rectal cancer. The pilot programme (醫療性生育保存補助試辦方案) took effect on 1 September 2025 (114 年 9 月 1 日) and covers ROC nationals aged 18 to 40 with breast cancer or a blood cancer: up to NT$70,000 per egg retrieval cycle for women, up to NT$8,000 for sperm preservation in men, maximum 2 cycles per person for life[12][13]. A rectal cancer patient currently pays out of pocket. It is a pilot and the terms may change, so re-check the announcement at the moment you decide rather than relying on this article's date. The scheme people confuse it with — IVF Subsidy 3.0 (不孕症試管嬰兒補助 3.0, up to NT$150,000 for a first cycle under 39, and so on) — subsidises infertility treatment for couples undergoing treatment, not fertility preservation in cancer patients[13].

Three: two provisions of the Assisted Reproduction Act catch people out. Article 2 defines the recipients as a husband and wife, with the wife able to gestate and deliver — so you may bank gametes while single, but you cannot undergo assisted reproduction in Taiwan as a single person. Article 21, paragraph 2 requires gametes to be destroyed on the death of the person who provided them, or once stored for more than ten years (extendable with written consent)[14]. What banking sperm means therefore has to be understood with your partner. It is not an all-purpose insurance policy for "some day".

Four: on whether vaginal dilators are covered, I could not find an official document. The relevant NHI Administration pages were unreachable while I checked, so I will not claim it either way — ask your case manager (個管師) whether it is self-paid or covered.

How to get the sentence out

You do not have to phrase it well. "I still want children" and "sexual function matters to me" are enough; your doctor will know who to refer you to. If you cannot say it, hand this page over.

Then make the questions specific. Are my ovaries or testicles inside the field, and roughly what dose do they get? If I do go for fertility preservation, how many days does it delay treatment, and is that delay acceptable for my disease? And which department, which doctor — can I make the appointment today?

For anyone still hesitating: not talking about these things does not stop them happening. It only means they happen at a point where you no longer have a choice.

From the physician

Every option here has to be booked before the first fraction, so the referral request belongs in your very next appointment, not a later one. The Taiwanese subsidy scheme is a pilot and its terms change — re-check the announcement at the moment you decide. If you cannot say it out loud, hand this page to your doctor.
