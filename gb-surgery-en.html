AFTER THE DIAGNOSIS

What taking it all out actually means

The international grading no longer asks what fraction came out, only how many cubic centimetres are left on the postoperative MRI. And every link between extent of resection and survival, to this day, comes from observational data.

Glioblastoma Guide · After the Diagnosis · 12 references
#Neurosurgery#Decisions#Imaging#Evidence

That question outside the recovery room — did they get it all — no longer has a standard answer, because the way of asking it has been replaced. Every comparison here comes with its primary source, so you can take it to a neurosurgical clinic for a second opinion.

"Did they get it all out?" — that is the first thing a family asked me outside the recovery room on the day of the operation. I have to start with something that is hard to say in clinic and has to be said anyway: internationally, that way of asking the question has already been replaced. The grading in use now does not ask what fraction came out. It asks one thing about the postoperative MRI: how much is left.

My position first: the radiotherapy part of glioblastoma treatment is the part I do myself; the other side of every comparison in this topic is my neurosurgical colleagues' operation and my neuro-oncology colleagues' drugs. Tumour-treating fields I do not use, and they are not a service of my department. So every comparison comes with its primary source, so that you can take it to the surgical and neuro-oncology clinics for a second opinion; hearing them out before you decide is the process I recommend, not a concession.

The question is not "what fraction came out", it is "how much is left"

The grading published by the RANO resect working group in Neuro-Oncology in 2023 was built on 1,008 patients with IDH-wildtype glioblastoma diagnosed under the 2021 classification, collected retrospectively at seven European and American neuro-oncology centres, with the main analysis run in the 744 of them who went on to standard chemoradiotherapy after surgery[1]. It has four tiers only: supramaximal (cutting beyond the enhancing border), maximal (the enhancing tumour essentially cleared), submaximal, and biopsy (tissue only). There is no tier called total resection.

What it measures matters more. The system stratifies patients purely by the volume of residual tumour on the postoperative scan, in cubic centimetres; in a multivariable model including age, performance status, tumour location and MGMT status, the volume of residual enhancing tumour stayed significant, while "what percentage was taken out" lost its prognostic meaning[1]. The six-tier system of 2021 had carried both; the authors graded their own evidence then as class IIB to IV[2].

People who get more taken out were already different from people who get less

The four tiers separate cleanly enough: median overall survival 24, 19, 15 and 10 months[1]. These are numbers from a retrospective cohort, not anybody's deadline — how numbers like these should be read is in "That number, and how I am going to tell you about it". Each step down the tiers carries a hazard ratio for death of 1.46, which held at 1.34 once the other factors were included[1]. A hazard ratio compares how fast events happen in two groups: 1.46 means that in the group one tier down, deaths occurred nearly 50% faster. It does not mean "the probability becomes 1.46 times as big".

But the same paper worked out the sentence that most needs saying out loud: age, preoperative enhancing tumour volume, tumour location and MGMT promoter methylation status are themselves associated with the extent of resection[1]. Put bluntly: the group who get more taken out started out in better shape.

Meta-analysis falls into the same hole. A 2016 JAMA Oncology pooling of 37 studies and 41,117 patients: gross total against subtotal resection, relative risk of death at 1 year 0.62, at 2 years 0.84[3]. Relative risk divides the proportion of people in whom the event actually happened in one group by the proportion in the other, so 0.62 means "how many out of a hundred died" was down by just under 40%; converted into people, that is roughly one extra person alive at 1 year for every 9 treated, and at the 2-year mark one extra for every 17[3]. But put "any resection" against "biopsy only" in that same 2-year cell and the relative risk is 0.94, P=0.04, which works out at 593 people treated for one extra[3]. Same question, different time point, and the answer differs by that much. The authors graded their own evidence as GRADE moderate to low[3]. To this day, no randomised trial has ever assigned patients at random to "more taken out" or "less taken out" — every number above is observed.

How far does supramaximal actually cut

The word is easily read as "cutting healthy brain out along with it". What it means is this: once all the enhancing tumour is gone, taking out a further part of the region that does not enhance on imaging but is already infiltrated by tumour. RANO 2023 set the threshold — the non-enhancing tumour reduced by more than 60%, with residual no greater than 5 cubic centimetres[1].

Two things belong together here. In patients taken to that tier, the rate of new postoperative neurological deficit did not go up; but the same authors caution that this probably reflects surgeons only daring to cut that way in relatively safe locations, and that the benefit is likely confined to circumscribed tumours in less sensitive positions[1]. The other is an age boundary: the RANO working group's 2026 age-stratified report (1,260 newly diagnosed patients, 512 of them aged 65 or over) found that only in patients under 65 was supramaximal resection associated with better survival (40 against 20 months); in the setting of re-operation after recurrence no benefit was seen in either age band[4]. So "the more taken out the better" does not work as a general rule.

That fluorescence trial: what exactly was randomised

The 2006 Lancet Oncology 5-ALA randomised trial enrolled 322 patients with suspected malignant glioma whose enhancing tumour had been assessed as completely resectable, and assigned them at random to fluorescence-guided or conventional white-light microsurgery[5]. Note what was randomised: the tool, not the amount removed. The two primary endpoints were the number of patients with no residual enhancing tumour on early postoperative MRI, and progression-free survival at 6 months. The results: complete resection 65% against 36% (a difference of 29 percentage points, P<0.0001), 6-month progression-free survival 41.0% against 21.1%; serious adverse events within seven days of surgery did not differ between the arms[5].

Overall survival was a secondary endpoint in this trial and the main paper's abstract does not report its result; and the trial was stopped early per protocol once the interim analysis met its target, and trials stopped early tend to overestimate the effect[5]. So I am not going to tell you that a fluorescent agent will let you live longer. And one more thing: the people who could enter this trial had already been assessed as having enhancing tumour that could be completely resected — people whose tumours were badly placed were never in it[5].

Swapping in a more expensive tool changes nothing either. A 2023 JCO prospective controlled trial across 11 German centres in 314 patients with newly diagnosed glioblastoma compared intraoperative MRI with 5-ALA: complete resection 81% against 78%, P=0.79; and the intraoperative MRI arm's operations ran a hundred minutes longer (316 against 215 minutes)[6]. What did carry prognostic meaning was 0 cubic centimetres of residual, in progression-free and in overall survival alike, and more markedly in MGMT-unmethylated tumours[6]. The target is zero; there are several tools, and between the tools nothing separates them.

On the Taiwan side I could only get half an answer: 5-ALA (Gliolan powder for oral solution) is licensed, licence number 衛署藥輸字第 025524 號 (Ministry of Health and Welfare imported-drug licence 025524), and the indication reads, word for word, "for visualisation of malignant tissue during surgery for malignant glioma (WHO grade III and IV) in adult patients"[7]. Whether the NHI reimburses it, whether you pay for it yourself, and how much, I could not find an official provision I could cite — ask your neurosurgical team or the hospital's medical affairs office directly.

Awake craniotomy: good-looking numbers, low-grade evidence

Intraoperative electrical stimulation mapping is the technique awake craniotomy uses to mark out language and motor areas in real time. A 2012 JCO meta-analysis of 90 studies and 8,091 adults: with mapping, late severe neurological deficit was 3.4%, without it 8.2% (adjusted odds ratio 0.39); imaging-confirmed gross total resection was 75% against 58%[8]. An odds ratio is not "the probability becomes 0.39 times as big"; where events are not rare the two differ a great deal. More to the point: the authors state plainly that this is a meta-analysis of observational studies, and that 99.9% of the mapped operations involved eloquent areas against 95.8% of the unmapped ones — the two groups are simply not the same patients[8].

The 2022 Lancet Oncology GLIOMAP study used propensity score matching (making the two groups as similar as possible on known characteristics, which is still not random assignment) to compare awake surgery with surgery under general anaesthesia; in the 536 matched patients, neurological deficit at 3 months was 22% against 33%, and at 6 months 26% against 41%[9]. The direction is consistent, but which anaesthetic was used was decided by the attending surgeon or the multidisciplinary meeting[9]. The actual randomised trial is called SAFE, and when I checked in September 2026 its status was still recruiting[10]. So in glioblastoma, awake against asleep has no published randomised result yet.

The people who only had a biopsy: what the data say

Start with the risk side. Two large cohorts of about a thousand patients each reported the same order of magnitude: any new postoperative neurological deficit in roughly 15.6% to 15.8%, and mostly mild[1][11]. For surgical mortality I could not find a figure I could cite, so I am not going to give you a percentage. Bleeding, infection, a new deficit — those risks have to be spelled out to you by your neurosurgeon, for the position your tumour is actually in.

Then the sentence I most want to get across. In the RANO grading, the hazard ratio for death with biopsy only, against supramaximal, is 2.4 — but the 95% confidence interval runs from 0.7 to 10.9, P=0.205, so it did not reach statistical significance[1]. A confidence interval is the plausible range for an estimate, not your personal probability; this one stretches from 0.7 all the way to 10.9, which is to say that even its direction is unstable. The same paper also saw that patients who only had a biopsy went directly into palliative care more often[1] — that is a distribution produced by clinical decisions, not an effect of the biopsy itself. So let me say this in plain words: having had only a biopsy does not mean giving up. The EANO guideline says as much too: even where no tumour-directed treatment is planned, a definite tissue diagnosis is still helpful for discussions with the patient and family[12].

Extent of resection is one of the few things in this disease we can work at. It is not everything and it is not a guarantee; and how much can safely come out is decided in large part by where the tumour grew, not by your will or your surgeon's. Catch your surgeon before discharge and ask this one question: on my postoperative MRI, how much of the enhancing part is left? Which situations after surgery mean coming back to hospital the same day is in "When to come back the same day: three tiers".

From the physician

Extent of resection is one of the few things in this disease you can work at, but it is not a guarantee: how much can safely come out is decided in large part by where the tumour grew. One question before discharge is enough — on my postoperative MRI, how many cubic centimetres of the enhancing part are left? And if the answer is that only a biopsy was done, that is not giving up; a definite tissue diagnosis is useful in its own right.
