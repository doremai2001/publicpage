TAIWAN, AND AFTERWARDS

It grows in the upper tract too

In Taiwan's registry, three to four of every ten urothelial carcinomas are in the upper tract. This article sets out the aristolochic acid evidence, the two ban announcements plus one management regulation, and the two analyses after the ban that point opposite ways.

Bladder Cancer Guide · Taiwan, and afterwards · 23 references
#Screening#Prevention#Surgery#Evidence

That this disease is unusually common in Taiwan is true. How unusually common is a question no cross-country statistic on a single basis has ever put Taiwan into. I have laid out how far the figure can be taken and why it stops there — and while I was at it I copied out the document numbers of the two ban announcements and of that management regulation for xi xin.

When blood in the urine gets chased down, the tumour is not always in the bladder. In some people it is growing in the renal pelvis or the ureter, and that is called upper tract urothelial carcinoma. It is the same cell as bladder cancer, but the operation, the staging definitions and the follow-up are all different, and the figures from the two sides do not cross over. The vocabulary is in "Ta, T1 and CIS on the report" and the imaging in "How staging is done, and what the scan can see"; this article is only about the stretch upstream.

Its share of the disease here is not the share the Western textbooks give. That much is true — and it routinely gets said with more confidence than the evidence will carry.

How much more common it is here, and where I have to stop

The Taiwan Cancer Registry's long-term trend file, diagnosis year 2023, invasive-cancer basis: 2,562 bladder cancers and 1,884 cancers of the renal pelvis and other urinary organs, the second being 42.4% of the two added together[1]. The European Association of Urology's 2025 upper tract guideline puts the upper tract at 5% to 10% of all urothelial carcinoma[2]. The ceiling I can write up to is this: three to four of every ten urothelial registrations in Taiwan are in the upper tract; in the West it is one to two — the guideline's own figure is 5% to 10%, and the national registries in the next paragraph run higher.

Three limits belong in the same breath as that sentence. First, the formal name of the numerator is "cancer of the renal pelvis and other urinary organs" (ICD-9 189.1 to 189.9), and it contains the urethra and other unspecified urinary organs, so it is not pure upper tract[1]. Second, the denominator is invasive cancer and does not include bladder carcinoma in situ, which is a large box in Taiwan's bladder cancer registry; put it back into the denominator and this proportion falls. Third, no cross-country ranking on a single basis has ever had Taiwan in it to compare — Cancer Incidence in Five Continents Volume XI does not include Taiwan[3]. So I do not write "the world's highest": that is a sentence which recurs in the literature, not a ranking anybody computed.

And the Western 5% to 10% is itself moving. The Norwegian national registry, 1999 to 2018, 3,096 people: the upper tract was 11.8% of all urothelial carcinoma, up from 9.9% to 12.8% across the period[4]. The Dutch national registry, 1993 to 2017, 13,314 people: age-standardised incidence went from 2.0 to 3.2 per 100,000[5]. The guideline says 5 to 10%, and recent national registries are above one in ten. One other difference: Taiwan's upper tract group has more women than men (1,090 women against 794 men in 2023)[1], which is the other way round from the West.

Aristolochic acid leaves a fingerprint, not an impression

A study of 151 Taiwanese patients with upper tract urothelial carcinoma alongside 25 renal cell carcinoma controls found the TP53 mutation spectrum of these tumours dominated by a rare A:T to T:A transversion, the same one found in the upper tract cancers associated with Balkan endemic nephropathy; among those with that mutation in TP53, FGFR3 or HRAS, 83% had aristolactam-DNA adducts detectable in the renal cortex[6]. The guideline's way of putting it is that these adducts can persist in the target tissue for decades and are a reliable biomarker of exposure[2]. The two routes of exposure are set out in the same guideline: crops contaminated by Aristolochia species, and taking herbal remedies prepared from Aristolochia species[2].

One more sentence belongs in the same section: the great majority of people who were exposed do not get this cancer — the guideline's own estimate is under one in ten[2].

The dose is written on the prescription, not in whether you ever took Chinese medicine

A case-control study on the national NHI database, 4,594 people newly diagnosed with urinary tract cancer in 2001 and 2002 against 174,701 controls, adjusted for age, sex, whether they lived in a blackfoot disease endemic township, and chronic urinary tract infection: a prescribed 61 to 100 grams of mutong (木通) carried an odds ratio of 1.6 (95% confidence interval 1.3 to 2.1; a confidence interval means that if you ran the same study many times over, the answer would land in that range), and more than 200 grams carried 2.1 (1.3 to 3.4); read as estimated cumulative aristolochic acid, more than 500 mg carried 2.0 (1.4 to 2.9), with a dose-response trend of p less than 0.001, and independent of arsenic exposure[7]. Two labels cannot be left off: an odds ratio is the odds of having it against not having it in each group, not a multiple of probability; and this study's endpoint is "urinary tract cancer", not the upper tract on its own.

How widespread was the exposure? In a 200,000-person sample cohort covering 1997 to 2003, 78,644 people had been prescribed a Chinese herbal preparation containing aristolochic acid, 526,867 prescriptions in seven years, and more than 85% of them accumulated less than 60 grams[8]. The two sets of figures only mean something side by side: a very large number of people took it, and very few people get this disease. The difference lies in the dose and in the years, not in whether someone ever saw a Chinese medicine practitioner.

Two bans and one management regulation: I have copied the document numbers out

On 4 November 2003, announcement 署授藥字第0920002350號 banned five aristolochic-acid-containing Chinese herbal drug materials — guang fang ji (廣防己), qing mu xiang (青木香), guan mu tong (關木通), ma dou ling (馬兜鈴) and tian xian teng (天仙藤) — from being manufactured, dispensed, imported, exported or sold, with effect from the date of the announcement[9]. The second announcement of the same day, 署授藥字第0920002349號, banned the manufacture and import of preparations containing those five materials and cancelled their marketing authorisations[10]. The prescriptions in the section above are the record of the years before that.

The third document is not a ban. It is about the one that was not banned. On 27 February 2004, announcement 署授藥字第0930000756號 set out the management rules for xi xin (細辛, asarum): the part used changes from the whole plant to the root; concentrated preparations are to be manufactured by water decoction; finished product may be sold only once it has passed testing by the manufacturer's own established HPLC method; and any xi xin-containing product on the market found to contain aristolochic acid is dealt with under the Pharmaceutical Affairs Act[11]. Xi xin was not banned. It was moved to a different part of the plant and a different process, with testing at the gate.

What you are given today by a qualified Chinese medicine practitioner, at a qualified Chinese pharmacy, is not the same thing as the materials that were banned twenty-odd years ago. Three reminders from the ministry's press release, copied as they stand: do not take medicines of unknown origin; when you are ill, see a qualified Chinese medicine practitioner; and when travelling abroad, do not act on somebody's recommendation and carry medicines home with you[12]. As for what you yourself took, I am not going to hand you a tick-box self-assessment — the name of the material, the dose and the year are written on the prescription, not in anybody's memory. Take the medicine bag or the prescription slip back to clinic and tell your doctor what you took and for how long.

Did it come down after the ban? The same registry, two analyses pointing opposite ways

An interrupted time-series analysis on the Taiwan Cancer Registry covering 1995 to 2013 reported two changes of slope in cancer of the renal pelvis and other urinary organs, at 2000 and at 2011, with a fall of 3.4% a year after the second turn; the authors' own wording is conservative — "possibly associated with"[13]. Another analysis, also on the national registry and covering 2001 to 2018, concludes the opposite: the incidence of upper tract urothelial carcinoma rose significantly, with an average annual percentage change of +1.47% (95% confidence interval 1.03 to 1.90, p less than 0.001)[14]. The same registry, two analyses pointing opposite ways; they differ in period, in how the cases were counted and in the statistical method.

I laid the trend file out myself and checked: the age-standardised incidence of this group peaked in 2010 at 4.93 and was 3.91 in 2023, while over the same years the number of cases went from 1,505 to 1,884[1]. Read as a rate it is coming down slowly; read as a head count it is still going up. So I do not write "the ban brought it down".

Aristolochic acid is not the whole of it

The guideline's wording on arsenic has to be translated as it stands: arsenic in drinking water is "tentatively linked" to upper tract cancer, particularly in Taiwan and Chile, and after Taiwan improved the arsenic contamination of its drinking water, a large population study showed the incidence of upper tract cancer falling[2]. Taiwan's own data point the same way: a population cohort (2,921 cases against 11,684 controls) showed that, whether or not someone lived in a blackfoot disease endemic area, the higher the aristolochic acid exposure the higher the risk, and that the two factors were additive[15]. Arsenic and blackfoot disease are written up on this site in "Electroplating, welding, and that old well", and the tobacco box is in "Smoking and this disease"; I am not rewriting either here.

Nor does any of this finish the explanation. As early as 2002, a 535-case series from a medical centre in Taipei recorded that most of its patients did not live in a blackfoot disease endemic area[16] — and that sentence is where the aristolochic acid work started. As for why there are so many women, what the authors can still offer is reasoning at the ecological level[3].

The same cell, a different set of things done to it

Upper tract cancer and bladder cancer share their classification and their histology. Every place they differ comes with a figure attached. About two-thirds of upper tract cancers are already muscle-invasive at diagnosis, where newly diagnosed bladder cancer runs at 15% to 25%, and about 9% have already metastasised at diagnosis. The same letters mean different things: T3 in the renal pelvis is invasion beyond the muscle layer into peripelvic fat or the renal parenchyma; in the ureter it is invasion into periureteric fat[2].

The surgery divides two ways as well: the standard treatment for high-risk disease is radical nephroureterectomy, while for low-risk disease the first choice is kidney-sparing management, which the guideline recommends strongly "irrespective of the status of the contralateral kidney" — regardless of how good the other kidney is[2]. Keeping the kidney is not the easier road: after ureteroscopic laser management the ipsilateral recurrence rate is 25% to 85%, and a second ureteroscopy is advised within eight weeks of the first procedure[2].

Once the top has been dealt with, the bottom still has to be looked after. A meta-analysis of 18 retrospective studies and 8,275 people: after radical nephroureterectomy, 2,402 of them (29%) recurred inside the bladder, at a median of 22.2 months[17]. A single instillation after the operation has randomised evidence behind it: a phase 3 trial across 44 Japanese institutions, 304 people, one instillation of pirarubicin within 24 hours of surgery, three-year recurrence-free survival 60.0% against 47.0%[18]. For how this is paid for in Taiwan I could not find a listed item I could quote word for word — ask the medical affairs office or your case manager. The other side does grow tumours too, though not in most people: of 462 unilateral upper tract cancers at one Taiwanese hospital, 52 (11.3%) later appeared on the contralateral side, with ten-year contralateral disease-free survival of 75.7%[19].

Kidney function is the constraint under every decision in this article

Take out a kidney with a ureter attached and kidney function will drop. A four-institution cohort of 388 people (median age 70) measured a mean post-operative fall in eGFR of 24%; taking 60 as the threshold, 49% qualified before the operation and only 19% after it[20]. Another cohort of 336 using the same threshold gives 48% before and 22% after, and those authors work out that keeping cisplatin-containing chemotherapy until after the operation costs 49% of high-risk patients that option altogether[21].

Adjuvant chemotherapy is a box with randomised evidence in it. The POUT trial enrolled 261 people with pT2 to T4 or node-positive disease after radical nephroureterectomy and gave four cycles of platinum-containing chemotherapy: five-year disease-free survival 62% against 45% (hazard ratio 0.55, 95% confidence interval 0.38 to 0.80; a hazard ratio divides the rate at which events happen in one group by the rate in the other), five-year overall survival 66% against 57% (hazard ratio 0.68, p=0.049) — though the authors write plainly that overall survival was not this trial's primary endpoint[22]. The price goes in the same paragraph: acute grade 3 or worse treatment-related adverse events were 44% (55/126) in the chemotherapy arm against 4% (5/129) under surveillance[23].

Put those two things together and you get the most useful sentence in the article: the timing of chemotherapy has to be talked about before the operation, not once it is over. Three things you can get said out loud at the next appointment — is my tumour in the renal pelvis or the ureter, and does it count as high risk or low risk? What is my eGFR now, and where is it expected to land after surgery? And if chemotherapy is to be given, before or after, and on what reasoning?

From the physician

If you took Chinese medicine over a long period, do not frighten yourself and do not draw your own conclusion either: the name of the material, the dose and the year are all written on the prescription, not in your memory. Take the medicine bag or the prescription slip to clinic and go through it there. Also ask your doctor to say plainly whether your tumour is in the renal pelvis or the ureter, whether it counts as high risk or low risk, and whether chemotherapy is to come before or after the operation.
