How follow-up is scheduled, and the second cancer people forget

The guideline itself admits there is no evidence that regular follow-up prolongs survival — so what are our reasons for scheduling it; plus the second cancer that oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma patients are so often not checked for: the head and neck.

Oesophageal Cancer Guide · After Treatment · 9 references
#Follow-up#Imaging#Screening#Recurrence

After surgery, half of recurrences are found because the patient has symptoms, 45% by CT, and endoscopy catches only 1%; after chemoradiotherapy, by contrast, endoscopy with biopsy is due every three months. The two groups' follow-up should look different, and this article explains why — and why a tumour marker cannot serve as an alarm.

"Doctor, how often do I come back? What tests will I have?" Patients who have finished treatment ask this expecting a definite timetable. I can give you one, but I have to say something first that most people are never told: how follow-up for oesophageal cancer should be scheduled is a question the international guideline admits it cannot answer. So this article does not start with "a scan every so many months"; it starts by making clear what our reasons for scheduling follow-up are — once the reasons are clear, the timetable means something. It ends with something that is often forgotten: the second cancer.

The guideline says it in black and white: no evidence that regular follow-up prolongs survival

The follow-up section of the ESMO 2022 oesophageal cancer guideline contains only two recommendations. One says that about 90% of recurrences occur within two years of completing local treatment, and that follow-up visits should concentrate on symptoms, nutrition and psychosocial support — evidence level [V, A]; the other says that in patients with a complete response after definitive chemoradiotherapy, endoscopy, biopsy and CT every three months may be considered for early detection of recurrence — level [IV, B][1]. Translating those codes into plain language: V is expert opinion, IV is retrospective cohort or case-control studies — neither recommendation rests on a randomised trial. The text itself is more direct, and I translate it as written: apart from patients who may be candidates for endoscopic re-intervention or early salvage surgery, there is no evidence that regular follow-up after initial treatment affects survival[1]. I could not find any randomised trial or systematic review of follow-up frequency in oesophageal cancer that overturns that sentence. So if anyone tells you "a CT every few months will help you live longer", that statement has no evidence behind it, and I am not going to say it.

Then why do we still schedule follow-up? Three reasons

Thin evidence does not mean no follow-up. I have three reasons for scheduling it; none of them is about prolonging survival, and all of them are real. One, dealing with the problems of having survived: weight, swallowing, reflux, diarrhoea, mood — these are still the heaviest burdens ten years after surgery (see "Eating after an oesophagectomy"), and the guideline says follow-up needs a multidisciplinary team of dietitian, gastroenterologist, psychologist and social worker[1]. Two, catching the recurrence that can still be dealt with: for the person with persistent or recurrent local disease after definitive chemoradiotherapy who is still operable, salvage surgery is a road with survival data and also with a death rate (the numbers are in "After recurrence or spread, what roads remain"); the guideline's every-three-months endoscopy-and-biopsy recommendation is scheduled for exactly this group[1]. Three, the second cancer — a whole section below. These three reasons determine the shape of follow-up: symptoms and nutrition are asked about every time; imaging is done for a specific purpose, not as a ritual.

People who had surgery and people who had only chemoradiotherapy follow different paths

The two groups need to be discussed separately, because they recur differently. At Memorial Sloan Kettering in New York, 1,147 surgical patients had routine CT and endoscopic follow-up: 38% recurred, of whom 55% had distant metastases, 28% local recurrence and 17% both; half of recurrences were found because the patient had symptoms, 45% by routine CT, and surveillance endoscopy caught only 1%; the recurrence rate fell from 27 per 100 person-years in the first year to 4 in the sixth, and the authors concluded that surveillance endoscopy has limited value for asymptomatic local recurrence and that surveillance imaging after the sixth year is probably unnecessary[2]. The reason is not hard to grasp: after surgery the oesophagus is almost gone, and local recurrence is mostly in lymph nodes outside the lumen, where an endoscope cannot see. After definitive chemoradiotherapy the oesophagus is still there and "never cleared" is the main mode of failure, which is why the guideline recommends endoscopy with biopsy plus CT every three months for this group[1]; the Japanese JCOG0909 trial is a prospective study built on exactly this logic: 94 stage II–III squamous cell carcinoma patients, complete response 59%, 27% subsequently underwent salvage surgery, three-year overall survival 74.2%[3]. The ESMO 2025 interim update added a note beside salvage surgery: it should only be performed in selected patients, at experienced centres[4]. So if you had definitive chemoradiotherapy, closer endoscopy in the first two years has a reason behind it; if you had surgery, the weight of follow-up is on symptoms and CT, and endoscopy is mainly to check whether the anastomosis has narrowed (see "Swallowing worse again: stricture or recurrence").

Tumour markers: drawn for reference, not usable as an alarm

Clinics in Taiwan are in the habit of drawing SCC-Ag and CEA at every visit, and I draw them too. But I have to be honest about what they can do. The Japanese JCOG0502 and JCOG0909 trials were pooled — 239 squamous cell carcinoma patients under intensive follow-up after definitive chemoradiotherapy — to analyse whether these two markers could predict recurrence: at the cut-off that reached 60% sensitivity, specificity was only 19.6% and 20.3%, and no cut-off met the pre-specified standard of "sensitivity at least 60% and specificity at least 70%"[5]. In plain language: to catch 60% of recurrences with these markers, you have to accept 80% false alarms. So a marker rising a little does not mean recurrence, and not rising does not mean all is well; it is a reference value, and what decides whether to do an endoscopy and biopsy is symptoms and imaging.

The second cancer often grows in the throat: the same sheet of mucosa

This is the thing I think is most often forgotten in follow-up. In a patient with oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma, the mouth, pharynx and larynx are the same sheet of mucosa that has soaked in tobacco and alcohol for years, and it grows a second squamous cell carcinoma at a different time — this is called field cancerization. The ESMO guideline writes: oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma is frequently accompanied by a second primary tumour of the head and neck, pooled prevalence 6.7%; those with a second primary have a worse prognosis; and patients with oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma should have a qualified clinical head-and-neck examination[1]. That 6.7% comes from a meta-analysis of 12 studies and 6,483 patients, all Japanese data: 85.3% were early-stage, 60.3% were in the hypopharynx, half were found at the same time and half afterwards[6]. Taiwanese figures: in 435 squamous cell carcinoma patients after curative surgery at Taipei and Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospitals, median follow-up 48.5 months, there was only one independent risk factor — age under 50 (hazard ratio 4.13 — a hazard ratio divides the speed at which events occur in one group by the speed in the other); in patients under 50, the cumulative incidence of a second primary head-and-neck cancer at three, five and ten years after surgery was 14.3%, 22.1% and 34.2%[7] — one-third at ten years. So in my follow-up, "please have ENT look at the throat" is a fixed item, arranged by the treating team; how often, no guideline gives a frequency, and I am not going to invent one. The reverse holds too: among 315 newly diagnosed head-and-neck squamous cell carcinoma patients in Taiwan who had image-enhanced endoscopy before treatment, 21.9% had a synchronous oesophageal lesion[8]; another Taiwanese dataset of 717 patients screened with ordinary white-light endoscopy caught a somewhat lower share; the difference between the two numbers is the tool, not that one is right and the other wrong — image-enhanced endoscopy pulls in even the early lesions. That dataset, and oesophageal screening for head-and-neck cancer patients, are in "Can endoscopy alone deal with an early lesion".

Stopping drinking is the one square on the follow-up plan you can move yourself

A Japanese multicentre prospective study of 331 patients after endoscopic resection of early oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma used Lugol's iodine staining to count the unstained blank areas on the oesophagus (those are places where the mucosa has already changed): among patients with 0, 1 to 9, and 10 or more blank areas, the combined proportion who grew a new oesophageal or head-and-neck squamous cell carcinoma within two years was 4.0%, 10.0% and 31.4%[9]. The same dataset also saw that stopping drinking halved the risk of growing another squamous cell carcinoma, while stopping smoking did not reach statistical significance — the numbers and how to read them are in "Things to settle before treatment starts". For most patients sitting in my clinic, the drinking started decades ago; I raise it because it is one of the few squares on the follow-up plan where you yourself can change the outcome. If you need help stopping, say so, and we refer you.

Five years of follow-up: the table you can draw up in advance

Once the reasons above are laid out, the follow-up table is no longer mysterious. Three fixed things at every visit: weight, changes in swallowing and reflux, mood and sleep. The first two years are the densest period for recurrence; people who had definitive chemoradiotherapy follow the guideline's endoscopy-with-biopsy plus CT every three months; people who had surgery are followed mainly by symptoms and CT, at a frequency your team sets by stage and pathology. Tumour markers are drawn to watch the trend, not used as an alarm. The ENT throat examination goes on the schedule, and people under 50 especially must not miss it. Any new difficulty swallowing, hoarseness, black stools or weight loss, at any time — do not wait for the next appointment. How the NHI handles PET or endoscopy done for follow-up — I could not find the clause, so ask your case manager. At the next visit you can bring this article and ask one question: "Which items in my follow-up are there to catch a salvage opportunity, and which are there to look after the life I have now?" If that cannot be answered, the table is just habit.

From the physician

The follow-up table is not a ritual. At every visit I need to know your weight, your swallowing and your mood; if you had definitive chemoradiotherapy, endoscopy with biopsy is closer together in the first two years; if you are a squamous cell carcinoma patient under 50, make sure the ENT throat examination is on the schedule. If you are still drinking, that is the one square you can change yourself — say so if you need help.
